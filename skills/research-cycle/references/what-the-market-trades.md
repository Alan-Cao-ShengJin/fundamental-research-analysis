# What the market is trading

Filings, calls and consensus are public and already in the price. A report built only from them describes what the market has finished pricing. Its value lies in a judgment on the question the marginal buyer is asking now, which is often not the question the filings make easiest to model. Establish that question in stage 1 of the [company sequence](company-sequence.md), build the model to answer it, lead the report with it and test it at challenge. Rerun this at every review: the live question moves.

## 1. Find what sets the price

Nobody will tell the study what buyers think, and the view moves faster than anything published. The price is the only real-time record of it, so read the price and the estimates around it, and use documents only to put reasons to what the numbers show. Run these reads in this order; each is available from the approved sources.

1. **Which year the price fits.** Divide the price by the stock's own historical mean forward multiple to get the earnings the market is paying for, and place that figure against consensus for the forward year, the year after and the year after that. Equivalently, compute the P/E on each year's consensus and see which one sits inside the historical band. If the price only makes sense on a later year, the market trades that year.
2. **Which line is live.** For each fiscal year, and for each business segment where the feed carries segment consensus, take the spread between the highest and lowest estimate, the estimate count and the up and down revisions in the last 100 days. A line with a wide spread and many recent revisions is live; one with a tight, stable range is settled. The highest estimate after a beat is the best published proxy for the leading buy-side view.
3. **Whether the price is ahead of the estimates.** Pull the point-in-time history of consensus for each year over the past six to twelve months and set it against the share price. Where the price has moved far more than the estimates for the years it fits, the market holds a view the published numbers have not caught up with; the gap is a quantity, not a mood.
4. **The surprise record.** Beats or misses for the last eight periods, their size and the price reaction on the day. Repeated beats with a rising price say consensus lags; beats with a falling price say the market is trading something other than the reported line.
5. **Guidance against the consensus of the day before.** Whether estimates moved to the guidance, stopped short of it or passed it, and how the price reacted. Estimates above guidance say the market discounts the plan as conservative.
6. **Price-reaction log.** The ten or so largest daily moves over the past year for the stock and its closest peers, with the news on each day from the [world-state record](world-state.md). The variable that explains most of the moves is what the market trades. Where the stock has moved with a peer group or a theme on days with no company news, name the theme.
7. **Records of what investors ask.** Call Q&A for the last two or three calls, and the public records of investor meetings where the market keeps them (for A shares, the investor-relations activity records filed with the exchange name the institutions present and their questions; for other markets, conference and non-deal-roadshow transcripts where public). What investors come back to, and what management declines to quantify, is the live question. Prepared remarks say what management wants discussed.
8. **The price-implied figure.** At the stock's own multiple, what today's price needs on the live variable. Decompose the market value: the established businesses at a supportable multiple, and the residual the price places on the new line, the pipeline or the option.

Weigh evidence by date: anything published before the last results or the last large move is background for the live question. Where the reads disagree, keep the two candidates, model both and say which the evidence favours. Write the result in one sentence, labelled inferred, with the reads that support it: the variable, the period, the number the price implies, and the reading the market holds now.

## 2. Which period the market trades

Find the first year whose numbers are not yet visible. When backlog, contracts, orders or guidance already cover the forward year, that year is settled and the market trades the one after it; the multiple on forward earnings is set by the view of that later year. Then:

- carry the component build through that year with the same detail as the forward year;
- show our forecast, consensus and the growth the price implies for it, side by side;
- explain the target multiple by our reading of that year, under the [target and earnings basis](../../investment-conclusion/references/decision-contract.md#target-and-earnings-basis), which still applies the multiple to today's forward earnings.

A report that argues the forward year when the market has moved past it is answering a question nobody is still asking.

## 3. The live buy-side view against stale consensus

Consensus lags. After a sharp price move, a results beat or a capacity announcement, the price carries a view that the published estimates have not caught up with. Signs that the buy side is ahead, all from the reads above: the price-implied figure sits above the highest published estimate; the price has run ahead of the point-in-time consensus for the years it fits; the latest quarter or half annualised with the capacity ramp already exceeds the consensus year; the revisions in the last 100 days are all in one direction.

Treat the price-implied figure as the live view and look for the evidence it rests on: the latest run rate, the timing of capacity, announced customers and contracts, pricing, and peers' numbers. "Beyond management's stated plan" is not a refutation: public plans are conservative and dated. A forecast for a fast-growing line starts from the run rate and the ramp, and uses the plan as a check.

If our forecast sits below the live view, say so, give the evidence, and state what would make the live view right. Being below a stale consensus is not a differentiated view, and being above it is not a bull case.

## 4. When the price is an asset, not the earnings

When the residual placed on something outside current earnings is a large share of the market value, the research effort goes to that thing. It can be a clinical asset, an unlaunched product, a new plant or capacity, a technology, a licence, a contract, a mine or a platform. Judge its competitive position against the rivals pursuing the same customers or the same prize, on the same evidence:

- **What it is and what it does** that the rivals' versions do not: the claimed advantage in plain terms.
- **Evidence against rivals.** The disclosed data for each (trial results, performance, cost, yield, customer wins, pricing), with its stage, scale and date beside each number, and what the comparison can and cannot show.
- **Timing.** Each rival's stage and next milestone. Second with something better can win; first with the same usually keeps the position.
- **Commercial frame.** The addressable demand, the price regime, and what comparable assets have fetched in deals or listings, which price the asset before it earns.
- **Value under each outcome.** Best in its class, one of several, or failed, each with its probability and the cash needed to get there, set against the residual the price places on it.

Conclude with a stated view (best in class, competitive, or behind), the evidence that would change it and when it is due. The [pharma card](../../company-model/references/sectors/pharma.md#competitive-position-of-a-pipeline-asset) applies this to a drug; the [industry lenses](../../industry-analysis/SKILL.md) supply the market and barrier evidence for the rest.

The established business still gets a full model, because it funds the asset and sets the downside. In the report it gets space in proportion to its share of value: a declining legacy franchise priced at a small part of the market value is context, not the argument.

## 5. Output and use

Stage 1 records: the live variable and period; the price-implied figure; the market's current reading and ours, each with its evidence; the reads that support the inference and any that disagree; the observation that settles it and its date. The contested questions follow from it. The view can move within weeks, so the record is dated and every review repeats reads 1 to 6 before anything else; a change in what the market trades reopens the debate even when the forecasts stand. The report's debate section leads with it; [research challenge](../../research-challenge/SKILL.md) tests whether the debate named is the one the market is trading; [monitor review](../../monitor-review/SKILL.md) watches the settling observation.
