# Beer

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Brewers sell beer by volume, mostly through wholesalers. In a mature market volume is
flat or falling, and revenue grows through price and through drinkers moving to dearer
beer. In poorer countries volume still grows.

Beer sits in price tiers, from mainstream to premium. Revenue for each unit of volume
rises as the mix moves up, and a premium beer earns several times what a mainstream one
does.

There are two channels.

- **Restaurants, bars and nightlife venues.** The price is higher, and brewers pay fees
  and support to be the venue's supplier. Sales follow eating out and going out.
- **Shops, supermarkets and delivery.** Sales follow what households spend, and
  promotions.

Demand follows the number of people of drinking age, the weather in summer, eating out
and incomes. Governments tax beer by volume or by value, and regulate where and when it
is sold.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Volume** against **revenue per unit of volume**.
- Growth from **price** against growth from **mix**, both inside revenue per unit.
- **Shipments** to wholesalers against **sales to drinkers**.
- Growth at **constant currency and scope** against reported growth.
- Revenue **before** and **after** the tax on beer.
- **Advances** from wholesalers, which are seasonal: compare the same date each year.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes spirits (see the
[baijiu card](baijiu.md) and the [spirits card](spirits.md)); it bottles soft drinks
(see the [packaged food and drink card](packaged-food-drink.md)); or it owns the bars
and restaurants (see the [restaurants card](restaurants.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Global brewers give volume and revenue per unit by region each quarter,
  at constant currency, with profit before depreciation. Chinese brewers give volume by
  brand or tier, revenue and cost per unit, twice a year or more often. Few split price
  from mix or venues from shops.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Output | Monthly beer output (the statistics bureau in China, brewers' associations elsewhere), and imports. The bureau's sample changes, so use its growth rates and not its levels |
| Eating out | Monthly revenue of restaurants. Little is published on bars and nightlife |
| Inputs | Prices of barley, aluminium, glass and paper |
| Tax | Budget announcements on the tax on beer |
| Peers | Other brewers' volumes in each market |

- **What leads profit.** Monthly beer output. Restaurants' revenue. Input prices, after
  the hedges. Announced price rises. Summer weather. Tax changes.
- **Backtest.** Volume times revenue per unit against revenue. Lagged input prices
  against cost per unit.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Breweries and distribution are fixed costs. When volume falls, cost per unit rises.
- Inputs are hedged, so a change in their price reaches the margin after six to twelve
  months.
- The mix of tiers moves profit more than volume does.
- Support paid to venues can take back a price rise.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Breweries are heavy plant, and depreciation is large against profit.
- Brewers in mature markets close old breweries. That costs money once and can release
  land.
- The business produces cash and pays much of it out.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Beer sold in venues falls when people go out less, and recovers slowly.
- Drinkers trade down a tier in a weak economy.
- Most of the profit is earned in the warm months, and the last quarter can be a loss.
- Traps:
  - shipments ahead of what drinkers buy;
  - a rise in revenue per unit that is mix, read as a price rise;
  - advances from wholesalers compared across different dates;
  - a tax rise the brewer cannot pass on;
  - land gains and grants inside profit;
  - falling volume assumed to cost only its own margin, when fixed costs stay;
  - growth at constant currency quoted while reported revenue falls.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether drinkers keep moving to dearer beer.
- When sales in venues recover.
- Whether volume is falling for good, as people age and drink less.
- Where input costs go.
- How much is paid out.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it; dividend yield for a mature brewer
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A brewer sells 100 units of volume: 80 of mainstream beer at 50 and 20 of premium
at 120. Revenue is 6,400, or 64 a unit. Costs that move with volume are 25 a unit
for mainstream and 35 for premium, 2,700 in all, and fixed costs are 2,000. Gross
profit is 1,700.

The next year mainstream volume falls 5%, to 76, and premium grows 10%, to 22, with no
price change. Volume is down 2%. Revenue is 6,440, up 0.6%, and revenue per unit is up
2.7%, all of it mix. Gross profit is 1,770, up 4%.

If instead all volume falls 6% with the same mix, revenue is 6,016, variable costs are
2,538 and gross profit is 1,478, down 13%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
