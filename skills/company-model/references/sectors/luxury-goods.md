# Luxury goods

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Luxury houses sell fashion, leather goods, watches and branded jewellery at high prices.
Most sales go through their own shops. Watches and some jewellery also go through
retailers that carry several brands.

Revenue grows through price rises in most years, through volume, through the mix of
products, and through a small number of new or larger shops. The strongest brands do not
mark goods down.

Buyers are the wealthy, and a wider group who buy a few pieces a year. Many buy while
travelling, so sales move between regions with exchange rates and tourism. Chinese
buyers, at home and abroad, are a large share of the total.

Numbers that get confused:

- Growth at **constant currency and scope** against reported growth.
- **Own shops** against **wholesale**.
- Growth of shops **open in both periods** against growth from new space.
- **Price**, **volume** and **mix**, which the houses seldom split.
- Sales by **where the goods are sold** against sales by the **buyer's nationality**.
- A **group's** results against those of the one or two brands that carry it.
- A financial year ending in **March** against calendar-year peers.

Signs the card does not fit: the brand sells sportswear or everyday clothing (see the
[sportswear and apparel card](sportswear-apparel.md)); it sells gold by weight (see the
[jewellery card](jewellery.md)); it retails other houses' brands (see the
[retail card](retail.md)); or the unit is beauty (see the
[beauty brands card](beauty-personal-care.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each division or brand, and each region, separately, with history beside the
forecast. Where the company gives divisions and regions but not one by the other, build
by division and use the regions as a check.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyers | Spending by nationality, and where they buy as tourism and exchange rates shift. It sets the growth assumed for the shops in each region | Peers' growth by region |

| Price | Yearly price rises by region, and moves to even out prices between regions | Leave it inside the row above |
| Wholesale | Sales to retailers and franchisees, their stock, and the house's own choice to cut accounts | Wholesale growth |

| Gross margin | It moves with price rises, the mix of products, the cost of gold, stones and leather, import duties, currency and how much is sold at full price | Its recent level, moved with currency |
| Shop costs | Rent, often a share of sales with a minimum, and staff. Mostly fixed | Their past level, grown with shops |
| Marketing | Shows, advertising and events | Its share of revenue |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Sales by division and region each quarter, with growth at constant
  currency. Profit twice a year. Shop numbers. Groups give little on single brands, and
  houses give little guidance.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Peers | Other houses' growth by region. The first to report each quarter set the tone |
| Watches | Monthly Swiss watch exports by country (the Swiss watch federation) |
| Travellers | Tourist arrivals, tax-free shopping figures and duty-free sales |
| Wealth | Share and property prices in the buyers' countries |
| Prices | Announced price rises, and resale prices for watches and bags |

- **What leads revenue.** Peers' results. Swiss watch exports. Tax-free shopping.
  Exchange rates, which move where people buy. Announced price rises.
- **Backtest.** Shops times sales for each against retail sales. Growth at constant
  currency plus currency against reported growth.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gross margin is high. Shops and staff are fixed, so profit moves much faster than
  sales in both directions. A rise in the cost of gold or in duties can still lower the
  margin in a year of growth.
- A brand at the top can raise prices. Brands below it lose buyers when they try.
- A brand's appeal runs in cycles, often tied to a change of designer.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Fashion houses need little plant. Jewellery and watch houses own workshops and hold
  more than a year of stock. Capital also goes into shops and, for the largest houses,
  buildings.
- A strong house produces a lot of cash and buys other brands with it.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows wealth and confidence, with a lag after markets fall.
- Taxes on luxury goods, limits on gifts and customs checks on travellers can move
  demand between countries.
- Traps:
  - growth that is all price while fewer pieces are sold;
  - tourists' spending moving between regions, read as growth;
  - one brand carrying a group;
  - a cut in wholesale read as weak demand, or its one-off lift to margin read as
    lasting;
  - hedges that delay a currency move by a year;
  - growth from new space quoted with growth from existing shops.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- When Chinese demand recovers.
- Whether price rises have gone too far for the occasional buyer.
- Which brands are gaining and losing appeal.
- How far margins fall in a downturn.

Yardstick: P/E, with enterprise value to operating profit beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A house has 100 shops selling 10 each: revenue is 1,000. Gross margin is 70%, which is
700, and shop and other costs are 450, mostly fixed. Profit is 250.

It raises prices 6%, sells 2% fewer pieces, and the mix adds 1%: existing shops grow 5%.
Revenue is 1,050. The goods cost about the same 300 to make, so gross profit is 750 and
profit 300, up 20%.

If the shops sell 5% fewer pieces at unchanged prices, revenue is 950, the goods cost
285, gross profit is 665 and profit 215, down 14%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
