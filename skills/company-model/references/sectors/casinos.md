# Casinos

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A casino earns what its players lose: the amount bet times the share the house wins.
There are three kinds of income.

- **Mass-market tables and slot machines.** Players change money for chips and play.
  The casino keeps a steady share of it, and the margin is high.
- **VIP play.** A few players bet very large amounts, often on credit. The casino wins a
  small share of the amount bet and pays much of it back as commissions and rebates to
  the players and to the promoters who bring them. The margin is thin.
- **Rooms, shops, food and shows** around the tables. Many rooms are given free to
  players.

The casino pays a tax on gaming revenue. It operates under a licence that expires and
that may require it to spend on things other than gaming.

Demand is visitors times what each spends. It follows incomes where the visitors come
from, visas, transport, and limits on moving money. A new resort next door shares out
the same visitors.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Gross gaming revenue** against revenue after commissions, rebates and loyalty
  points.
- **Mass-market** play against **VIP** play.
- The **money changed for chips**, which companies call the drop, the **amount bet**,
  which they call rolling chip volume, and the **amount won**.
- The **share won** in a period against the share expected over time. The difference is
  luck.
- A **resort's** profit against the group's, and a listed subsidiary's against its
  parent's.
- The **market's** gaming revenue against the company's share of it.

Signs the card does not fit: the company runs lotteries or online betting, whose costs
are marketing and technology; it runs hotels without gaming (see the
[hotels card](hotels.md)); or it makes gaming machines.

## 2. The driver tree

changed and the share won is the main one. Market revenue times share is the check on
it.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Each quarter, for each resort: money changed for chips, the amount bet by
  VIPs, the share won, revenue by kind and profit before depreciation, with the effect
  of luck.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Macau | Gaming revenue on the first day of each month and its quarterly split by kind of play (the gaming regulator). Visitor arrivals by origin and hotel occupancy (the statistics office) |
| Other markets | Nevada's monthly figures. Regulators' figures in the Philippines and elsewhere. Singapore's two resorts report only through their owners |
| Travel | Visa schemes, flights and the holiday calendar |
| Rules | Licence terms, rules on promoters, and limits on moving money across borders |

- **What leads revenue.** Monthly gaming revenue. Visitor arrivals. Holiday weeks.
  Weekly estimates quoted by brokers, labelled as reports. New resorts opening. Visa
  changes.
- **Backtest.** The amount bet times the share won against VIP revenue. Money changed
  for chips times the share won against mass revenue. Market revenue times share against
  the company's.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Tax and commissions move with revenue. Staff and buildings are fixed.
- Mass-market play earns several times the margin of VIP play, so the mix matters more
  than the total.
- The share won at the tables varies from quarter to quarter by chance.
- Share can be bought with rebates and free rooms, at the cost of margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A resort costs a great deal to build. Some operators carry heavy debt and others hold
  net cash.
- A mature casino produces a lot of cash. The question is how it is split between debt,
  new building and shareholders.
- The licence runs for a fixed term.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Policy can change a market overnight: visas, limits on moving money, rules on
  promoters.
- New markets opening elsewhere compete for the same players.
- Traps:
  - gaming revenue growth that is low-margin VIP play;
  - a lucky quarter read as a trend;
  - share gained with rebates;
  - full hotels where most rooms are given away;
  - a new resort assumed to fill in its first year;
  - a parent valued on a subsidiary's profit without taking out the partners' share;
  - profit quoted before the spending the licence requires.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether demand holds after a recovery.
- How the cash is split between debt, new building and shareholders.
- What happens when the licence is renewed.
- Whether promotions are eating the margin.

Yardstick: EV/EBITDA, with P/E and dividend yield beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A casino wins 2,500 from mass tables and 1,500 from VIP play. Tax is taken here as 40%
of each; use the market's own rate. VIP commissions are 625. Mass play leaves 1,500 and
VIP play leaves 275.

VIP players bet 50,000 in a quarter. At the normal share of 3% the casino wins 1,500. If
it wins 3.5% instead, it wins 1,750. Commissions are paid on the amount bet, so the
extra 250 bears only tax, and 150 of it reaches profit. That is luck, and stays out of
the forecast.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
