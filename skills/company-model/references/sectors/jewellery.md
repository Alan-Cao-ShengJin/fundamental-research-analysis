# Jewellery

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Jewellers sell gold, diamond and gem-set pieces through shops. In China most shops are
run by franchisees. There are two ways of pricing, and they earn very differently.

- **Gold priced by weight.** The piece sells at the day's gold price plus a fee for the
  work. The jeweller earns a small amount for each gram, and its revenue moves with the
  gold price.
- **Fixed-price pieces.** Designed gold and gem-set pieces sell at a price for the
  piece, and earn far more.

With franchisees, the jeweller books the goods it sells to them, or a fee on what they
sell. The franchisee carries the shop and much of the stock.

People buy for weddings, gifts and festivals, for themselves, and as a way to save. A
sharp rise in the gold price cuts the weight people buy.

The stock is gold. A jeweller funds it with borrowed gold or hedges it with futures, and
gains or loses on what is left unhedged when the price moves.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Retail sales** across all shops, franchisees' included, against the jeweller's
  **revenue**. Retail sales include value-added tax and revenue does not.
- Same-store sales by **value** against same-store sales by **volume**, which may be
  counted in grams or in pieces.
- **Weight sold** against revenue, which moves with the gold price.
- **Gross margin** as a percentage, which falls when gold rises, against gross profit
  for each gram.
- Gains on **gold stock**, which reach gross profit as the gold is sold, against losses
  on **borrowed gold and hedges**, which may sit below operating profit. Read the two
  together.
- Shops **opened** against shops opened net of closures.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is a luxury house that sells branded jewellery
at prices far above its materials (see the [luxury goods card](luxury-goods.md)); it
mines gold (see the [metal miners card](metal-miners.md)); or it grows or trades stones.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** The large Hong Kong-listed jewellers publish retail sales growth and
  same-store sales, by value and by volume and by region, each quarter. Results give
  shop numbers, revenue by product and by channel, and the share of fixed-price pieces.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Gold | The gold price. Quarterly gold consumption by weight (the gold association, the World Gold Council) |
| Official data | Monthly retail sales of gold, silver and jewellery |
| Weddings | Marriage registrations each quarter |
| Peers | Other jewellers' quarterly updates and shop numbers |

- **What leads revenue.** The gold price, most of all a sharp move. Sales over the main
  festivals. Marriage registrations. Franchisees' restocking.
- **Backtest.** Weight sold times the gold price against gold revenue. Shops times sales
  for each.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gold priced by weight earns a small fee for each gram. Against today's gold price,
  its percentage margin falls when gold rises. The reported margin can rise instead,
  because gold bought earlier at a lower price is being sold. That part is a gain on
  stock.
- A larger share of fixed-price pieces lifts the margin more than anything else.
- Shops are fixed costs, so same-store sales move profit by more than they move
  revenue.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Stock is the capital, and it is gold, often borrowed.
- Franchisees carry most of the shops. The jeweller still holds a large stock of its
  own, sometimes a year of sales, funded partly with borrowed gold. Check its debt
  before assuming a high payout.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows weddings, incomes and the gold price.
- A design that sells well can be copied within months.
- Traps:
  - revenue growth that is only the gold price;
  - gains on gold stock inside profit, or hedge losses left out of it;
  - franchisees' shops filled with stock, read as demand;
  - shop numbers quoted without closures;
  - same-store sales by value read as volume;
  - the share of fixed-price pieces assumed to keep rising.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- What the gold price does to the weight people buy.
- How long the fashion for designed gold lasts.
- How many shops close.
- Whether demand for diamonds comes back.

Yardstick: P/E, with dividend yield beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A jeweller sells 100 grams at a gold price of 500 plus a fee of 30 a gram: revenue is
53,000 and gross profit 3,000. Gold rises 20%, to 600, and the weight sold falls 15%.
Revenue is 85 times 630, which is 53,550, up 1%. Gross profit is 85 times 30, which is
2,550, down 15%.

It then sells 20 of the 85 grams as fixed-price pieces that earn 150 a gram. Gross
profit is 65 times 30 plus 20 times 150, which is 4,950.

It holds 50 grams of stock unhedged. The rise of 100 a gram is a gain of 5,000 on the
stock, as large as a year's gross profit. It reaches gross profit as the stock is sold,
and should be shown apart from the fee income.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
