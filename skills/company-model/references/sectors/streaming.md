# Music and video streaming

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Streaming services sell access to music, video, audio and live broadcasts. They earn in
four ways.

- **Subscriptions.** Paying subscribers times revenue per subscriber. Most services
  have a free tier, paid for by advertising, that feeds the paid one.
- **Advertising.** Time spent listening or watching, times the ads shown, times their
  price.
- **Live streaming**, large in China. Viewers buy virtual gifts for performers, and the
  platform pays much of that on to the performers and their agencies.
- **Other income**: concerts, merchandise and content licensed to other services.

Content is the main cost, and music and video work differently.

- A **music** service pays the labels and publishers that own the rights, as a share of
  what it earns or as a minimum for each subscriber, under contracts renewed every few
  years.
- A **video** service buys or makes its shows. The cost goes on the balance sheet and is
  charged from the date a show is released, over the years it is expected to be
  watched, most of it early. Shows still being made are not yet charged. Some content
  is paid for as a share of the revenue it brings, and never reaches the balance
  sheet.

Listeners and viewers decide. Hit content and price changes move subscribers.
Regulators set rules on content, on minors and on live streaming.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Monthly active users**, **paying users**, and the ratio between them.
- Subscribers at the **end** of a period against the **average** over it.
- **Revenue per paying user** against the list price. Discounts, family plans and
  bundles sit between them.
- **Cash spent on content** against the charge for it in the income statement.
- Gifts **bought by viewers** against what the platform keeps.
- Growth from the service's **own business** against growth from a company it bought.

Signs the card does not fit: most revenue is advertising on content that users make (see
the [e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)); the company
makes games (see the [games card](games.md)); it makes films for cinemas or runs
cinemas (see the [film and television production card](film-tv-production.md) and the
[cinemas card](cinemas.md)); or the service is a small
part of a telecom or device company.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each service and each kind of income separately, with history beside the
forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Users | Monthly active users by service, on the free and paid tiers | Third-party counts of app users |
| Subscribers | Opening subscribers, plus those who join, less those who leave. Or active users times the share who pay | Net additions. Where the company gives only subscription revenue, split its growth into subscribers and price by assumption, and say so |
| Revenue per subscriber | Price rises, the mix of plans, discounts and bundles | Subscription revenue over average subscribers |
| Subscription revenue | Average subscribers times revenue per subscriber | As reported |
| Advertising | Time spent times ads shown times price, or active users times advertising revenue per user | Its own growth |
| Live streaming | Paying viewers times spend per payer | Segment revenue on its trend |
| Other income | Concerts, merchandise and licensing, each on its own row | Their own growth |

| Content sold on | Shows licensed to other services and broadcasters, part of it swapped for their shows without cash | Its own growth |
| Performers' share | The share of gifts paid to performers and agencies | Its past share |

| Marketing and development | Advertising for the service, and engineers | Their share of revenue |
| Acquisitions | A service bought, from the date it is consolidated, apart from the company's own growth | As announced |
| Investments | Gains and losses on stakes, and the share of associates' results, apart from operations | A named row, zero in the forecast |
| Debt and interest | Loans and convertible bonds, by the year they fall due, and their cost | As reported |
| Cash and payout | Cash, content commitments, dividends and buybacks | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Each quarter: revenue by line, paying subscribers and revenue per
  subscriber, active users, and content cost. Some services have stopped giving
  subscribers each quarter and give only subscription revenue. Video services give
  the content charge each quarter. Once a year they give the content bought, the split
  between licensed and own-made, and what they have committed to buy.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Users | Monthly app users (QuestMobile in China, Sensor Tower elsewhere) |
| Music market | Yearly recorded-music revenue by country (IFPI). The large labels' streaming revenue each quarter |
| Video | Rankings of shows by viewing, from data firms such as Yunhe in China. The slate of shows due |
| Peers | Other services' subscribers, prices and content spending |
| Rules | Rules on content, minors, live streaming and app-store fees |

- **What leads revenue.** The slate of shows and releases. Announced price changes. App
  users. The labels' streaming growth.
- **Backtest.** Average subscribers times revenue per subscriber against subscription
  revenue. Cash spent on content against the charge, over several years.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Rights owners take a large share of what a music service earns. Its margin moves with
  the terms at each renewal, with minimum payments, and with income that carries no
  royalty.
- Most of a video service's content is a fixed cost, so its margin rises with
  subscribers. The part paid as a share of revenue moves with revenue.
- Content is charged fastest in its first year, so a service that is growing its
  content spends more cash than it charges.
- Performers take much of what viewers spend on gifts.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little fixed capital. The assets are content, and the commitments
  to buy more do not all appear on the balance sheet.
- Subscribers pay in advance, so cash runs a little ahead of revenue.
- Many services hold large stakes in other companies.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Subscriptions hold up in a weak economy. Advertising does not.
- Rules can limit live streaming, gifts and what minors may spend.
- Traps:
  - subscriber growth bought with discounts and bundles;
  - a fall in revenue per subscriber hidden by more subscribers;
  - a split into subscribers and price that the company no longer discloses;
  - a hit show's quarter read as the run rate;
  - two services compared when they charge content over different periods;
  - growth lifted by a company bought during the year;
  - investment gains inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether prices can rise without losing subscribers.
- Whether content spending can be held.
- How much an advertising tier adds.
- How much time short video takes from them.
- What is paid out.

Yardstick: P/E once the service is profitable; EV/Sales, read with subscriber growth,
before that. For a service with heavy debt, EV/EBITDA after the content charge, with
the debt falling due beside it ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A music service has 100m monthly users. A fifth pay 10 a month: 20m subscribers give
2,400m a year. Rights owners take 55%, 1,320m, leaving 1,080m.

It lifts the share who pay to 22% and revenue per subscriber by 5%, to 10.5. Revenue is
22m times 10.5 times 12, which is 2,772m, up 15.5%.

A video service spends 1,000 on shows each year and charges each year's shows over three
years: 60%, 30% and 10%. With steady spending the charge is 1,000. If it raises spending
to 1,300, the charge is 780 plus 300 plus 100, which is 1,180, while cash out is 1,300.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
