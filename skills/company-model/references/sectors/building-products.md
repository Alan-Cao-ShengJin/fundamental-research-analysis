# Building products and home furnishings

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies make branded materials and fittings for buildings: waterproofing,
plasterboard, pipes, paint and coatings, tiles, bathroom fittings, doors and hardware,
flooring, and fitted furniture such as kitchens and wardrobes.

Who buys decides the price, the margin and how fast the maker is paid.

- **Developers and large contractors** buy in bulk at low prices and pay late, sometimes
  with bills or with flats in place of cash.
- **Dealers** resell to small contractors and to households. They pay sooner and the
  maker earns more.
- **Shops**, the maker's own or franchised, sell fitted furniture and finishing products
  to households, order by order.
- **Home-improvement chains, wholesalers and online shops**, which matter most outside
  China. One chain can be a third of a maker's sales, and its stock moves the maker's
  shipments.

Demand has three parts. New building needs structural products when work starts and
finishing products near completion. Renovation of existing homes is steadier and grows
as the stock of homes ages. Public works take pipes and waterproofing. Outside China
most demand for finishing products is repair and remodelling, which follows home sales,
home prices and interest rates. In a shrinking market the leaders grow by taking share
from small makers and by selling more kinds of product through the same dealers.

Numbers that get confused:

- Building **starts** against **completions**. Each product follows one or the other,
  months apart.
- Sales to **developers** against sales through **dealers and shops**.
- **Revenue** against **cash**. Receivables and bills can grow faster than sales.
- For fitted furniture: **shops**, **orders per shop** and **value per order**, and
  orders against deliveries.
- A price cut that follows **cheaper raw materials** against one forced by competition.

Signs the card does not fit: the product is a standard material priced by the market,
such as cement or flat glass (see the
[steel, aluminium and cement card](steel-aluminium-cement.md)); the company develops
property; it makes appliances (see the [home appliances card](home-appliances.md)); or
it retails other makers' products (see the [retail card](retail.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by product, and for some by kind of customer. Receivables, bills
  and the charges against them. The number of dealers and shops.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Building | Monthly starts, completions and home sales. Sales of second-hand homes, which lead renovation |
| Home fittings | Monthly retail sales of furniture and building materials, and an index of sales at building-materials markets |
| Inputs | Prices of bitumen, plastic resin, pigments, board and gas |
| Policy | Programmes to renovate old housing estates, and subsidies for home renovation |
| Other markets | Housing starts, sales of existing homes, and indices of spending on remodelling, such as the Harvard housing centre's quarterly index in the US. The large home-improvement chains' own sales |

- **What leads revenue.** Starts lead structural products by about half a year.
  Completions and home sales lead finishing products and furniture by half a year to a
  year. Input prices lead the margin by a quarter. Rivals' price notices.
- **Backtest.** Market times share against volume. Receivables against revenue by kind
  of customer.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Raw materials are a large share of cost. Many come from oil, and fittings use copper
  and zinc. Margin swings with them until prices follow.
- A brand that households ask for by name keeps its price. A product bought by a
  developer's purchasing team does not.
- Freight limits how far a product can travel, so a wide network of plants is a cost
  advantage.
- Selling to developers looks profitable until the cost of waiting for payment and of
  bad debts is counted.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Where developers are the customers, receivables are the main risk. A maker can report
  growing profit and no cash for years.
- Moving sales from developers to dealers lowers growth at first and brings the cash
  back.
- Plants are modest in cost and are added region by region.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- New building follows the property cycle. Renovation softens the fall and does not
  remove it.
- Traps:
  - growth won by giving developers longer credit;
  - receivables and bills growing faster than revenue;
  - bad-debt charges put off, or flats accepted in payment at more than they are worth;
  - a margin lifted by cheaper inputs;
  - dealers filled with stock;
  - more shops with fewer orders in each;
  - subsidies inside profit;
  - one retail chain behind a third of sales.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- When new building stops falling.
- How much renovation can replace it.
- Whether the leaders keep gaining share and hold their prices.
- Whether profit is turning into cash.
- Whether new product lines and exports work.

Yardstick: P/E, read with cash from operations
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker sells 60 to developers at a gross margin of 20% and 40 to dealers at 35%. Gross
profit is 12 plus 14, which is 26. Developers pay after a year and dealers at once.

A developer that owes 10 fails. The write-off is 10, more than a third of the year's
gross profit.

The maker moves 20 of sales from developers to dealers. Revenue is unchanged, gross
profit rises to 8 plus 21, which is 29, and receivables fall by a third.

A maker that sells through retail chains reports volume up 2%, price up 5% and mix down
1%: revenue grows 6%. If a chain then cuts its stock by 3% of the maker's yearly sales,
shipments fall 1% while shoppers buy 2% more.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
