# Wind equipment

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Wind equipment makers supply the developers of wind farms.

- **Turbine makers** win orders by tender, deliver about a year later onshore and two to
  three years later offshore, and book revenue at delivery or as the project is built.
  Many then service the turbines for years under contract.
- **Component makers** supply blades, towers, gearboxes, bearings, castings and, for
  farms at sea, foundations and undersea cables. Their sales follow the turbine makers'
  deliveries and what is being built at sea.
- Some turbine makers also **build wind farms**, to sell or to keep. A farm that is kept
  goes on the [power generation card](power-generation.md).

Developers decide demand. They order when a farm's return clears their hurdle, which
depends on the power price or the support scheme, interest rates, permits and a grid
connection. Governments set the pace through auctions and targets. Turbines keep getting
larger, so a market can grow in power while the number of turbines falls.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Orders won**, the **backlog**, **deliveries** and **installations** connected to the
  grid. Each is a different date for the same turbine.
- The **tender price** today against the average price in the **backlog** being
  delivered.
- Price **per unit of power**, with and without the tower, against revenue per turbine.
  Use the tender price that matches what the company's revenue includes.
- **Onshore** against **offshore**, which is dearer, slower and riskier.
- **Turbine sales** against **service**, which is steadier and earns more.

Signs the card does not fit: the company mainly owns wind farms and sells power; it
makes solar equipment (see the [solar card](solar.md)); or it makes cables or bearings
mainly for other industries.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Orders and backlog each quarter in units of power, split onshore and
  offshore and by market, with the average price for Western makers. Deliveries, service
  revenue and margin by segment. Chinese makers give orders in hand and external sales in
  units of power, and the mix by turbine size.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Tenders and prices | In China, public tender volumes and winning prices, tracked monthly by brokers and trade press. Elsewhere, auction results by country |
| Installations | Monthly new capacity connected in China (National Energy Administration). Yearly installations and makers' shares by country (Global Wind Energy Council, the Chinese Wind Energy Association, BloombergNEF) |
| Offshore | Project lists with approval and building dates, by province or country |
| Costs | Prices of steel plate, copper, resin and glass or carbon fibre |
| Rules | Auction calendars, support schemes, grid connection rules and trade measures |

- **What leads revenue.** Tender volumes and prices lead revenue by about a year onshore
  and two to three years offshore. Approvals of offshore projects. The year-end rush to
  connect before a deadline. Material prices.
- **Backtest.** Deliveries times the average price against turbine revenue. Tenders of a
  year or two ago against deliveries now.

## 4. Costs and margin

- A turbine's price is fixed at the tender and its cost is known only when it is built.
  Rising material and transport costs in between come out of the margin.
- Makers have often won share by cutting price. The margin on those orders shows a year
  or two later.
- Each new, larger model lowers cost per unit of power and brings the risk of a fault
  that has to be fixed across every turbine sold.
- Service earns a much higher margin than turbines and grows with the fleet.
- Offshore work earns more when it goes well, and loses far more when a project is
  delayed.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Larger turbines need new blade moulds and factories, often near the coast.
- Customers pay in stages and hold back a part until the turbines have run for some
  time. Receivables are long, and cash arrives late in the year.
- Warranty runs for several years, so today's profit depends on estimates of future
  repairs.
- A farm built to sell sits on the balance sheet, funded by debt, until a buyer is
  found. A year without sales leaves the debt and no gain.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Orders come in waves around policy changes. A rush before a subsidy ends is followed
  by a slump.
- Offshore projects wait on approvals for sea use, shipping lanes and grid links, and
  slip by years.
- Trade measures and local-content rules decide who can sell where.
- Traps:
  - orders or backlog read as this year's revenue;
  - today's tender price applied to turbines sold a year ago;
  - a margin recovery assumed before the low-priced orders have been delivered;
  - warranty charges treated as one-off;
  - gains on wind farms sold, read as recurring;
  - installations counted in power while the number of turbines falls;
  - receivables growing faster than revenue.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether tender prices have stopped falling.
- When offshore building speeds up.
- Whether makers can sell abroad at better prices.
- How large quality costs on new models will be.
- How much of profit comes from selling wind farms.

Yardstick: P/E when orders and prices are rising; P/B in a downturn
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A turbine maker starts with a backlog of 30 units of power, wins 15 and delivers 12. The
backlog ends at 33.

This year's deliveries were sold a year ago at 100 a unit and cost 92 to build: revenue
is 1,200 and gross profit 96, a margin of 8%. Orders won this year were priced at 90. If
cost falls to 85 with larger turbines, next year's margin is 5.6%. If cost stays at 92,
those orders lose money.

It services 200 units of power for a fee of 2 each: 400 of revenue at a margin of 25%,
which is 100, more than the turbines earn.

It also builds a wind farm of 10 units of power at a cost of 60 a unit and sells it for
75: a gain of 150, more than the turbines' 96. The next year it sells none.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
