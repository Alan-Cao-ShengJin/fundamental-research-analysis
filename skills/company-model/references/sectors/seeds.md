# Seeds

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Seed companies breed crop varieties and sell the seed to farmers each season: maize,
rice, soybeans, wheat and vegetables. Hybrid seed has to be bought new every year.

The business runs on a fixed calendar.

- **Breeding** takes years, and a new variety needs approval before it can be sold.
- **Seed production** is contracted with growers a year ahead. Its cost is set by the
  area contracted, the yield and what growers are paid, which follows the grain price.
- **Selling** happens before planting, through dealers who pay deposits in advance.
  After the season dealers return what they did not sell. Revenue is usually booked
  when the seed ships, less the returns expected, and rebates to dealers are settled
  afterwards.

Demand is the area planted to each crop, times the seed used on it, times the share of
farmers who buy seed, times the company's share. Area follows grain prices and policy.
A company's share follows its varieties: a new one can take a large share within a few
seasons, and an old one fades.

Where genetically modified crops are allowed, the owner of a trait is paid a fee on
each bag, and seed carrying the trait sells for more.

Numbers that get confused:

- The **selling season**, which runs across the year end, against the financial year.
- Seed **shipped to dealers** against seed **sold to farmers**. Returns sit between
  them.
- **Advances** from dealers, which show how the coming season is going. Read them at the
  date deposits are highest for the crop, which may not be the year end.
- Seed **produced** against seed **sold**, and the stock carried over.
- The price of a **bag** against the part that is the trait owner's fee.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes crop chemicals or fertiliser (see the
[specialty chemicals card](specialty-chemicals.md) and the
[commodity chemicals card](commodity-chemicals.md)); it farms animals (see the
[livestock and feed card](livestock-feed.md)); or it trades grain (see the
[trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue and margin by crop once or twice a year, stock, and advances from
  dealers at each period end. Few give volume and price by variety.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Area | Official planting area and intentions (the agriculture ministry in China, the USDA in the US, the crop agency in Brazil) |
| Seed supply | The yearly area and yield of seed production, and the industry's stock against its use, for hybrid maize and rice |
| Prices | Grain prices, spot and futures. Seed prices by variety, from dealers |
| Approvals | Lists of varieties approved, and approvals for genetically modified crops |
| Weather | Conditions in the regions where seed is produced |

- **What leads revenue.** Dealers' advances at the year end. Grain prices in the autumn,
  which decide what is planted and what growers are paid. The autumn figures for seed
  produced. Approval lists.
- **Backtest.** Volume times price against revenue by crop. Advances at the year end
  against the next season's revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- The cost of seed is fixed a year before it is sold, so a fall in price goes straight
  to the margin.
- A variety only the company can sell earns a high margin. A variety many can sell is
  priced like grain plus a little.
- When the industry has produced too much, prices fall and old stock is written down.
- Research is a fixed cost that takes years to pay.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Stock is large and seasonal, and seed loses its value as it ages.
- In China dealers pay in advance, which funds the season. In Brazil and elsewhere seed
  is sold on credit to farmers, which brings bad debts.
- Several companies have grown by acquisition and carry goodwill and debt.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Seed supply follows a cycle of its own: a good year brings more seed production, then
  a glut.
- Governments decide which varieties and traits may be sold, and when.
- Traps:
  - shipments counted before returns;
  - a leading variety past its peak;
  - the industry's stock ignored;
  - research added to the balance sheet;
  - credit losses on farmers abroad, and currency moves on those sales;
  - a timetable for genetically modified crops assumed before approvals;
  - a season that falls either side of the year end.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How fast genetically modified crops are adopted, and how much of their value the seed
  company keeps.
- Whether the industry's stock clears.
- Whether the next varieties replace the current leaders.
- What the businesses abroad earn.

Yardstick: P/E, read with the varieties due. Where earnings are thin, investors look to
revenue and to what the business abroad is worth
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company contracts growers to produce 100 units of seed at 12 each, a price set by last
year's grain price, and spends 3 a unit on processing. It ships 95 to dealers at 30 and
10 come back. Revenue is 85 times 30, which is 2,550. The seed sold cost 1,275, so gross
profit is 1,275.

It is left with 15 units at a cost of 225. If old seed can be sold next season for only
12 a unit, it writes down 45.

A new variety carrying a trait sells for 40, of which 6 is the trait owner's fee. The
company keeps 34.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
