# Buses and coaches

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- **City buses** go to transit operators and city governments, mostly by public tender.
  Demand follows budgets, subsidy rules, the age of the fleet and zero-emission rules.
- **Coaches** go mostly to private intercity and tour operators, and to some public
  ones. Demand follows travel, and falls where rail takes the route.
- **School, staff and light buses** go to schools, employers and small operators, often
  through dealers.
- **Exports** are won as tenders or through local partners. They arrive in large lots,
  sometimes with local assembly and financing support.
- **After-sales**: parts, mid-life refurbishment and, for electric buses, replacement
  batteries.
- **Where it sells.** Some makers sell from one home base and export. Others build in
  each market they serve. "Home" below means any market the company builds and sells
  in, each with its own data.

Most buses are sold direct to the operator, so there is little dealer stock. Where
dealers do hold stock, as with school buses in some markets, add the dealer-stock row
from the [truck card](trucks.md).

Makers count differently. Some report units by size class (large, medium, light). Some
report equivalent units, where an articulated bus counts as two. Check the definition
before comparing a company with an industry series, and use the company's own unit for
volumes and for revenue per bus.

Signs the card does not fit: the company mainly sells chassis or kits to body builders;
it runs bus services rather than making buses; buses are a small line inside a truck
group; or charging, batteries or financing earn more than about 15% of profit, in which
case that unit gets its own card.

## 2. The driver tree

Each row is a row, or a block of rows, in the revenue build, with history beside the
forecast.

There are two routes to units. Use one as the main route and the other as a check.
Where buses are ordered and delivered within months and industry sales by maker are
published, build from industry sales and share. Where the company sells by tender and
reports a backlog, build from the backlog.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry sales at home | By end use (city bus, coach, school and other) and size class, on one stated basis and one length cut-off | Use the series that exists and state its basis and cut-off |
| Split by powertrain | Electric, hybrid, gas, fuel cell and diesel, as far as the market's rules treat them differently, within each end use | One row for the electric share |
| Company share | Share of each cell, giving home units | Take shares from registration data by maker and scale them to the company's reported total |
| Home units | The sum of the cells. Where the company reports only total sales, home units are total sales less exports | |
| Firm backlog | Opening backlog, plus new firm orders, plus options converted, less deliveries | Leave the backlog rows out and use share times industry |
| Options | Opening options, plus new options, less those converted and those that expire | |
| Deliveries from backlog | The firm backlog due in the period, limited by production slots and by the scarcest supplier | |
| Export units | Named contracts with their delivery dates, plus a base level by region | One row, grown from the same period a year earlier |
| Revenue per bus | By size class and powertrain, home and export separately, on the unit the company counts in | Home and export averages, or one group average, labelled as such |
| Bus revenue | Units times revenue per bus, by line | |
| Parts, service and refurbishment | Buses in service times spend per bus. The fleet is the maker's own past deliveries less retirements, plus other makers' buses where it sells parts for them | The past ratio of this revenue to bus revenue, labelled as such |
| Pre-owned buses | Buses taken in trade and resold, where material | Leave them inside bus revenue |

Units alone mislead. One large electric city bus can bring the revenue of several light
buses, so build the mix before the total.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Reports give units by size class, and revenue at home and abroad where
  disclosed. Some companies publish a monthly production and sales bulletin. Makers that
  sell to public agencies often report a backlog, split into firm orders and options.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly sales by maker from the bus industry's statistics network, relayed by trade sites, for buses above a stated length. Registrations from insurance records, by end use and maker. Exports by maker |
| Europe | Registrations by country and fuel from the manufacturers' association (ACEA), quarterly, and from national motor trade bodies (SMMT in the UK). City buses and coaches move differently |
| North America | Federal transit funding awards for bus purchases. The National Transit Database, for fleets and their ages. The coach builders' sales survey. The makers' own backlogs and bid pipelines |
| Elsewhere | Tender announcements and national associations, of uneven detail |

- **What leads revenue.** Public funding awards, which lead orders, which lead deliveries,
  often by more than a year. Tender awards and option exercises. The start and end dates of
  subsidy programmes. City budgets. Travel volumes for coaches. The age of the fleet.
- **Backtest.** Industry sales times share against the company's reported units, after
  matching the length cut-off and whether light buses are included. Units times revenue
  per bus against reported revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost: chassis, engine or battery pack, and body. In an
  electric bus the battery is the largest single item.
- Volumes are low and buses are built to order, so labour is a larger share than in
  trucks, and the margin depends on how full the plant is.
- Margin depends on what is sold and where: export against home, electric against
  diesel, large against light. Carry a margin for each line.
- A tender price is fixed at award. Costs that rise between award and delivery come out
  of the margin, which matters most where the backlog runs for years.
- Battery warranties and service commitments are costs that arrive later.
- Parts and refurbishment earn more per unit of revenue than new buses.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Where the backlog is long, deliveries are limited by production slots and by the
  scarcest supplier. Elsewhere assembly capacity is rarely the limit. Local-content rules
  can require a plant in the buyer's country.
- Receivables can be long: some public buyers pay slowly, subsidies can be owed by the
  state for years, and overseas buyers need credit. Where buyers pay on delivery or in
  stages they are short. Check which.
- Makers often guarantee their customers' loans or promise to buy buses back. These are
  commitments outside the balance sheet until a customer defaults.
- Tender work brings customer deposits and work in progress. Show both.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Policy sets the cycle. A subsidy pulls purchases forward before it ends and leaves a
  gap after it. Test whether the latest strong period is such a pull-forward.
- Buses bought in one subsidy wave come up for replacement together.
- A subsidy for replacing batteries can delay purchases of new buses.
- Export sales arrive in large lots. One tender can move a quarter, and a tariff,
  local-content rule or procurement ban can close a market.
- Traps:
  - series with different length cut-offs, which do not reconcile;
  - units against equivalent units;
  - options counted as if they were firm orders;
  - factory sales that include exports against home registrations;
  - revenue per bus read as a price, when it also carries mix and currency;
  - subsidies inside revenue or receivables;
  - sales to operating or leasing companies in the same group.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether exports, electric buses above all, are a lasting business or a window that
  closes as local-content rules and tariffs spread.
- Whether home demand holds after a subsidy-driven replacement wave.
- Whether the margin on electric buses falls as battery costs fall and rivals follow.
- Whether receivables and guarantees hide losses.
- For a maker that pays out most of its profit, whether the dividend can be sustained.

Yardstick: P/E, dividend yield for a high-payout maker, and EV/EBITDA for a maker with a
long order book and debt ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

The home industry sells 100 city buses, 60 electric and 40 diesel, and 80 coaches. The
company's shares are 30%, 20% and 50%, so it sells 18 + 8 + 40 = 66 at home. Exports are
one contract for 20 electric buses delivered this year, plus a base of 30, so 50.

Revenue per bus is 200 for an electric city bus, 90 for a diesel one, 110 for a coach,
320 for an exported electric bus and 150 for other exports. Revenue is 3,600 + 720 +
4,400 + 6,400 + 4,500 = 19,620 on 116 buses.

If the 20-bus contract slips into next year, units fall 17% to 96 but revenue falls 33%
to 13,220. Units times an average price would have caught only about half of the fall.

On the backlog route, an opening firm backlog of 500, new firm orders of 300 and 100
options converted, less 350 delivered, leaves 550. Options start at 400, gain 150, lose
the 100 converted and 50 that expire, and end at 400.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
