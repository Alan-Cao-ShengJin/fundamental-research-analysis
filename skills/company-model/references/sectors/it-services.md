# IT services

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies sell their people's time and skill under contracts: consulting, building
and maintaining software systems, running a client's technology, and handling its
back-office processes. Three kinds are built a little differently.

- **Global IT services firms** bill by the hour or day, at a fixed price for a project,
  or as a yearly fee for running something. Much of their staff works in lower-cost
  countries. They report the share of time billed, the share of staff who leave, and the
  value of contracts signed.
- **Outsourcers paid per head**, common in China, place engineers in a client's teams
  and bill for each person each month. One or two clients can be most of the revenue.
  Headcount and revenue per employee are the drivers.
- **Systems integrators**, also common in China, resell hardware and software as part of
  a project, often for government and state-owned clients, and are paid late.

Clients' budgets decide demand. Projects that can be put off are cut first in a
slowdown, and outsourcing contracts run on. New technology creates work, and it can also
reduce the hours a task needs.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Contracts signed**, stated as total value over their life, against revenue.
- **Staff** against **billable staff**, and the share of their time that is billed.
- Growth **at constant exchange rates** against reported growth.
- Revenue that includes **hardware resold** and **subcontractors** against the company's
  own work.
- Growth from the company's **own business** against growth from acquisitions.
- **Revenue** against **cash**, where clients pay at the end of the year.
- A themed sales figure, such as sales tied to AI, that matches no line of reported
  revenue.

Signs the card does not fit: the company sells its own software for a licence or
subscription (see the [software card](software.md)); it supplies temporary workers or
recruits staff (see the [staffing card](staffing.md)); it tests and inspects products
(see the [testing and inspection card](testing-inspection.md)); or it rents out
computing capacity it owns, which needs capex and debt (see the
[towers and data centres card](towers-data-centres.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Large IT services companies report each quarter: growth at constant
  exchange rates by client industry and region, the value of contracts signed, staff
  numbers, the share who left, the share of time billed, and a margin bridge. Most guide
  growth for the year. Chinese companies report twice a year: revenue by line, total
  staff, the largest clients' share, and receivables. Integrators that book revenue at
  acceptance earn most of it in the last quarter.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Clients | Forecasts of technology spending by industry (Gartner, IDC). Clients' own results in the main industries served |
| Peers | The results and guidance of the largest companies, which report first |
| Contracts | Quarterly counts of outsourcing contracts signed (ISG) |
| Labour | Job postings, pay surveys and visa rules |
| China | Monthly revenue of the software and IT services industry (the industry ministry), and the largest client's own annual report |
| India | The industry association's yearly outlook, and the large companies' quarterly fact sheets |

- **What leads revenue.** Contracts signed lead revenue by two to four quarters. Hiring
  shows what management expects. A low share of time billed means room to grow without
  hiring. Clients set budgets at the start of the year.
- **Backtest.** Average staff times revenue per employee against revenue. Contracts
  signed against revenue a few quarters later.

## 4. Costs and margin

- People are most of the cost. Pay rises once or twice a year and lowers the margin in
  those quarters.
- The margin is managed with a few levers: more time billed, more work done in low-cost
  locations, more junior staff under each senior one.
- A large contract earns little in its first year, while the work is being taken over.
- Clients ask for lower prices at each renewal, and expect any saving from new tools to
  be shared with them.
- Resold hardware adds revenue at almost no margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little fixed capital. Capacity is people, hired a few months ahead
  of the work.
- Cash is tied up in receivables and in work done but not yet billed. At Chinese
  companies the two can exceed half a year's revenue, and government clients pay slowly.
- Growth by acquisition brings goodwill.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows clients' profits. Work that can be put off falls first and recovers
  first.
- Visa rules and taxes on outsourcing change where work can be done.
- Clients' shutdowns at the year end, and the number of working days, move a quarter's
  revenue.
- Traps:
  - contracts signed that are mostly renewals;
  - hiring read as proof of demand, when it shows only what management expects;
  - a share of time billed that is already at its ceiling;
  - revenue growth from resold hardware;
  - a margin helped by a weak home currency;
  - one large contract ending, or one client behind most of the revenue;
  - work done but not billed growing faster than revenue;
  - growth that was bought.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether AI reduces the hours clients pay for, or creates more work.
- When clients spend again on projects they have put off.
- Whether prices hold.
- Whether clients bring the work back in-house.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company has 1,000 billable staff. They bill 80% of 1,800 hours a year at 50 an hour:
revenue is 72m. Staff cost 50,000 each, 50m in all, so gross profit is 22m.

If the share of time billed rises to 82%, revenue rises 1.8m with no more cost, and
gross profit rises 8%. A pay rise of 6% costs 3m, 14% of gross profit, unless rates rise
too.

An outsourcer paid per head has 10,000 staff earning 250,000 of revenue each: 2,500m.
Its main client is 1,000m of that. The client cuts its rate 5% and takes a tenth of the
work back in-house. Its revenue falls to 855m, and group revenue falls 5.8% before any
growth elsewhere.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
