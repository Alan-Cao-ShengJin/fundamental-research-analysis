# Generic drugs and drug ingredients

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies make drugs that are no longer protected by patents, and the ingredients
that go into them.

- **Generic drugs** are copies of older drugs, sold on price. **Biosimilars** are the
  same for biological drugs, and cost far more to develop.
- **Branded generics and over-the-counter products** are sold under a brand to doctors,
  pharmacies and consumers, and hold their prices better.
- **Active ingredients and intermediates** are sold to other drug makers by weight.
  Their prices follow capacity in the industry.

The amount of medicine used grows slowly with an ageing population. What a maker earns
depends on how price is set in each market, so build each market in its own way.

- **China and other tender markets.** The state buys the main generics in tenders, drug
  by drug. The winners are promised most of the hospitals' volume at a much lower
  price. The losers lose the hospital market for that drug.
- **The US.** A few buying groups take most of the volume, and competition pushes the
  price of older generics down every year. A maker earns most on a newly approved
  product, above all when it is the first copy, and the launch date is often fixed by a
  settlement with the patent owner. One such product can be much of a year's profit,
  and then collapse when more copies arrive.
- **Branded markets**, such as India, Russia and other emerging countries. Drugs are sold
  under brands through doctors and pharmacies. The maker raises prices most years,
  within any price controls, and growth comes from the sales force and new brands.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Volume** against **price**, drug by drug. A tender win can lift volume and still cut
  revenue.
- A tender **won** against sales under it, which start months later and run for a set
  term.
- Revenue booked at the **price to the hospital**, with the distributor's cut shown as a
  selling cost, against revenue booked at the price to the distributor.
- In the US, the **list price** against what is kept after rebates and price
  adjustments.
- Profit **with** and **without** a product that has few rivals for a short time.
- Ingredients sold to **others** against those the company uses itself.

Signs the card does not fit: new patented drugs and a research pipeline are most of the
value (see the [innovative drugs card](pharma.md)); the company makes drugs under
contract for others (see the
[contract research and manufacturing card](contract-research-manufacturing.md)); or it
distributes drugs (see the [drug distribution card](drug-distribution.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each market separately, and within it the largest products one by one and the
rest as a group, with history beside the forecast. Where the company gives revenue by
segment and by region but not one by the other, build by segment and state the split
assumed.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tender markets | By product: the volume allotted after a tender times the tender price. Before a tender, the market's volume times share, at the current price. A maker that sold none of a drug gains its whole allotment as new revenue | Revenue over volume, with volume growth |
| Tenders ahead | Which products face the next rounds, and which expired tenders are bid again, with the likely price cut and the chance of winning | A named risk on each product not yet tendered |

| Branded markets | For each country: the market's growth, the company's rank and share, price rises within any controls, new brands and the size of the sales force | Growth by country as reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by region and product group each quarter, the number of filings
  awaiting approval, launches, results of tenders and the findings of plant
  inspections. Few give sales by product.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Tenders | The rules and results of each national and regional tender, which are public |
| Approvals | Lists of approvals and filings from the drug regulators. Inspection findings and warning letters. Patent settlements and court dates |
| Sales | Hospital and pharmacy sales by drug from data vendors (IQVIA and local equivalents), mostly paid and quoted by brokers. Weekly US prescriptions. Monthly pharmacy sales by company in India |
| Ingredients | Published prices of bulk ingredients and vitamins, where they exist. Customs export prices |
| Policy | Insurance payment rules, price-control lists, and rules on which copies count as equivalent |

- **What leads revenue.** Tender results come months before the new prices apply.
  Approvals and launch dates. Rivals' filings for the same drug. Weekly prices of
  ingredients. The status of plant inspections.
- **Backtest.** Volume times price against revenue for the largest products. Published
  ingredient prices against the ingredient segment's revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- After a tender, price and gross margin fall, and at most makers selling cost falls
  with them. Profit depends on whether the extra volume and the saved selling cost make
  up for the price.
- The lowest-cost maker wins tenders and still earns. Others win and lose money, or
  lose the market.
- In the US the base range earns little, and a few new products earn most of the
  profit.
- Branded markets earn a high gross margin and carry a large sales force.
- Ingredients behave like commodities: margins rise when a rival's plant is shut and
  fall when capacity is added.
- Harder products, such as injectables and biosimilars, keep their prices longer and
  cost more to develop.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Plants must meet the standards of every market they export to, and are inspected.
- Developing a range of new copies takes steady research spending with uncertain
  returns.
- Distributors and hospitals pay slowly in some markets.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand is steady. In tender markets policy pushes prices down, and in the US
  competition does.
- Ingredient prices move in cycles of their own.
- Traps:
  - a tender win read as growth before the price cut is counted;
  - a product not yet tendered treated as safe;
  - distributors stocking up after a win, read as demand;
  - a falling selling-cost ratio read as efficiency;
  - a year lifted by one product with few rivals read as normal;
  - a P/E on earnings that include such a product;
  - research costs moved to the balance sheet;
  - a year of high ingredient prices read as normal;
  - a plant under a regulator's warning;
  - grants inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How much of the range has still to go through tenders.
- What replaces a product whose rivals are about to arrive.
- Whether the move into biosimilars and new drugs is real.
- Where ingredient prices are in their cycle.
- Whether exports of harder products can grow.

Yardstick: P/E, read with growth, on earnings without any product that has few rivals
for a short time and without the losses of a unit developing new drugs ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A drug sells 100 units to hospitals at 10: revenue is 1,000. It costs 1 a unit to make
and 4 a unit to promote. Profit is 500.

In a tender the company wins at a price of 3 and is allotted 175 units. Revenue is
525, down by nearly half. Manufacture costs 175 and promotion falls to 0.15 a unit,
which is 26. Profit is 324, down 35%. A maker that sold none of the drug before and
wins the same 175 units adds 525 of revenue.

In the US, a first copy sells 100 units in six months at 60, against the original's 100:
revenue is 6,000. Then five rivals arrive. The price falls to 12 and the company keeps
30 units: revenue for the next six months is 360.

An ingredient maker sells 4,000 tonnes at 0.5 a tonne: revenue is 2,000 and, at a cost
of 0.35, profit is 600. A rival's new plant cuts the price 15%. On the same tonnes,
revenue is 1,700 and profit 300, half what it was.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
