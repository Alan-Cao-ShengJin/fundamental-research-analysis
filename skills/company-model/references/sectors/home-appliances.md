# Home appliances

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Appliance makers sell air conditioners, fridges, washing machines, kitchen appliances,
water heaters, televisions and small appliances. Many have other units:

- **Parts** sold to other makers, such as compressors and motors.
- **Air conditioning for buildings**, sold project by project.
- **Goods made for foreign brands** to sell under their own names, at a thin margin.
- **Robots and automation**, which belong on the [machinery card](machinery.md).

Goods reach buyers through dealers, retail chains, the maker's own online shops, and
bulk sales to property developers. In the US and Europe a few retail chains and
homebuilders take most of the sales. Where dealers stand in between, the maker's shipments
and what households buy can differ for several quarters.

Three things drive demand at home: replacing old appliances, fitting out new homes, and
first purchases of things households did not own before. Hot summers sell air
conditioners. Government trade-in subsidies pull purchases forward. Exports follow
demand abroad, foreign retailers' stock, and tariffs. A maker that produces where it
sells competes with imports, and tariffs on them and on its own parts move its margin.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Shipments** from the factory, home and export apart, against **retail sales** and
  against **installations**.
- **Stock in the channel.**
- **Volume** against **average price**, which moves with the mix and with price wars.
- **Rebates** promised to dealers, which sit on the balance sheet and can be built up or
  released.
- Exports under the maker's **own brand** against goods made for **others**.

Signs the card does not fit: the company sells robot vacuums and other consumer
electronics (see the
[consumer electronics brands card](consumer-electronics-brands.md)); it makes furniture
and fittings (see the [building products card](building-products.md)); or it mainly
makes parts for cars.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese makers give revenue by product and by home and abroad twice a
  year, and totals each quarter. US and European makers give sales by region each
  quarter, the industry's growth as they see it, and a bridge of the margin into price
  and mix, materials, tariffs and cost savings. Few give units.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Shipments | Monthly production, shipments at home and abroad, and stock for the main appliances, with makers' production plans for the coming months (ChinaIOL) |
| Retail | Weekly and monthly retail volume and average price, online and in shops (AVC) |
| Official data | Monthly output. Customs exports by product. Housing completions |
| Policy | Trade-in subsidy rules and how much funding is left |
| Inputs | Prices of copper, aluminium, steel and plastics |
| Other markets | Appliance shipments from each country's trade association, some of them paid data. Housing starts and sales of existing homes. Tariff decisions |

- **What leads revenue.** Makers' production plans. Weekly retail data. Input prices,
  which reach cost a quarter later. Housing completions. Subsidy announcements. Summer
  temperatures. Export orders.
- **Backtest.** Industry shipments times share against the company's volume. Volume
  times average price against revenue by product.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost. Their prices reach the margin about a quarter later,
  after stock and hedges.
- Where a few large brands hold most of a market, they have kept prices up when costs
  fell. A new low-priced entrant can end that.
- Selling direct and online removes the dealer's margin and moves some of it to the
  maker.
- Goods made for other brands earn much less than the maker's own.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Plants are cheap against the cash a strong maker produces. The large Chinese makers
  hold net cash and pay out much of their profit. Some Western makers carry heavy debt,
  and its cost matters as much as the margin.
- In China dealers pay before delivery in good times, which funds the maker. Large
  retailers elsewhere pay late.
- Plants abroad are built to get inside tariff walls.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows housing and the age of appliances in use. A subsidy lifts one year and
  weakens the next.
- Exports swing with foreign retailers' stock and with tariffs.
- Traps:
  - shipments above retail sales while the channel fills;
  - a subsidy year read as normal;
  - a lower average price that is a price war, read as mix;
  - a margin lifted by cheap materials;
  - rebates released into profit;
  - gains on currency and interest on cash read as operating profit;
  - exports rushed out before a tariff;
  - a few retailers owing most of the receivables.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- What happens to demand when subsidies end.
- Whether the leaders hold their prices against new entrants.
- How much the maker's own brands can grow abroad.
- Whether the business-customer units become large.
- How much is paid out.
- For makers that face imports, what tariffs do.

Yardstick: P/E, with dividend yield beside it. For a maker with heavy debt or thin
earnings, EV/EBITDA and the debt falling due
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker sells 100 units at 3,000: revenue is 300,000. Materials are 1,950 a unit and
other factory costs 300, so gross profit is 750 a unit. Selling and other costs are 15%
of revenue. Profit is 30,000.

Copper is a fifth of the materials, 390 a unit. If copper rises 20%, cost rises 78 a unit
and profit falls by 7,800, about a quarter, unless the price rises 2.6%.

Households buy 100 units and dealers add 10 to stock: the maker ships 110. Next year
households buy 105 and dealers cut stock by 5. The maker ships 100, down 9%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
