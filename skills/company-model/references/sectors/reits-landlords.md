# REITs and property landlords

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A landlord owns buildings and lets the space: shops and malls, offices, warehouses, flats,
and specialist buildings such as clinics or student housing. A real estate investment
trust (REIT) is a landlord that pays out most of its income and, in return, pays little or
no tax on it. Some are run by their own staff. Others pay an outside manager, often part
of the group that sold them the buildings.

Tenants decide demand. Shop tenants follow their own sales, office tenants follow jobs,
and warehouse tenants follow trade and online shopping. New buildings coming onto the
market set how much choice tenants have. Rent changes slowly, because only the leases
that expire in a year are repriced. Interest rates matter as much as rent: they set the
cost of the debt and the price buyers will pay for the buildings.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Passing rent**, which tenants pay now, against **market rent**, which a new lease
  would pay. The gap is the **reversion** when a lease expires.
- **Net property income**, which is rent less the cost of running the buildings.
  **Distributable income**, which is what is left after management costs, interest and
  tax. **Reported profit**, which under some accounting rules also includes changes in
  the buildings' value.
- **Funds from operations (FFO)**, the measure used in the US: profit before depreciation
  and gains on sales. **Adjusted FFO** also takes off recurring capex and rent booked but
  not yet received. Each company defines the adjusted figure itself.
- **Same-property** growth against total growth, which includes buildings bought and
  sold.
- The **cap rate**, which is a building's net income over its value.

Signs the card does not fit: the company mainly builds homes for sale (see the
[developers card](property-developers.md)); it manages buildings for other owners (see
the [property management card](property-management.md)); it holds mortgages and not
buildings, which is a lending business built from what the loans earn less what the
funding costs (see the [non-bank lenders card](non-bank-lenders.md)); or it owns hotels,
data centres or towers, which have their own drivers. A trust with a small holding of
these builds it as a separate unit.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each property type and market separately, with history beside the forecast. Keep
the buildings owned throughout both periods apart from those bought, sold or under
development.

A trust that grows mainly by buying buildings needs the purchases row built with care:
the amount it plans to buy, the yield it buys at, and how each purchase is paid for.

A trust with thousands of small buildings on long leases does not report area. Build it
from annualised rent: opening rent, plus the increases written into leases, plus or
minus the change on leases that expire, less rent lost to tenants that fail, plus net
purchases at the guided amount and yield, spread through the year.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Trusts report twice a year or quarterly: occupancy, reversion, the share
  of leases expiring each year, the average lease length, the largest tenants, and for
  malls the tenants' sales and visitor numbers. On debt they give the amount due each
  year, the share at fixed rates and the average cost. In most markets the buildings are
  revalued once or twice a year, and the cap rates used are published. US trusts keep
  buildings at cost and do not revalue. They publish a quarterly supplement with
  same-property figures, leasing spreads and the yields on purchases.
- **Industry**, which differs by city and property type:

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Property consultants (CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield, Colliers, Savills, Knight Frank) | Quarterly rents, vacancy, new supply and space taken up, by city and property type. Prices and yields of buildings sold |
| Government | Rent and price indices by property type, where the valuation or planning office publishes them. Retail sales, employment and trade |
| Supply | Buildings under construction and their completion dates, from consultants and planning records |
| Rates | Government bond yields and interbank rates, which lead the cost of debt |
| Other trusts | Their reversion, occupancy and valuations in the same city |

- **What leads income.** The gap between passing and market rent on leases about to
  expire. New supply due. Tenants' sales. Forward interest rates against the loans
  falling due. Prices paid for buildings, which lead valuations.
- **Backtest.** Area times occupancy times rent against rental revenue. Debt times its
  cost against interest. Last year's expiring leases times the reported reversion against
  the change in rent.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Who pays the running costs depends on the lease. Where tenants pay them, net property
  income is almost all of rent. Where the landlord pays, it is a smaller share and moves
  with energy and wage costs.
- Interest is the largest cost after running the buildings. For a trust with heavy debt,
  a rise in rates can take away more than a good year of rent adds.
- An outside manager's fees rise with the size of the trust, whether or not unitholders
  are better off.
- Empty space still costs upkeep and tax, so a fall in occupancy cuts income by more than
  the lost rent.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A trust pays out most of its income, so it can fund little growth from what it keeps.
  The rest comes from new units and new debt. A purchase adds to income per unit only if
  its yield is above the cost of both.
- When the unit price is low, new units cost more than purchases yield, and purchases
  stop.
- Gearing is capped, by regulation in some markets and by loan terms in others. A fall in
  property values raises gearing with no new borrowing.
- Older buildings need refurbishment from time to time to hold their rent. This is capex,
  and it does not show in distributable income.
- Land held on a lease that runs out loses value as the end approaches.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Interest rates work twice: on the cost of debt, and on the cap rate and so on value.
- Each property type has its own supply cycle. Rents fall when a wave of new buildings
  completes, whatever demand is doing.
- Some changes are lasting: online shopping for malls, home working for offices.
- Traps:
  - a distribution held up by paying the manager in units, by paying out capital or
    gains on sales, or by income support from the seller of a building;
  - occupancy bought with rent-free periods;
  - reversion quoted for part of the portfolio;
  - revaluation gains read as profit;
  - valuations that lag the prices buildings actually sell for;
  - a purchase from the trust's own sponsor at the sponsor's price;
  - cheap debt about to be refinanced at today's rates;
  - income from overseas buildings before currency;
  - adjusted FFO compared across companies that define it differently.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where interest rates go, and what that does to the distribution and to values.
- Whether book values are right, which is what the discount to net asset value is about.
- Whether rents have stopped falling, or rising.
- Whether purchases add to income per unit or only to size.
- Whether the distribution is covered by cash after capex.

Yardstick: distribution yield and price to net asset value for trusts in Asia and Europe;
price to FFO or adjusted FFO in the US; discount to net asset value for landlords that
are not trusts ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A trust owns 1,000 of lettable area, 95% let at a rent of 1.0: revenue is 950. Property
costs are 250, so net property income is 700. Management costs are 70. Debt of 4,000 at
3% costs 120. Distributable income is 510, or 0.51 on each of 1,000 units.

Next year a fifth of the leases expire and are relet 10% lower, so rent falls 2% and
revenue by 19. A quarter of the debt is refinanced at 5%, adding 20 of interest.
Distributable income falls to 471, down 8%, with occupancy unchanged.

At a cap rate of 5% the buildings are worth 14,000 and net assets are 10,000. At 5.5%
they are worth 12,727. Net assets fall 13% and gearing rises from 29% to 31%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
