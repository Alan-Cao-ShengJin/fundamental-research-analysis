# Property developers and homebuilders

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A developer buys land, builds homes on it and sells them. Some also build offices and
shops to sell or to keep.

How the sale is booked differs by market, and it shapes the whole model.

- **Sold before completion.** In much of Asia the buyer signs and pays during
  construction. The developer books revenue only when the home is handed over, usually
  one to three years later. In a few markets revenue is booked in stages as building
  proceeds.
- **Sold at or near completion.** A US homebuilder starts many homes before it has a
  buyer and sells them during building or after. It books revenue when the sale closes,
  often in the quarter the order was taken.

Home buyers decide demand. They follow incomes, mortgage rates and how easy a mortgage is
to get, and what they expect prices to do. Governments shape it through rules on who may
buy, on deposits and on prices, and in some countries by selling the land. Lenders to the
developer decide whether it can keep building.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Contracted sales**, signed in the period, against **revenue**, booked at handover.
- **Total** sales of the projects the company takes part in, against the share
  **attributable** to it. Many projects are joint ventures.
- The **margin on revenue booked** now, set when the land was bought and the homes sold,
  against the **margin on homes being sold** now.
- **Core profit**, before changes in the value of investment properties, currency and
  one-off items, against reported profit. Each company defines it.
- **Cash**, of which part is buyers' money held in supervised accounts until the building
  reaches set stages.

Signs the card does not fit: rent earns most of the profit (see the
[landlords card](reits-landlords.md)); the company builds for other owners for a fee; it
prepares land for a government and is paid by it; or it manages buildings (see the
[property management card](property-management.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each market separately, by city or region, with history beside the forecast. Build
rental property the company keeps on the landlords card. Homes built to rent and then
sold stay here.

There are two routes to sales. A developer that reports projects and land bank is built
from what it has to sell and the share that sells. A homebuilder that reports selling
communities is built from the number of communities and orders per community. Its
closings come from the homes it starts, and not only from its backlog of orders, which
can cover a small part of a year's closings.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Many Asian developers publish contracted sales and area every month. Twice
  a year they give sold-but-unbooked sales, the land bank by city, their attributable
  share, margin, debt and its cost. US homebuilders report quarterly: orders, the
  cancellation rate, backlog, closings, communities, incentives, and lots owned and
  optioned.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly sales by area and value, starts, completions and investment, and prices in 70 cities (National Bureau of Statistics). Monthly sales of the largest developers (CRIC, China Index Academy). Land auction results. Mortgage rates |
| Hong Kong and Singapore | Transactions registered and official price indices. Monthly sales by developers. Government land sale results |
| United States | Monthly new home sales, starts and permits (Census Bureau). Builders' confidence (NAHB). Weekly mortgage rates (Freddie Mac) and mortgage applications (MBA). Stock of existing homes for sale (NAR) |
| Everywhere | Second-hand home prices and volumes, which lead new home prices |

- **What leads revenue.** Where homes are sold before completion, contracted sales lead
  revenue by the time it takes to build. For a homebuilder, starts lead closings.
  Land purchases lead launches. Mortgage rates and policy changes lead sales. The stock
  of unsold homes leads prices.
- **Backtest.** Opening unbooked sales plus contracted sales less revenue against the
  closing balance. Where homes are sold before completion, the price of homes sold two
  years ago against revenue per unit of area booked now. For a homebuilder, opening
  homes in stock plus starts less closings against the closing stock.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Land is the largest cost and its price is fixed when it is bought. The margin is
  decided by what homes sell for against what the land cost.
- The margin reported now belongs to homes sold some time ago. When prices fall, the
  reported margin keeps falling for years after sales stabilise.
- Interest on land and construction loans is added to the cost of the homes. It reaches
  the income statement through cost of sales, so the finance cost line understates it.
- A homebuilder's incentives cut the price without showing in list prices.
- Where land gains are taxed, the tax takes a larger share as the margin rises.
- Profit from joint ventures sits below revenue, so revenue understates activity.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The balance sheet is the business. Land and homes under construction are funded by
  buyers' deposits, debt and partners' money.
- Buyers' money may be held in supervised accounts and released by stage of
  construction.
- Many developers guarantee buyers' mortgages until the title passes.
- Debt can sit in joint ventures, outside the balance sheet. Perpetual securities are
  counted as equity but are paid like debt.
- A homebuilder that holds land under option needs far less capital than one that owns
  it.
- In a squeeze, who can still borrow decides who survives.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Sales, starts and completions follow each other with lags. Policy on mortgages, on who
  may buy and on developers' borrowing can turn the market in either direction.
- In a downturn sales fall, less cash comes in, land and stock are written down, and debt
  falls due at the same time.
- Traps:
  - contracted sales read as revenue;
  - total sales read as the company's share;
  - the margin being booked read as the margin being earned now;
  - cash that is not free to use;
  - debt in joint ventures, and perpetual securities;
  - profit lifted by revaluation gains or currency;
  - a land bank valued at prices buyers no longer pay;
  - handovers bunched at the year end, which makes the half-years unequal;
  - orders before cancellations.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- When sales and prices stop falling, or how long a recovery lasts.
- Whether the company can refinance its debt.
- Whether land bought now earns an adequate margin.
- How much of the land bank is worth less than it cost.
- Whether rental income deserves to be valued apart from development.

Yardstick: discount to net asset value, with P/B and P/E on booked sales beside it; P/B
and the balance sheet when solvency is in question; P/B against return on equity, and
P/E, for US homebuilders
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A developer that sells before completion starts the year with 150 sold but not yet
booked. It signs sales of 100 and hands over homes sold two years ago for 90. Revenue is
90, and the unbooked balance ends at 160.

On the homes handed over, land was 36, construction 27 and interest added to cost 4.5.
Gross profit is 22.5, a margin of 25%.

Homes sold this year, on the same costs, went for 10% less. For each 81 of price the cost
is still 67.5, a margin of 17%. That is the margin the company will report in two years.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
