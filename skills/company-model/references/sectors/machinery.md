# Machinery

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- **Machines** for construction, mining, ports and materials handling, sold to
  contractors, mines, port operators and rental companies. Standard machines are sold in
  volume, through dealers or direct. The largest mining and port machines are built to
  order.
- **Where it sells.** Some makers sell from one home base and export. Others build in
  several regions, and for them the region of sale replaces home and export.
- **Parts and service** on the machines already working. For some makers this is most of
  revenue, and it is steadier than machine sales.
- **Financing and rental** of the maker's own machines, by its own arm or with partners.

The buyer's spending plans decide demand: building starts and public works for
construction machines, mine spending for mining machines, trade volumes for port
machines. Behind that sits the age of the fleet, because machines bought in one boom wear
out together. Credit matters too. Many machines are sold on instalments or leases, so
looser terms can lift sales now at a cost later.

Three pairs of numbers get confused. **Orders** against **deliveries**, which is when
revenue is booked. **Sales to dealers** against **dealers' sales to users**, with dealer
stock between them. **Units** against **value**, since small machines dominate the count
and large ones the revenue.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is a contractor that builds projects (see the
[construction and engineering card](construction-engineering.md)); it rents
machines it buys from others; it makes production lines for one industry, where orders
follow that industry's capacity plans; or a finance arm earns more than about 15% of
segment profit, in which case that arm gets its own build.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each product line and market separately, with history beside the forecast.

There are two routes. For standard machines with published industry sales, build from
industry sales and share. For machines built to order, build from orders and backlog. Use
the other route as a check where both exist.

Some makers publish market demand in units but their own sales only in money. Then build
sales by region as last period's sales moved by three things, each on its own row: volume,
from market demand and any change in share; price; and currency. A book-to-bill ratio
given without a backlog shows direction and is a check, not a route.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry sales | Units by product and size class, home and export apart | The series that exists, with its basis stated |
| What drives industry sales | Replacement, from the machines sold in earlier years now wearing out. New demand, from the buyers' spending. Purchases pulled forward by an emission rule or a subsidy | Industry growth, labelled as such |
| Company share | Share by product and size, giving units | Share of the total |
| Exports | Units by region, tested against the industry's exports | One row |
| Dealer stock | Sales to dealers less dealers' sales to users | Leave it out |
| Orders and backlog | Opening backlog, plus orders, less cancellations, less deliveries. Deliveries follow the backlog and the production rate | Leave the route out |
| Revenue per machine | By product and size, with price changes, mix and currency apart | Line revenue divided by units, or growth in line revenue where units are not given |
| Machine revenue | Units times revenue per machine, by line | Revenue by line |
| Parts and service | Machines in service times spend per machine, which follows hours worked. Where the number in service is not given, add up past unit sales over a working life | Its own past growth, moved by hours worked. Do not tie it to this year's machine sales, which would make it as cyclical as they are |
| Currency | Sales at constant exchange rates, then the translation effect | Reported growth only |
| Financing and rental | The book times its yield, less funding cost and credit losses | Segment profit only |
| Credit | Receivables, financed sales and guarantees given, with the provisions against them | Receivables days |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure varies widely. Some makers report orders, backlog and
  book-to-bill each quarter, with the share of revenue from parts and service. Some report
  the change in dealer stock. Others give only revenue and gross margin by product line
  and by home against overseas, twice a year, with no units.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly sales of excavators and other machines from the construction machinery association (CCMA), home and export apart. Monthly hours worked by machines. Building starts, public-works funding and mine investment from national statistics. Customs exports |
| North America and Europe | Housing starts and construction spending. Rental companies' spending plans. Makers' own statements of dealer stock and backlog |
| Mining | Miners' spending plans in their own reports, and commodity prices |
| Global | Large makers' own published estimates of market demand by product and region, and their monthly figures for hours worked by their machines |

- **What leads revenue.** Hours worked by machines. Building starts and public-works
  funding. Miners' budgets and commodity prices. Dealer stock. Orders. Dates of emission
  rules and renewal subsidies.
- **Backtest.** Industry sales times share against the company's units. Units times
  revenue per machine against line revenue. Opening backlog and orders against deliveries.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost: steel, hydraulics and engines. Staff and depreciation
  are largely fixed, so the margin moves with volume.
- Margin differs by line and by market. Large machines, exports and parts usually earn
  more than small machines sold at home. Carry a margin for each line.
- In a downturn, makers compete on discounts and on credit terms. Easier terms do not
  show in the price, so read revenue per machine with receivables.
- Losses on financed sales arrive a year or more after the sale.
- Export revenue per machine in the reporting currency moves with the exchange rate. For a
  maker selling worldwide, currency can explain more of a year's change in sales than
  volume does.
- Tariffs on machines and parts shipped between the maker's own plants and markets are a
  direct cost.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Where a maker finances its own sales, credit is the main balance-sheet risk. Check
  receivables against revenue, sales financed by the maker or by partners, and guarantees
  to buy machines back if the customer defaults. These
  guarantees sit outside the balance sheet until they are called.
- Operating cash flow falling behind profit is the first sign that sales are being bought
  with credit.
- Stock includes finished machines and machines taken back from customers.
- Factory capex is modest, and goes to new models and plants abroad.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The replacement cycle is long. A boom in sales is followed, years later, by a boom in
  replacement, and by a slump in between.
- An emission rule, a renewal subsidy or a round of public-works spending pulls sales
  forward and leaves a gap.
- Mining machines follow commodity prices with a lag of a year or more.
- Exports can be concentrated in a few markets and face tariffs and anti-dumping duties.
- Traps:
  - industry sales that include exports read as home demand;
  - units read as value;
  - orders read as revenue, and orders that can still be cancelled;
  - sales growth with receivables growing faster;
  - guarantees and partner financing outside the balance sheet;
  - dealer stock rising;
  - sales to a leasing company in the same group;
  - the group's figures against the listed company's.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where the market is in the replacement cycle.
- Whether exports last.
- Whether credit terms are hiding losses.
- How long a mining upcycle runs.
- Whether parts, service and larger machines lift the margin for good.

Yardstick: P/E on earnings through the cycle, P/B near a trough
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

The industry sold 100 machines eight years ago and 80 of them are replaced this year. New
demand adds 20, so the industry sells 100. At a 25% share the company sells 25 at home at
4 each: 100. It exports 15 at 5 each: 75. Its 300 machines in service each need 0.1 of
parts and service: 30. Revenue is 205.

Of the 100 sold at home, 60 is financed over three years. The company collects 40 now and
20 of the financed part, so cash in is 60 and receivables rise by 40. Home sales earn a
10% margin, which is 10. If one in ten financed customers defaults, the loss is 6, more
than half the profit on those sales.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
