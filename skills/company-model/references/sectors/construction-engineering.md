# Construction and engineering

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A contractor builds for a client under a contract: housing, roads, railways, factories,
power and water plants. Some only build. Some design, buy the equipment and build, and
hand over a working plant. Some do repeat work for utilities and telecom companies under
master agreements, paid for each unit of work done. Revenue is booked as the work
progresses.

Many contractors have other units, which need their own builds:

- **Design and survey**, paid in fees, at a much higher margin.
- **Projects the contractor invests in**, such as toll roads and water plants it builds
  and then runs. See the [toll roads, ports and airports card](transport-infrastructure.md).
- **Property development.** See the [developers card](property-developers.md).
- **Equipment** it makes for its own industry.

The client's spending decides demand: government budgets for public works and how they
are funded, developers' building starts, companies' plans for factories and data
centres, and utilities' networks. Work abroad follows the host country's finances.

Numbers that get confused:

- **New contracts** signed, **backlog**, and **revenue**.
- Contracts that are **firm and funded** against framework agreements and projects the
  contractor must help finance.
- Work **done**, work **billed** and cash **collected**. Work done and not yet billed
  sits in contract assets, and work billed and not yet paid sits in receivables.
- **Profit** against **cash from operations**.
- A **fixed-price** contract, where the contractor bears any overrun, against one that
  pays **cost plus a fee** or an agreed price for each unit of work.
- Backlog that is a **signed order** against backlog that is an **estimate** of work
  expected under a master agreement.

Signs the card does not fit: most profit comes from running concessions; the company
develops property; it makes building materials; or it sells design and consulting hours
with no construction, where staff numbers and billing rates are the build.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Large Chinese contractors publish new contracts every quarter, some every
  month, by segment and by home and abroad, and the backlog twice a year. Western
  contractors report backlog and margin by segment each quarter, and some report order
  intake. All report receivables, contract assets and cash from operations.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Public works | Investment in fixed assets by type, monthly. Government bond issuance for projects. Budgets and funding programmes |
| Building | Property starts and completions. Construction spending. Surveys of construction orders |
| Private projects | Announced factories, data centres and power projects |
| Costs | Prices of steel, cement and asphalt. Construction wages |

- **What leads revenue.** New contracts lead revenue by one to two years on large
  projects, and by months on repeat work. Government
  funding leads the pace of work and of payment. Material prices lead the margin on
  fixed-price work.
- **Backtest.** Opening backlog times the share converted, against revenue. Receivables
  and contract assets against revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Margins are thin in general building. Materials, subcontractors and labour are nearly
  all of revenue, so a small overrun on a fixed-price job removes its profit. Specialist
  work done with the contractor's own crews earns more.
- Design work, managing a whole project, and work abroad earn more than plain building.
- Size does little for the margin. Winning work by cutting price lowers it.
- Losses are recognised late: a contract looks fine until the cost to finish is
  re-estimated.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A general contractor needs little plant. What it ties up is working capital and money
  invested in projects. A specialist with its own crews and equipment spends more on
  capex, and the number of skilled crews limits how fast it can grow.
- When clients pay late, receivables and contract assets grow faster than revenue and
  debt rises with them. Subcontractors are then paid late in turn.
- A project the contractor finances ties up capital for decades, often in a company
  that is not consolidated.
- Performance bonds and guarantees are liabilities that do not show until called.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Public works are used to support the economy, so orders can rise when other sectors
  fall. Whether the work is paid for depends on government finances.
- Housing construction follows the property cycle.
- Traps:
  - new contracts that include frameworks and unfunded projects;
  - revenue booked on work the client cannot pay for;
  - profit year after year with little cash from operations;
  - contract assets growing faster than revenue;
  - bad-debt charges put off;
  - margin that rests on claims the client has not agreed;
  - perpetual securities counted as equity;
  - debt in projects kept off the balance sheet;
  - gains on selling assets inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether clients will pay what they owe.
- Whether new work is growing, and at what margin.
- How much profit turns into cash.
- How much is paid out.
- Whether new kinds of work, such as data centres and power networks, change the mix.

Yardstick: P/E; P/B and dividend yield for state-owned contractors; EV/EBITDA is also
used for Western contractors; the backlog beside them ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A contractor starts with a backlog of 300, wins 120 and books revenue of 100. The backlog
ends at 320, a little over three years of work.

Gross margin is 10%, so gross profit is 10. Overheads are 5 and profit is 5. A third of
the work is at a fixed price. A 3% overrun on it costs 1, a fifth of profit.

Of the 100 of revenue, clients pay 85 in the year. Costs are 90, of which 80 is paid, and
overheads of 5 are paid in full. Cash from operations is nil against a profit of 5.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
