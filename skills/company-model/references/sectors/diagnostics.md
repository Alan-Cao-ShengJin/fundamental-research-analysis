# Diagnostics

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Diagnostics companies make the analysers that laboratories use to test blood, tissue and
other samples, and the reagents each test uses up. Customers are hospital laboratories,
independent laboratories, clinics and blood banks.

An analyser usually runs only its maker's reagents. So each instrument brings years of
reagent sales, and the reagents earn far more than the instrument. Makers sell
instruments outright, lease them, or place them free against a promise to buy reagents.
They also sell service contracts.

Demand is the number of tests done, which grows with ageing, check-ups and new kinds of
test. Insurers and governments set what a laboratory is paid for a test. In China,
provinces buy reagents by tender at lower prices, cut the fees for tests, and limit
which tests may be ordered together.

Numbers that get confused:

- Instruments **placed** against instruments **in active use**, and the base at the
  **start**, the **end** and on **average** over the period. Companies may count
  instruments installed at home and instruments sold abroad, which are different
  measures.
- Revenue from **instruments**, from **reagents** and from **service**.
- Instruments **sold** against instruments leased or placed free, which stay on the
  maker's balance sheet.
- The price the **laboratory pays** against the price the **maker receives** from its
  distributor.
- **Sales to** distributors against what they **sell on**.
- Growth at **constant currency** against reported growth.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes scanners, surgical equipment or implants
(see the [medical devices card](medical-devices.md)); it runs laboratories (see the
[hospitals card](hospitals-healthcare-services.md)); or it sells instruments to research
laboratories and drug makers, whose spending follows research funding and not patients.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Sales by kind of product, by field of testing and by region. Some give
  instruments installed in the year and the size of the base. Few give tests or prices.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Testing | Hospital visits and admissions. Test volumes from independent laboratories' results |
| Tenders and fees | The documents and results of each regional reagent tender, and notices cutting test fees |
| Peers | The large global makers' growth by region, and what they say about China |
| Approvals | Regulators' approval databases |
| Trade | Customs exports of instruments and reagents |

- **What leads revenue.** Instruments placed lead reagent sales by a few quarters.
  Hospital visits. The timetable of tenders. Peers' comments. Distributors' stock.
- **Backtest.** Average instruments in use times tests times price against reagent
  revenue. Reagent revenue for each instrument, year by year.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Reagents earn a high margin and instruments much less, or nothing where they are
  placed free. The mix between them decides the gross margin.
- A cut in reagent prices falls almost wholly on profit unless volume rises.
- A direct sales and service network is a fixed cost. Selling through distributors
  costs less and earns a lower price.
- Building a network abroad costs money for years before it pays.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Plants are modest. Capital goes into instruments at customers, stock and receivables.
- An instrument stays in a laboratory for five to ten years, so the base changes
  slowly.
- A product approval takes years.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The number of tests grows steadily where the rules do not change. Prices, and rules
  on which tests may be ordered, are what change a year.
- An epidemic can multiply sales of one test for a year or two and then leave.
- Traps:
  - instruments placed read as instruments in use;
  - a full year's tests credited to instruments placed during the year;
  - sales to distributors at the year end read as demand;
  - a tender's price cut treated as a one-off, when the next family of tests follows;
  - an epidemic year left in the base;
  - fast growth abroad from a small base;
  - growth that is only the exchange rate.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How far price cuts and limits on testing go in China.
- How far local makers replace imported instruments.
- How fast Chinese makers grow abroad.
- What new, faster instruments add.

Yardstick: P/E, read with growth; EV/EBITDA beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker has 1,000 instruments in use, each running 20,000 tests a year at a reagent
price of 5: reagent revenue is 100m. It adds 150 instruments evenly through the next
year, tests per instrument rise 2% and the price falls 8%. On an average base of 1,075,
revenue is 100.9m, up 1%. Only in the year after, with all 1,150 in use from the start
and the same tests and price, does it reach 107.9m.

Reagents earn a gross margin of 70%. A tender cuts the maker's price by 30% on a family
of tests that is a fifth of reagent sales. Revenue falls 6%, and gross profit falls from
70m to 64m, down 9%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
