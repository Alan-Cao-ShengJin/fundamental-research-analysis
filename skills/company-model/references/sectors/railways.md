# Railways

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A railway owns track and trains and charges for what it moves.

- **Freight**, paid by the wagon load or by weight and distance. Bulk goods such as coal,
  grain, ores and chemicals have few other ways to travel. Containers and trailers moved
  together with trucking firms, called intermodal, compete with trucks on price.
- **Passengers**, paid by the journey, on high-speed, long-distance or commuter lines.
- **Use of its track.** A company that owns a line may charge other operators' trains for
  running on it, and for stations and power.

Freight follows what industry produces and the country trades: power plants' coal, the
harvest, factory output, imports through the ports. Passenger traffic follows incomes and
travel, and competing flights and roads.

Who sets the price differs. North American freight railways set most of their own prices
and add a surcharge that follows the price of fuel. In China, India and many other
countries the government sets or caps freight rates and fares.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Wagon loads** against **weight times distance**. A container and a coal wagon count
  the same in the first and very differently in the second.
- **Revenue per wagon**, which moves with price, with the mix of goods and distances, and
  with the fuel surcharge. Only the first is pricing.
- The **operating ratio**: operating costs over revenue. Lower is better.
- **Passengers** against **passengers times distance**.
- Revenue from the company's **own trains** against fees from **other operators'
  trains** on its line.

Signs the card does not fit: the asset is a concession that returns to the government
(see the [toll roads, ports and airports card](transport-infrastructure.md)); most of the
profit comes from property around the stations, which is built on the
[landlords card](reits-landlords.md) and the [developers card](property-developers.md);
or the company arranges rail transport and owns no track.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** North American freight railways publish wagon loads by group of goods
  every week. Each quarter they give revenue, volume and revenue per wagon by group, the
  operating ratio, fuel price and use, staff numbers and measures of service such as
  train speed. Some Chinese railways publish monthly tonnage, and report in full twice a
  year. Japanese railways publish monthly passenger revenue.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Rail volumes | Weekly wagon loads by group of goods (Association of American Railroads). Monthly freight and passenger volumes from transport ministries and state railway groups |
| Goods moved | Coal output and power plants' stocks. Grain exports. Industrial output. Imports through ports |
| Competing transport | Trucking rates (DAT, Cass). Airline seats on competing routes |
| Fuel | Diesel prices (EIA in the US) |
| Rules | Regulators' decisions on rates, mergers and service. Government notices on freight rates and fares |

- **What leads revenue.** Weekly wagon loads give most of a quarter before it is
  reported. Diesel prices lead the surcharge by a month or two. Trucking rates lead
  intermodal pricing. Coal stocks and gas prices lead coal volume.
- **Backtest.** Volume times revenue per wagon against freight revenue. Fuel used times
  the diesel price against fuel cost.

## 4. Costs and margin

- For a railway that runs its own trains, staff are the largest cost, then fuel,
  bought-in services and depreciation. A line that contracts out its operations pays
  fees in their place.
- Fuel moves with volume at once. Crews and train starts can be cut over a few quarters.
  Track and overheads are fixed. So costs are less fixed than a road's, and the margin
  moves with volume by less.
- Price rises above cost inflation, year after year, are the main source of a freight
  railway's margin gains. Longer trains and fewer locomotives are the other.
- When fuel falls, the surcharge falls a month or two later, which lifts the margin for a
  quarter, and the reverse when fuel rises.
- A passenger line's costs barely change with the number of passengers, so the share of
  seats filled decides its profit.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A railway that owns its track must keep renewing it. On an old network this capex is
  a large share of revenue every year and cannot be put off for long. A new line needs
  little for its first decades.
- Capacity is added a little at a time, with passing loops and second tracks.
- Debt is borrowed by the company as a whole. Cash left after capex goes to dividends and
  buybacks.
- Where the state builds new lines, the listed company may buy them from its parent, at a
  price that matters more than a year's operations.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Freight follows the industrial cycle. Coal has its own path, set by gas prices and
  energy policy. Tariffs on trade move intermodal.
- Bad weather and congestion cut volume and raise cost in the same quarter.
- Regulators limit prices for customers with no other transport, review mergers for
  years, and in state systems set the rates and how revenue is shared between lines.
- Traps:
  - revenue per wagon that rose with the fuel surcharge or the mix, read as pricing;
  - volume growth in low-revenue intermodal read like growth in bulk goods;
  - an operating ratio helped by gains on land sales or by upkeep put off;
  - a one-off pay settlement;
  - a change in how a state network shares revenue, read as growth;
  - a line bought from the parent at a high price.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How volume grows against the economy and against trucks.
- Whether prices can keep rising faster than costs.
- How low the operating ratio can go.
- Whether a merger will be approved, and what it is worth.
- In state systems, whether rates and fares will be raised and what assets the parent
  sells to the company.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it; dividend yield for railways with regulated
rates ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A railway moves 1,000 wagon loads at 3,000 each: revenue is 3.0m. Next year volume grows
2%. Prices rise 3%, a shift towards containers takes 1% off revenue per wagon, and a
lower fuel surcharge takes off 2%. Revenue per wagon is flat and revenue grows 2%, though
pricing was up 3%.

Costs are 1.8m, an operating ratio of 60%, and profit is 1.2m. If revenue grows 2% and
costs 1%, profit rises 3.5% and the ratio falls to 59.4%.

A high-speed line runs 100 of its own trains a day with 1,000 seats, 80% filled, at a
fare of 300: 8,760m a year. Other operators run 300 trains a day over it and pay 20,000
each for the distance they cover: 2,190m. A 5% rise in fares adds 438m, nearly all of it
profit.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
