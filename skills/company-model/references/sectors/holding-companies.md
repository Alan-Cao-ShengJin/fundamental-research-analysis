# Holding companies and conglomerates

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A holding company owns stakes in several businesses that have little to do with each
other: listed subsidiaries and associates, unlisted businesses, property and financial
investments. It sells nothing itself. Its income is the dividends its businesses pay up
to it, and its costs are its head office and the interest on its own debt.

An **operating conglomerate** is different. It runs and consolidates its businesses,
borrows centrally and moves cash between them. Build it from its divisions, on the
second table in section 2.

Investors buy it for the value of what it holds, usually at a discount to that value.
So the work is in two parts. Forecast each material business on its own card. Then value
the whole as the sum of its parts.

Four things move the share price:

- the value of the holdings;
- the discount investors apply, which widens and narrows with their trust in the
  controlling owner and with markets;
- what the company does with its money: buying back shares, selling or listing a
  business, or making a large new investment;
- the debt at the parent.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Consolidated** profit, the part **attributable** to shareholders after partners'
  shares, and the company's share of each business's earnings.
- **Net asset value** at book against net asset value with holdings at **market**
  prices.
- The **gross** value of the holdings against the value **after** the parent's debt.
- Debt and cash of the **parent alone** against the **consolidated** figures. Cash
  inside a subsidiary is not the parent's to spend, unless the group pools it.
- **Recurring** profit against gains on sales and revaluations.
- **Dividends received** by the parent against dividends it **pays**.

Signs the card does not fit: one business is most of the value, in which case use that
business's card and treat the rest as small; the company manages other people's money
for fees (see the [asset and wealth managers card](asset-wealth-managers.md)); or it is a
listed fund that publishes its net asset value.

## 2. The driver tree

List every holding, with the share owned and how it is accounted for. Then build these
rows, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Each business | Its own forecast, on the card for its kind. For a listed stake that is not modelled, consensus, and say so | Its reported profit, held flat |
| Attributable profit | Each business's profit times the share owned, less head-office costs, the parent's interest and tax | As reported, with one-off items taken out |
| One-off items | Gains on sales, revaluations and write-downs, on their own row | Zero in the forecast, except gains on sales already agreed |
| Listed holdings | Shares held times the market price | |
| Unlisted holdings | A multiple of earnings from listed peers, the price of a recent deal, or an independent valuation. Say which, and how old it is | Book value, labelled as such |
| Property and investments | The latest valuation, and funds at their reported value | Book value |
| Parent's net debt | The parent's own borrowing less its own cash, and guarantees it has given | The parent-only balance sheet in the notes |
| Net asset value | Holdings at the values above, less the parent's net debt and other liabilities, per share | |
| Discount | One less the share price over net asset value, against its own history | |
| Parent's cash flow | Dividends received and proceeds of sales, less head-office costs, interest, dividends paid, new investments and buybacks | As far as the parent-only accounts allow |
| Parent's borrowing | Net debt over the gross value of the holdings, and when the debt falls due | As reported |
| Share count | Buybacks, which add to value per share when made at a discount | Hold flat |

An operating conglomerate is built from its divisions and the group's debt. Use these
rows in place of the parent rows above.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Each division | Revenue and operating profit before depreciation, on the card for its kind. Note whether the figures include the group's share of associates, and which lease basis they use | As reported, held flat |
| Division value | A multiple of profit from listed peers or a recent deal, for each division. A listed subsidiary at its market price, with its own debt and cash left out of the group's | Book value, labelled as such |
| Group net debt | Consolidated debt less cash, by the year it falls due, without the net debt of any subsidiary already valued at its market price | As reported |
| Partners | Partners' share of each division, taken out at the level where it arises | The minorities line |
| Attributable profit | Division profits, less depreciation, interest, tax and partners' share | As reported, with one-off items taken out |
| Currency | Profit by currency | The split the company gives |
| Sales agreed | A division being sold goes in at the agreed price once the deal is firm, with the gain on its own row | As announced |
| Net asset value and discount | Division values, less group net debt and partners' shares, per share, against the share price | |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** The annual report lists principal subsidiaries and associates with the
  share owned, gives profit by segment, and shows the parent's own balance sheet in the
  notes. Some publish their own net asset value. Announcements cover sales, purchases
  and buybacks.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Listed holdings | Their share prices, daily, and their own results and forecasts |
| Unlisted holdings | Deals in similar businesses, and listed peers' multiples |
| Each business | The data named on its own card |
| Peers | The discounts at which other holding companies trade |
| Credit | The parent's bond yields, which show how lenders see it |

- **What leads value.** The prices of the listed holdings. Announced sales, listings and
  buybacks. A change in a stake. The parent's borrowing costs.
- **Backtest.** The sum of each business's attributable profit, less parent costs,
  against reported attributable profit. Dividends received against dividends paid.

## 4. Costs and margin

- The parent's own costs are head office, interest and, in some structures, tax on
  dividends received or on gains when a holding is sold.
- Where an outside manager runs the company, its fee comes out before shareholders see
  anything.
- These costs are small against the holdings and large against the dividends that pay
  for them.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The parent can pay interest and dividends only from cash that reaches it. A business
  that earns well and pays nothing up does not help.
- The parent's lenders rank behind each subsidiary's own lenders.
- A controlling owner may have pledged its shares for loans. A falling price can then
  force sales.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The value is the sum of several cycles. The discount widens when markets fall and when
  the owner does something investors dislike.
- A government can end a concession or block a sale.
- Traps:
  - holdings carried at book value when the market says otherwise;
  - unlisted holdings at management's own valuation;
  - a holding counted twice through a cross-shareholding;
  - consolidated cash treated as the parent's, where the subsidiaries do not pool it;
  - a listed subsidiary's debt or cash counted again in the group's;
  - divisions valued on profit that includes associates, with the associates then added
    again;
  - partners' shares left out;
  - gains on sales read as recurring profit;
  - a discount assumed to close with nothing to close it;
  - perpetual securities counted as equity;
  - deals with the controlling owner on terms that favour it.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Why the discount is what it is, and what would narrow it.
- How well the company has used its money in the past.
- What the unlisted holdings are worth.
- How much debt the parent carries.
- Whether the controlling owner treats other shareholders fairly.

Yardstick: the sum of the parts less a holding discount, judged against the company's
own past discount; dividend yield beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A holding company owns half of a listed business worth 1,000, which is 500. It owns all
of an unlisted business earning 30, valued at ten times, which is 300. It owns property
worth 200. The parent's net debt is 250. Net asset value is 750, or 7.5 a share on 100
shares. At a share price of 4.5 the discount is 40%.

If the listed business rises 20%, net asset value rises to 850, up 13%.

The parent receives dividends of 40. Head office costs 8 and interest 12. That leaves 20,
against a dividend to its own shareholders of 18.

It buys back 10 shares at 4.5, borrowing 45. Net asset value is 705 on 90 shares, 7.83 a
share, up 4%.

An operating conglomerate has two divisions earning 100 and 60 before depreciation,
valued at 8 and 6 times: 800 and 360. A partner owns 20% of the second, which is 72.
Group net debt is 400. Net asset value is 688.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
