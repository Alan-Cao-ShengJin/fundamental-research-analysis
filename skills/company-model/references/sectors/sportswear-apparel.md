# Sportswear and apparel brands

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- A **brand owner** designs and markets shoes, clothing and accessories, and buys most of
  them from contract manufacturers.
- It reaches the consumer by three routes: **wholesale**, either to multi-brand retailers
  or to partners who run stores under the brand; its **own stores** and outlets; and
  **online**, meaning its own e-commerce, including its own shops on marketplaces. A sale
  to another retailer is wholesale even when that retailer sells online.
- A group can own several brands at different stages. Each brand is its own build.
- The end consumer decides demand: how wanted the brand is, whether it has hit products,
  and price. Weak spending pushes buyers to cheaper brands and deeper discounts.

The same shoe is counted twice, and the two counts differ. **Retail sales**, or
sell-through, are what consumers buy across every channel, at retail value. They show the
health of the brand. **Revenue**, or sell-in, is what the company books: the retail price
in its own stores and online, and the wholesale price on goods shipped to partners. The
gap between the two comes from the channel mix, the wholesale discount and the change in
partners' stock.

Signs the card does not fit: the company manufactures for other brands; it retails other
people's brands (see the [retail card](retail.md)); it earns mainly royalties from
licensees; it is a luxury house (see the [luxury goods card](luxury-goods.md)); it is a
fashion chain that sells only through its own stores, which is a retailer; or listed
stakes and joint ventures earn more than about 15% of profit, in which case they get
their own build.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each brand separately, with history beside the forecast.

There are two routes. Where the company publishes retail sales, or sells through stores
that carry only its brand, build retail sales first and convert them to revenue. Where it
sells mainly to multi-brand retailers and publishes no retail sales, build revenue
directly: by market, then by channel and category, on growth at constant exchange rates.
Own stores are then stores times revenue per store, and wholesale comes from the order
book and from the retailers' own sales and stock. In that case skip the rows down to
wholesale revenue.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retail sales | Offline (stores times sales per store) plus online | The company's retail sales growth applied to a base, labelled as such |
| Stores | By type (own, partner, outlet, kids), with openings and closures and the average for the period. Floor area where store size is changing | Period-end counts, averaged |
| Sales per store | Same-store growth, split into traffic and spend per visit where disclosed, plus the effect of new and closed stores | Revenue per average store, labelled as such |
| Online | Own site and each platform, with growth and share of sales | One row |
| Discount | Selling price as a share of the tag price, offline and online | Management's description of discount depth |
| Channel stock | Partners' stock in months of sales, and its change in the period | Leave it out and let sell-in equal sell-through |
| Wholesale revenue | Partners' sell-through, times the wholesale price as a share of retail, plus the change in partners' stock | Wholesale revenue growth |
| Direct revenue | Own stores and online at the realised price, net of sales tax and returns | |
| Revenue | By brand, then by market and category (shoes against clothing, sport against lifestyle) where the company reports them | |
| Currency | Growth at constant exchange rates, then the translation effect to reported revenue | Reported growth only |

Where the brand sells mostly wholesale and reports an order book, add it as a row. Orders
lead revenue by one or two seasons.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure varies widely. Chinese brands publish quarterly updates on
  retail sales growth by brand, in ranges and at retail value, with comments on discounts
  and channel stock. They give revenue by brand and channel and store counts twice a
  year. Western brands report quarterly revenue by region, by channel (wholesale against
  direct) and by category, with inventories and the reasons gross margin moved. Few of
  them give retail sales or sales per store.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | National retail sales of clothing. Online sales by brand from third-party trackers of the big platforms. Peers' quarterly updates. Results of listed retailers that sell the brands |
| North America and Europe | Results and inventory of the retailers that sell the brands. Card-spending and footfall trackers. Government retail sales |
| Supply chain | Monthly revenue of the listed contract manufacturers (Feng Tay and Yue Yuen are two), which leads the brands' sell-in. Each has its own main customers, so use the one that supplies the brand, and its manufacturing revenue only |

- **What leads revenue.** Contract manufacturers' monthly revenue. The brand's wholesale
  order book. Retailers' stock and order comments. Shopping-festival and holiday
  results. Weather. Online trackers.
- **Backtest.** Retail sales growth against revenue growth for each brand, with the gap
  explained by channel mix, stock and discount. Stores times sales per store against
  offline revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Product is bought from contract manufacturers, often in US dollars, so the exchange
  rate, freight and import duties move its cost.
- Gross margin moves with the share sold direct, discount depth, the mix of shoes against
  clothing and of new against old stock, and write-downs of old stock.
- Selling direct raises gross margin and operating costs together. Compare channels and
  years on operating margin.
- Own stores carry fixed rent and staff, so their profit moves more than their sales.
- Advertising, endorsements and sponsorships are committed years ahead and rise in big
  event years.
- Online sales carry platform commissions, promotion fees and returns.
- Government grants and tax rebates can be part of the margin. Show it with and without
  them.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- There is little factory capex. Spending goes on store fit-out, logistics and systems,
  and leases are the main fixed commitment.
- Inventory is the main risk: its days, its age and the provisions against it. Add the
  partners' stock, which the company may have to take back or clear with rebates.
- Receivables from partners lengthen when the channel is weak.
- Brands generate cash and often spend it on other brands and stakes. Keep those apart
  from the operating business.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The stock cycle: partners over-order, discounts deepen, partners then buy less than they
  sell, and later restock. Revenue overstates the move in both directions.
- The brand cycle: a brand can be in demand for years and then fade. Brands in one group
  sit at different points.
- Sales bunch around festivals and the holiday quarter, and move with the weather.
- A big tournament lifts a year's sales and its marketing cost. A tariff on the sourcing
  country raises product cost and can pull shipments forward. A boycott or a shift in
  national sentiment can change a year.
- Traps:
  - retail sales growth read as revenue growth;
  - taking over partners' stores, which lifts revenue and gross margin with no more
    demand from consumers;
  - store counts without type or size;
  - same-store sales on a pool that changes;
  - online sales before returns;
  - partners' network sales counted as company revenue;
  - margins that include subsidies;
  - inventory provisions released into profit;
  - currency-neutral growth against reported growth;
  - a 53-week year.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether weakness is the brand or the whole category.
- Whether the channel is clean: discount depth and partners' stock.
- Whether newer brands can make up for a mature core brand.
- Whether the margin holds without subsidies and with more direct selling.
- Whether growth abroad is real.

Yardstick: P/E; EV/EBIT for the global brands; a sum of the parts where listed stakes are
large ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A brand has 1,000 stores selling 5 each, so 5,000 offline, plus 2,000 online: retail
sales of 7,000. Partners run stores that sell 3,000 of the offline total. The company
sells to them at 45% of the retail price, so their sell-through is worth 1,350 to it.
This year they also cut their stock by 150, so wholesale revenue is 1,200. Its own stores
and online book 2,000 each. Revenue is 5,200.

Next year consumers buy exactly the same, but partners rebuild the 150 of stock.
Wholesale revenue is 1,500 and revenue is 5,500, up 6%, with retail sales flat.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
