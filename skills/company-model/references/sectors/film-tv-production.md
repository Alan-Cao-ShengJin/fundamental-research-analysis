# Film and television production

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Producers and distributors invest in films and series and earn from each one over
several stages.

- **Films.** The box office is shared. After the ticketing fee, any levy and tax,
  cinemas keep roughly half. The distributor takes a fee, and the producers share the
  rest by their stakes. A film earns most of its box office within weeks. Then come
  fees from streaming services, television and sales abroad.
- **Characters.** An owner of lasting characters, often from animation, also earns from
  licences, goods and sequels, for years.
- **Series** are sold to streaming services and broadcasters for a price per episode.
  Most are sold before they are made, so the platform sets the price and not the
  audience. Revenue is booked when the series is delivered and cleared to air, which
  bunches it into a few quarters, and payment comes long after.
- **Ticketing platforms** take a fee on each ticket and are paid to promote and
  distribute films.

A company's part in a film decides what it books. The lead producer and distributor may
book the producers' whole side as revenue and pay partners their shares as a cost. An
investor books only its own share. A distributor books a fee.

Each title is its own bet. Audiences decide, and regulators decide what is released and
when.

Numbers that get confused:

- A film's **box office** against the **producers' share** of it, and against the
  **company's stake**.
- Revenue booked **with** partners' shares against revenue booked **without** them.
- Films **counted in the period** against films released in it.
- **Unreleased titles**, held at cost on the balance sheet, against what they will earn.
- Income from **this year's releases** against income from older titles and characters.

Signs the card does not fit: the company runs cinemas (see the
[cinemas card](cinemas.md), and build that unit there); it streams to subscribers (see
the [streaming card](streaming.md)); it makes games (see the [games card](games.md)); or
it is a broadcaster that lives on advertising.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Reports name the films counted in the period, their combined box office,
  and the films waiting for release. When a film passes a threshold, the company
  announces the range of revenue it has earned from it. Stakes and costs by title are
  seldom given.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Box office | Daily box office by title (Maoyan and Dengta in China, Comscore and Box Office Mojo elsewhere) |
| Films due | The release calendar, the regulator's lists of films registered and approved, and advance sales |
| Audience | Scores on rating sites in the first days |
| Series | The broadcast regulator's lists of series registered and licensed. Rankings of series by viewing. The platforms' spending on content |

- **What leads revenue.** Release dates being set. Advance sales and the first weekend.
  Audience scores. Approvals. Money received from platforms in advance.
- **Backtest.** The company's film revenue against its titles' box office, year by year.
  Title cost charged against the fall in unreleased titles.

## 4. Costs and margin

- A title's cost is fixed before release, so everything above it is profit and
  everything below it is loss.
- A film earns in weeks. For most films the later fees are small beside the box office.
  For an owner of lasting characters they can be large and last for years.
- Animation takes years to make, costs less in stars, and can be reused in sequels and
  goods.
- A series sold in advance earns a margin agreed before it is made. The risks are a
  cost overrun, a delay in clearance and the platform's credit.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Money is tied up in titles for years before they are released.
- A hit leaves the company with more cash than it can put to work quickly.
- Platforms and broadcasters pay late.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Results follow the release calendar, and one title can decide a year.
- Regulators can delay or refuse a release.
- Traps:
  - a hit year read as the normal one;
  - a film's full box office credited to a company with a small stake;
  - revenue booked with partners' shares set beside revenue booked without;
  - unreleased titles carried at cost for years;
  - a release date that slips into the next year;
  - a series counted in the year it airs when it was booked in another;
  - a sequel assumed to match the original;
  - associates' losses and grants inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How strong the coming titles are, and when they are released.
- Whether a hit can be repeated and turned into a lasting series.
- What streaming services will pay.
- How much the company's characters earn outside cinemas.

Yardstick: P/E on the average earnings of several years, with cash and unreleased titles
beside it ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A film takes 2,000 at the box office. After the ticketing fee, levy and tax, 1,800 is
shared. Cinemas keep half, 900. The distributor takes 10% of the rest, 90, and the
producers share 810. The company owns 30%, which is 243, against 30% of a cost of 500,
which is 150. Its profit on the film is 93.

If the company also leads and distributes the film, it books the whole 900 as revenue
and its partners' 567 as a cost. It keeps 333: its 243 and the distributor's 90. Its
revenue is nearly four times what its stake alone would show.

A film that takes 600 on the same terms returns 73 to the company against its cost of
150: a loss of 77.

A producer delivers a series of 40 episodes at 5 an episode: 200 of revenue in the
quarter it is cleared to air. The series cost 150, so it earns 50. If clearance slips
past the year end, the year shows none of it and the 150 stays on the balance sheet.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
