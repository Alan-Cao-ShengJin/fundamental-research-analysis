# Electronics contract manufacturing

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Contract manufacturers build products that other companies design and brand: phones,
computers, servers and network gear, and, at the diversified makers, equipment for cars,
healthcare, energy and industry. Some only assemble. Others design the product as well
and sell it to the brand.

Their revenue includes the components they buy on the customer's behalf and pass on at
cost, so the margin on revenue is thin. The same work gives very different revenue
depending on whether the maker buys the main components or the customer supplies them.

There are two kinds of maker, and they are built differently.

- **Makers tied to a few brands and products.** Demand is the devices those customers
  build, times the share of each product they give this maker, times the price per unit.
  The brand decides all three, splits each product between two or three makers, and
  moves production between countries. For servers, demand follows the cloud companies'
  spending and the supply of processors.
- **Diversified makers** with many customers in many industries. They report by segment
  and not by customer. Build each segment from the growth of its end markets and the
  programmes it has won and lost.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Revenue** that includes passed-through components against the **value the company
  adds**.
- Components the maker **buys and resells** against components the customer **supplies
  free**.
- **Gross margin** as a percentage, **gross profit** in money, and **segment operating
  profit**.
- The maker's **share** of a customer's product.
- **Receivables sold** to banks against the receivables left on the balance sheet.
- **Adjusted** profit against reported profit, where restructuring recurs.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns the brand (see the
[consumer electronics brands card](consumer-electronics-brands.md)); it makes parts to
sell to assemblers (see the [electronic components card](electronic-components.md)); it
makes display panels (see the [display panels card](display-panels.md)); or it packages
chips (see the [foundries card](foundries.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Taiwanese makers publish revenue every month, some with units, and
  describe the mix of products each quarter, often as ranges. Diversified makers listed
  in the US give revenue and margin by segment, growth by end market, working capital in
  days, and the largest customers' share of sales. Few name their customers or give
  units.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Devices | Quarterly shipments of phones, computers, tablets and servers by brand, and of laptops and servers by contract maker (IDC, Counterpoint, Canalys, TrendForce) |
| Brands | The brands' own results and launch dates |
| Servers | The cloud companies' capital spending, and chip designers' guidance on the supply of processors and accelerators |
| Other end markets | Vehicle production, medical device makers' growth, industrial capex |
| Trade | Customs exports of phones, computers and servers by country, which show where production is moving |

- **What leads revenue.** Monthly revenue of the Taiwanese supply chain. The brands'
  launch calendar. The cloud companies' spending. Reports of changes to a brand's
  orders, which are often wrong and should be labelled as reports.
- **Backtest.** Devices times share times price against revenue by customer. Segment
  growth against the growth of its end markets. Gross profit in money against value
  added.

## 4. Costs and margin

- An assembler earns a small percentage on a large revenue. What matters is gross profit
  in money and the return on the capital tied up.
- More or less pass-through changes revenue and the percentage margin without changing
  gross profit. It does change the stock the maker carries and the interest it pays.
- The customer takes a price cut every year, so cost has to fall as fast.
- New products and new plants lose money at the start.
- A maker that designs the product earns more than one that only assembles it.
- Products for regulated industries, such as medical devices and cars, run for longer
  and earn more than consumer devices.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Lines are built for one product. If the product fails or the order moves, the line is
  idle.
- Assemblers of servers hold very expensive components in stock, so fast growth uses
  cash unless the customer pays deposits or supplies the parts.
- Brands pay slowly and suppliers are paid slowly in turn. Receivables sold to banks are
  borrowing that does not show as debt.
- Moving production to another country costs money for years before it saves any.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows how often people replace devices, the waves of spending on data
  centres, and the stock held in the chain.
- Tariffs and customers' wish to spread risk move work between countries.
- Traps:
  - revenue growth from passed-through components read as profit growth;
  - a fall in percentage margin that only reflects more pass-through;
  - one customer behind most of the sales;
  - a share of one product assumed for the next;
  - stock built ahead of a launch that sells less than planned;
  - receivables sold, left out of debt;
  - restructuring charges left out of profit every year;
  - grants from local governments inside profit;
  - gains on the exchange rate.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How much profit assemblers keep on AI servers, and for how long.
- Who gains and loses share at the main customer.
- What moving production costs.
- Whether new uses, such as cars and robots, become real businesses.
- What the capital employed earns.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

An assembler builds 1,000 devices at 500 each: revenue is 500,000. Components it buys and
passes on are 450,000, so it adds 50,000 of value. Gross profit is 15,000, which is 3% of
revenue and 30% of the value added.

The customer starts supplying the main chip itself, worth 200 a device. Revenue falls to
300,000. Gross profit is still 15,000 and the margin is now 5%.

A diversified maker has three segments with revenue of 500, 350 and 150 and operating
margins of 5%, 6% and 4%: profit is 25, 21 and 6, which is 52. The first segment grows
20% and the third shrinks 10%. Revenue is 1,085, up 8.5%, and profit is 30, 21 and 5.4,
which is 56.4. If capital grows in step with revenue, from 300 to 326, the return before
tax stays at 17%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
