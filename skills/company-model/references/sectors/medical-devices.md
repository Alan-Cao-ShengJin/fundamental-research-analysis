# Medical devices

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies sell products used to treat patients. There are three kinds, and they
earn differently.

- **Equipment**: scanners, monitors, ultrasound, endoscopes, surgical robots. Hospitals
  buy it out of their capital budgets, often by tender, and pay for service afterwards.
- **Consumables and implants** used in each procedure: stents, joints, lenses,
  catheters, staplers, wound dressings. Sales follow the number of procedures.
- **Products patients keep using**, such as glucose sensors, sold to a growing base of
  users.

Procedures grow with ageing and with access to treatment. Equipment follows hospital
budgets, which governments fund and can freeze or boost. In China hospitals need a
licence to buy some of the largest machines. Insurers and governments set
what is paid, and a change in their rules can cut demand for a product.

In China the state buys many implants and consumables by tender, as it does drugs, at
much lower prices. Hospitals in China are also turning from imported products to local
ones, and local makers are selling more abroad. Elsewhere, hospitals and their buying
groups take a small price cut most years.

Numbers that get confused:

- Revenue from **equipment** against revenue from **service and consumables**, which
  recurs.
- The price the **hospital pays** against the price the **maker receives** from its
  distributor. A tender cuts the first by much more than the second.
- **Procedures** done against **units** the maker shipped. Distributors' stock sits in
  between.
- A tender **won** against equipment delivered, installed and booked.
- An ordinary **tender** to buy equipment against a state tender held to **cut prices**.
- Growth from the company's **own business** against acquisitions and currency.
- **Adjusted** profit against reported profit, where restructuring recurs.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes laboratory analysers and reagents (see
the [diagnostics card](diagnostics.md)); it makes drugs; it runs hospitals (see the
[hospitals card](hospitals-healthcare-services.md)); it distributes devices made by
others (see the [drug distribution card](drug-distribution.md)); or it makes low-value
items such as gloves, whose price follows industry capacity.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each product line and each region separately, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equipment sales | By product line: units times average price. Units are hospitals' purchases, from tender records and budgets, times the company's share, with replacement of old machines apart from new sites. The price falls where the state buys in bulk. Installation follows the order by a quarter or two, and longer for the largest machines | Revenue by line as reported, moved with the market's tender value and the company's share of it |
| Orders in hand | Orders won and not yet installed, and customers' advances | As reported |

| Consumables and implants | Procedures, times the company's share, times units per procedure, times the price it receives. The change in distributors' stock | Growth by product line and region as the company reports it, split into market, share and price from registries and peers' comments |
| Price | Outside tenders, the yearly change hospitals and buying groups take | Leave it inside revenue growth |
| Price-cutting tenders | Which consumables and implants face a state tender, the likely cut in the hospital price and in the maker's price, and the share of volume won | A named risk on each product not yet tendered |
| Payment rules | Changes in what insurers pay for a product or a procedure | A named risk |
| Users | For products patients keep using: opening users, plus new ones, less those who stop, times yearly spend | User and revenue growth as reported |
| Regions | Sales by region, through distributors or the company's own staff, with product approvals in each market | Growth by region |
| New products | Approvals expected, by market and date, and the time to build sales | Only products already filed |
| Gross margin | The mix of products, price cuts, scale, freight and tariffs | Its recent level, moved with the mix |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by product line and by region. Western makers give growth by
  division each quarter, without acquisitions and currency, and seldom give units,
  procedures or prices. Some Chinese makers give tender wins and approvals.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Hospital purchases | Public tender records for medical equipment, added up monthly by product and brand by data vendors and quoted by brokers. Government funding for hospital equipment |
| Tenders for consumables | The documents and results of each national and regional tender |
| Procedures | National hospital visit and admission figures. Hospital groups' and device makers' comments on procedure volumes. National joint registries, which count hip and knee operations by brand each year |
| Approvals | Regulators' approval databases, and recall notices |
| Trade | Customs exports by product, and tariffs |
| Payment rules | Insurance payment rates and rules on paying per case |

- **What leads revenue.** Tender records lead equipment revenue by one or two quarters.
  Hospital visits and peers' comments on procedures. The timetable of tenders for
  consumables. Approvals. Changes in payment rules.
- **Backtest.** Tenders won against equipment revenue a quarter or two later.
  Procedures times share times price against revenue for the main implants.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gross margins are high on implants and consumables and lower on equipment. Selling and
  research are the large costs.
- A tender cuts the hospital's price deeply. Much of the cut comes out of the
  distributor's margin, so the maker's price falls by less. Whether volume makes up the
  rest depends on the share won.
- Consumables and service earn more than the equipment that uses them.
- Building a sales and service network abroad costs money for years before it pays.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Plants are modest. Capital goes into instrument sets and robots at hospitals, stock
  and receivables.
- An implant maker holds every size of every product near the operating theatre, so
  stock is large.
- Large Western makers grow by buying smaller ones, and carry goodwill.
- A product approval takes years and a recall can undo it.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Procedures grow steadily over the years and can soften for a few quarters. Equipment
  buying moves with hospital budgets and with policy, and can stop for a year.
- Tenders reset prices one product family at a time.
- Trade rules affect both imports of parts and sales abroad.
- Traps:
  - tender wins read as revenue already earned;
  - one-off payments to distributors when a tender cuts the value of their stock;
  - a year of government-funded equipment buying read as normal;
  - fast growth abroad from a small base;
  - one product behind most of the profit;
  - a margin target taken as delivered;
  - goodwill from acquisitions.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether hospital buying has recovered for good.
- How many products have still to go through tenders.
- How far local makers replace imports, and how far they can sell abroad.
- Whether the company is gaining or losing share in its main procedures.
- What new products add, and whether margins improve as promised.

Yardstick: P/E, read with growth; EV/EBITDA beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

An implant sells to hospitals at 100. The maker receives 40 and has 20% of 1,000
procedures: 200 units and revenue of 8,000. After a tender the hospital price is 30, the
maker receives 22, and its share is 35%: 350 units and revenue of 7,700. At a cost of 8 a
unit, gross profit falls from 6,400 to 4,900.

A maker outside the tender market has 30% of 1,000 procedures at a price of 50: revenue
is 15,000. Procedures grow 4%, its share slips to 29% and the price falls 1%. Revenue is
1,040 times 29% times 49.5, which is 14,929, down 0.5%.

Hospitals buy 500 scanners and an equipment maker wins a fifth: 100 units at 10 each,
which is 1,000. The next year hospitals buy 600, its share is 22% and bulk buying cuts
the price 5%. It sells 132 units at 9.5, which is 1,254, up 25%. It has 1,000 machines
in service, 700 of them past warranty and 60% of those under contract at 0.5 a year:
service revenue is 210.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
