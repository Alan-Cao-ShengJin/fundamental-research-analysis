# Property agents and services firms

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These firms earn fees from property they do not own. There are three kinds of fee, and
most firms have more than one.

- **Commissions on deals.** The firm arranges the sale of a home or a building, or a
  lease, and is paid a share of the deal's value when it closes. Some firms employ the
  agents. Some run a platform that other agencies use, or license their brand to them,
  and take a fee or royalty from each of their deals.
- **Outsourcing contracts.** The firm runs buildings and workplaces for companies, or
  manages their building projects, under contracts that last several years. Much of the
  revenue is cost passed through to the client at no margin.
- **Other fees**: valuations, arranging and servicing property loans, the title and
  escrow work that closes a home sale, and for housing platforms, renovation and rental
  services.

Commissions follow the number and value of deals in the property market, which follow
interest rates, credit and confidence. Sales of buildings swing the most. Leasing is
steadier, because firms need space in good years and bad. Outsourcing grows as companies
hand their buildings to outside firms, and depends on contracts being renewed.

Numbers that get confused:

- The **value of deals** arranged, against the **commission**, against what is left
  after paying the agents.
- A deal counted **once**, or **once for each side** the firm acted for. Count the
  market the same way before working out a share.
- Revenue that includes **costs passed through**, against **fee revenue**. Some firms
  report both. Others give the passed-through costs, and fee revenue has to be worked
  out.
- On a platform, deals done by the firm's **own stores**, where it books the whole
  commission, against deals done by **connected agencies**, where it books only its fee.
  Revenue can fall while the value of deals rises.
- The firm's **own profit measure**, which leaves out costs it chooses to.

Signs the card does not fit: the company manages estates for a fee per unit of floor
area (see the [property management card](property-management.md)); it owns the buildings
(see the [landlords card](reits-landlords.md)); or it runs a listings website and sells
advertising to agents (see the
[e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)). A services firm
may also manage property funds or develop buildings. Build those units on the
[asset and wealth managers card](asset-wealth-managers.md) and the
[developers card](property-developers.md).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Global firms report quarterly: revenue by line and region, passed-through
  costs, and growth in leasing and sales commissions. Few give the value of deals or the
  agents' share. Housing platforms report the value of deals for existing and new homes,
  by own stores and connected agencies, the number of stores and agents, and a margin by
  line after the agents' share.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Home sales | Monthly sales of existing and new homes, and contracts signed but not yet closed (NAR and the Census Bureau in the US, land registries elsewhere). In China, new home sales (National Bureau of Statistics) and second-hand deals in large cities from local housing authorities |
| Sales of buildings | Quarterly value of commercial buildings sold, by region (MSCI) |
| Leasing | Quarterly space leased by city, from the firms' own research departments |
| Credit | Mortgage rates and applications. Lending on commercial property (MBA in the US) |

- **What leads revenue.** Mortgage rates. Contracts signed, which lead closings by a
  month or two. Homes listed for sale. The firms' own comments on deals in progress.
  Outsourcing contracts won.
- **Backtest.** Market deal value times share times the commission rate against
  commission revenue. Opening outsourcing fee revenue plus wins less losses against the
  year's fee revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Agents take a large part of each commission, and the best take more. The split
  differs widely: agents paid only by commission keep most of it, and salaried agents
  much less. What the firm keeps then has to cover stores, salaried staff and
  technology, which are fixed. So profit on deals moves much more than deal value.
- Outsourcing earns a thin, steady margin. Measure it on fee revenue. Where the client
  pays cost plus a fee, the firm bears little cost risk. Where the price is fixed, it
  bears all of it.
- On a platform, a deal through its own stores brings more revenue and a lower margin
  than a deal through a connected agency.
- New services such as renovation carry the cost of the work, so they add revenue faster
  than profit.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little fixed capital, but large firms borrow to buy others, and
  many carry net debt.
- In outsourcing the firm pays suppliers and staff and then bills the client, so
  receivables are large against fee revenue.
- Commissions on new homes are owed by developers, who may pay late or not at all.
- A development or investment arm uses capital and earns in lumps.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Deals are cyclical. In a weak market the value of building sales can fall by half or
  more, and it recovers with interest rates.
- Rules and lawsuits on commission rates can lower them across a market.
- Home sales are seasonal. The spring and summer quarters earn most of a year's profit,
  so one quarter says little about the year.
- Traps:
  - revenue with passed-through costs read as growth or compared with fee revenue;
  - a shift from own stores to connected agencies read as lost business;
  - a peak year of deals read as normal;
  - the number of agents read as capacity, when sales per agent fall in a weak market;
  - the firm's own profit measure, which leaves out share-based pay and the cost of
    deals;
  - commissions on new homes booked and not collected;
  - gains on developments read as recurring;
  - growth before and after currency and acquisitions.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where deal volumes are in the cycle, and when they recover.
- Whether the steady businesses are now large enough to change how the firm is valued.
- Whether commission rates hold.
- For platforms, whether share gains can outrun a shrinking market, and whether the new
  services will earn a profit.
- What is done with the cash: buybacks or acquisitions.

Yardstick: P/E on the company's adjusted earnings, read with what the adjustments leave
out; EV/EBITDA beside it, and first where earnings are thin
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

An agent arranges sales worth 1,000 at a commission of 2%, which is 20. It pays 12 to its
agents and keeps 8. Fixed costs are 6, so profit is 2. If deal value falls 20%, it keeps
6.4 and profit falls to 0.4.

A platform's own stores arrange 400 at 2.5%: revenue 10, of which it keeps 4. Connected
agencies arrange 600 and pay it 0.25%: revenue 1.5, all kept. Next year its own stores
arrange 300 and the agencies 800. Deal value is up 10%. Revenue falls from 11.5 to 9.5,
down 17%, and what it keeps falls from 5.5 to 5.

An outsourcing business has fee revenue of 100 and passes through costs of 150. It earns
12, a margin of 4.8% on reported revenue and 12% on fee revenue.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
