# Tyres

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A tyre maker sells tyres for cars, for trucks and buses, and for special uses: mines,
farms, aircraft and two-wheelers. There are two markets.

- **Replacement.** Tyres to replace worn ones, sold through distributors and dealers.
  For most makers this is the larger market and the more profitable.
- **Original equipment.** Tyres sold to vehicle makers for new vehicles, at lower
  prices, partly to win the replacement sale later.

Replacement demand follows the number of vehicles in use and how far they are driven, so
it is steady. Truck tyres follow freight. Original equipment follows vehicle production,
and mining tyres follow mine output. Dealers' stock moves between the two: makers can
ship more than drivers buy for a few quarters, and then less.

The maker sets its price. It moves with raw materials, with competition from imports and
with tariffs. Much of the price a maker earns is mix: larger rims and premium brands
sell for far more.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Sell-in**, from maker to dealer, against **sell-out**, from dealer to driver.
- Volume in **units** against volume in **tonnes**.
- **Price** against **mix**. Companies often report the two together.
- The duty a tyre pays, which depends on the **country of the plant**, the destination,
  and sometimes the company itself.
- A market figure that covers **all makers** against one that covers only an
  association's members.

Signs the card does not fit: the company grows or trades rubber; it retails tyres made
by others; or it makes other vehicle parts (see the [auto parts card](auto-parts.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Western makers explain each quarter's sales as volume, price and mix, and
  currency, and give results by segment. Some publish the market's sell-in by region
  every month. Chinese makers publish output, sales volume, and the change in average
  price and in raw material cost each quarter, and capacity by kind of tyre or by
  plant. Most give one blended figure for all tyres, so the split by kind and by
  destination has to be assumed, and labelled as an assumption.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Shipments | Tyre shipments by market from the industry associations (USTMA in the US, ETRMA in Europe, the China Rubber Industry Association) |
| Trade | Customs exports of tyres from China, Thailand and Vietnam, in volume and value. Anti-dumping and countervailing duty rulings, by country and by company. General tariffs by destination |
| Raw materials | Prices of natural rubber on the Shanghai and Singapore exchanges, and of synthetic rubber and carbon black |
| Demand | Vehicles in use, distance driven, freight activity and vehicle production |
| Freight | Container freight rates |

- **What leads profit.** Raw material prices lead the margin by one or two quarters.
  Duty rulings and their yearly reviews. Container freight. Dealers' stock, where a maker or
  association reports it. Announced price rises.
- **Backtest.** The reported changes in volume, price and mix, and currency against
  revenue growth. Lagged raw material prices against cost per tyre.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Raw materials are the largest cost and move with rubber and oil. When they fall,
  margins widen until prices follow, and the reverse when they rise.
- A premium maker holds its price better and carries higher fixed costs. A budget maker
  lives on low cost and on where its plants are.
- For an exporter, freight and duties can be as large as its margin.
- Plants are fixed costs, so the share of capacity in use matters.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A plant takes one to two years to build and longer to fill.
- Chinese makers build abroad to stay inside other countries' trade walls. A plant's
  value depends on the duty its country faces, which can change.
- Western makers are closing plants in high-cost countries, at a one-off cost.
- Stock at dealers and at the maker rises before price increases and duty changes.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Replacement volume is steady. What swings is the margin, with raw materials and trade
  policy.
- A new duty can close a market to one country's plants within months and open it to
  another's.
- Traps:
  - sell-in read as demand while dealers build stock;
  - mix read as price;
  - exports rushed out before a duty takes effect;
  - the margin at a low point in raw materials read as normal;
  - a duty rate that is reviewed every year;
  - a new plant counted at full output in its first year;
  - a spike in freight rates.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where raw material prices go.
- Whether premium makers can hold their prices.
- How much share low-cost makers take abroad, and what duties do to them.
- What plants abroad earn.
- Whether larger rims and tyres for electric cars keep lifting the mix.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker sells 100 tyres at 50: revenue is 5,000. Raw materials are 22 a tyre and other
costs 20, so it earns 8 a tyre and 800 in all.

Volume falls 1%. Price rises 0.5% and mix adds 1.5%. Revenue grows 1%. Raw materials rise
10%, by 2.2 a tyre, from mid-year: 1.1 over the year. Profit per tyre is 8 plus 1 from
price and mix, less 1.1, which is 7.9.

An exporter sells a tyre to an importer for 40. The duty on its plant's country rises
from 5% to 20%, which costs the importer 6 more a tyre. The maker gives up part of that
in price or loses the order, unless it can ship from a plant in another country.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
