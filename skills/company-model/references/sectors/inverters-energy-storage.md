# Solar inverters and energy storage systems

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies make the power electronics around solar farms and batteries, and
assemble battery storage systems.

- **Inverters** turn a panel's output into grid power. Large ones go to developers and
  builders of solar farms, by tender or contract. Small ones for homes and businesses go
  through distributors and installers. They are sold in units of power.
- **Storage systems** are containers of battery cells with the power conversion, cooling
  and controls around them. They go to developers of grid storage and of solar farms,
  and are sold in units of energy. Most makers buy the cells. Small systems for homes
  are sold with an inverter through distributors.
- Some makers also **build solar farms to sell**, mainly at home.

Developers, utilities and households decide demand. Grid storage follows the grid's need
for flexible power, the gap between cheap and dear hours, capacity payments and
government mandates. Household systems follow power prices, interest rates, outages and
subsidies, and how the buyer pays: in cash, by loan or by lease.

The price differs widely by market. In China, the largest market, sales are won on
price. Other markets pay more for a record of reliable systems, local service and
meeting grid rules. So the mix of regions decides the margin. Cells are the largest cost
of a storage system, and system prices follow cell prices with a lag.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Power** for inverters against **energy** for storage. They cannot be added.
- **Shipments** against **revenue**. A large system is booked when it is delivered,
  accepted or switched on, which can be quarters after it ships. The two can move in
  opposite directions in a period.
- The price and margin **in the maker's own country** against **abroad**.
- **Sales to distributors** against **installations** by their customers, for household
  products.
- **Contracts signed** against revenue.
- Equipment revenue against revenue from **solar farms built and sold**.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes the battery cells (see the
[batteries card](batteries.md)); it makes solar panels (see the [solar card](solar.md));
it owns storage plants or solar farms and sells power (see the
[power generation card](power-generation.md)); or inverters are a small part of a
general maker of electrical equipment.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese makers give revenue and margin by segment and for home and abroad
  twice a year, shipments once a year, and only totals each quarter. Makers listed in
  the US give shipments and guidance each quarter, with contracts in hand for grid
  storage and comments on distributors' stock for household products.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Installations | Solar farms: monthly new capacity in China (National Energy Administration), and quarterly or yearly figures elsewhere (SEIA, SolarPower Europe). Home solar and batteries in the US: the industry association's quarterly report, the energy agency's monthly small-scale figures, and California's monthly data. Storage additions by country: China's energy administration and its storage industry alliance, the US energy agency's monthly battery capacity, and trackers such as Wood Mackenzie and BloombergNEF |
| Shares | Yearly rankings of inverter makers by shipments and of storage system makers (Wood Mackenzie, S&P Global) |
| Prices | Weekly prices of storage cells (SMM, InfoLink). Winning prices in China's storage tenders. A yearly survey of full system prices (BloombergNEF) |
| Trade | China's customs exports of inverters by country, monthly, in value and units |
| Rules | Tariffs and rules on equipment from named countries. The export tax rebate. Storage mandates and capacity payments. Home subsidy schemes |

- **What leads revenue.** Tender results and contracts announced. Customs exports of
  inverters by country. Cell prices, which lead storage cost. Stock and customers'
  advances on the balance sheet. The dates when rules change.
- **Backtest.** Shipments times revenue per unit against segment revenue, year by year.
  The lagged cell price against the storage margin.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Cells are the largest cost of a storage system. When cell prices fall, the maker's
  margin widens until system prices follow. When they rise, contracts at fixed prices
  are squeezed.
- For a Chinese maker the same product earns far more abroad than in China. A change
  in the mix of regions moves the margin more than a change in cost.
- Inverter cost falls with scale and with each new design.
- Service networks and warranties abroad are a cost that grows with the installed base.
- Research is a fixed cost.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Assembly needs modest capex. The capital is in stock and receivables.
- A large project is built ahead, shipped by sea and booked at acceptance, so stock
  rises before revenue does.
- Plants abroad exist to meet local rules, and cost more to run.
- Distributors of household products build stock in a boom and stop buying for quarters
  after it.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand in each country follows policy and project returns, with a rush before each
  deadline.
- Trade rules can close a market to makers from one country.
- Traps:
  - shipments read as revenue;
  - the margin abroad applied to sales in China, or assumed to last as more makers go
    abroad;
  - a margin that rests on tax credits;
  - sales pulled forward by a tax-credit deadline, read as the new level;
  - a margin widened by falling cell prices, read as normal;
  - sales to distributors read as demand;
  - gains on solar farms sold, read as recurring;
  - power and energy added together;
  - a currency gain inside profit;
  - a fire or recall treated as one-off when the warranty runs for years.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How long margins abroad hold as rivals follow.
- How fast demand for storage grows, and how much of it comes from data centres.
- What trade rules do to sales in the US.
- Who bears a rise in cell prices.
- When household demand recovers in each market, and what happens when tax credits end.

Yardstick: P/E while the business is growing; P/B in a downturn
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker ships 10 units of energy: 4 at home at a price of 60 and 6 abroad at 100.
Revenue is 840. Cells cost 35 a unit and the rest of the system 15, so cost is 500 and
gross profit 340. Home earns 40 of it and abroad 300.

Cell prices rise 20%, by 7 a unit. On contracts already priced, cost rises 70 and gross
profit falls to 270, down 21%.

If 2 of the 6 units shipped abroad are accepted only next year, this year's revenue is
640 and not 840, though shipments were 10.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
