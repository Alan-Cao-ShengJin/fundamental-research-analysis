# Shipbuilding

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A shipyard builds ships to order for shipowners: bulk carriers, tankers, container
ships, gas carriers, car carriers and offshore units. Many yards also build warships,
make marine engines, and repair or convert ships.

The contract fixes the price, usually in dollars, two to four years before delivery. The
owner pays in stages, often with the largest part at delivery. Most yards book revenue
as the ship is built. So this year's profit comes from prices agreed two or more years
ago, set against this year's cost of steel, equipment and labour.

Shipowners decide demand. They order when freight rates are high and money is easy, when
old ships must be replaced, and when new rules on fuel and emissions make old ships
uneconomic. Ordering comes in waves many years apart. Between waves, yards fight for the
few orders there are.

Numbers that get confused:

- **New orders**, the **order book**, **deliveries** and **revenue**.
- Units: the **number** of ships, their **carrying capacity** in tonnes, **compensated
  gross tonnes**, which adjust for how hard a ship is to build, and **value**.
- The price of ships **ordered today** against the average price of ships **being
  built**.
- The **currency of the contract** against the currency of the yard's costs.
- Orders that are **in force**, with a deposit paid, against letters of intent and
  options.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns and operates ships (see the
[shipping card](shipping.md)); it builds mostly for a navy, where contracts pay cost plus
a fee (see the [aerospace and defence card](aerospace-defence.md)); or it builds offshore
oil platforms as one-off projects.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each kind of ship separately where the yard reports it, with history beside the
forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New orders | The world's ordering by ship type times the yard's share, at the price on the day | Orders the yard has announced, and its target for the year |
| Order book | Opening book, plus orders, less deliveries and cancellations. By ship type and year of delivery, at contract prices | The reported backlog and the years of work it covers |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Yards announce each order: the ship type, the number, often the price and
  the delivery date. Results give the order book by value and by year of delivery, and
  revenue and margin by segment.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Orders and prices | Monthly world ordering, order book, deliveries and an index of new ship prices by type (Clarksons, quoted in the trade press). Shipping bodies' reports (BIMCO) |
| By country | Monthly completions, new orders and backlog from the national shipbuilding associations of China, Korea and Japan |
| Costs | Steel plate prices. Wages. Engine makers' prices and lead times |
| Demand | Freight rates and second-hand ship prices. Dates of new emission rules |
| Currency | The dollar against the yard's currency |

- **What leads profit.** New ship prices and ordering lead revenue and margin by two to
  three years. Steel plate prices reach cost in months. Freight rates lead ordering. The
  exchange rate.
- **Backtest.** The value of ships delivered, plus the change in work in progress,
  against revenue. The lagged price index less the cost index against the margin.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Steel, equipment and labour are nearly all of cost, and the price is fixed. A rise in
  cost after signing comes straight out of the margin.
- The first ship of a new design takes far more hours than the fifth.
- A weak home currency lifts the margin on dollar contracts, unless they were hedged.
- When ordering is slow, yards take work at cost to keep their docks full. Those ships
  hold margins down for years after prices recover.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Stage payments fund the building. A yard with a full order book holds net cash. Where
  most of the price is paid at delivery, the yard borrows to build.
- Owners need a bank guarantee that their payments will be returned if the ship is not
  delivered. A yard that cannot get guarantees cannot take orders.
- Capacity is docks and skilled workers. Docks closed in the last slump are slow to
  reopen, and workers are harder to find.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The cycle is very long. A full order book gives years of visible revenue, at prices
  already fixed.
- When freight rates collapse, owners delay, renegotiate or walk away from orders.
- Governments support their yards with credit and orders, and trade measures can favour
  one country's yards over another's.
- Traps:
  - a boom in orders read as profit now;
  - today's price index applied to ships ordered years ago;
  - orders not yet in force, and options;
  - a margin lifted by cheaper steel or a weaker currency, read as lasting;
  - loss provisions released;
  - capacity counted that has no workers;
  - comparisons across a merger of yards.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How long the wave of ordering lasts.
- Whether new ship prices keep rising.
- How high margins go once the cheap old orders are delivered.
- How much new capacity is added, and where.
- What trade measures against one country's ships do.

Yardstick: P/B, and P/E on a year two or three years out, when the ships ordered now are
delivered ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A yard delivers 10 ships this year that were ordered three years ago at 80 each. They
cost 78 to build. Revenue is 800 and profit 20, a margin of 2.5%.

Last year it took orders at 100 a ship. At today's cost of 82, those earn 18 each. When
they are built, in two years, revenue is 1,000 and profit 180.

Steel is 20 of the cost of each ship. If it rises 20% before those ships are built, cost
rises by 4 a ship and profit falls to 140.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
