# Hotels

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Hotel companies earn from rooms in two ways, and most do both.

- **Owned and leased hotels.** The company sells the rooms and bears all the costs:
  staff, rent, energy and upkeep.
- **Managed and franchised hotels.** The company puts its brand on hotels owned by
  others and takes a fee on each hotel's revenue, and sometimes on its profit. In China
  many franchised hotels also take a manager sent by the brand, and pay for its booking
  system and supplies. Growth comes from hotels joining.

Beside these come loyalty schemes and credit-card partnerships.

Business and leisure travel decide demand, with incomes, events and holidays. Supply
matters as much: new hotels in a city share out the same guests.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Occupancy** times the **average room rate** is **revenue per available room**.
- Revenue per room for **hotels open in both periods** against the figure for **all
  hotels**, which new hotels in cheaper cities pull down.
- The revenue of **all hotels under a brand** against the brand owner's **fee revenue**.
- Hotels **open** against hotels **signed** and not yet open, and **openings** against
  openings net of hotels that leave.
- Fee revenue that includes **costs recharged to owners** at no profit.
- Profit **before rent** on leased hotels against profit after it.
- Revenue per room quoted **with** value-added tax against revenue, which is without
  it.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns hotel buildings and lets them to an
operator (see the [landlords card](reits-landlords.md)); gaming is most of its profit
(see the [casinos card](casinos.md)); or it sells other companies' rooms online (see the
[online travel card](online-travel.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Each quarter: revenue per room, occupancy and rate by brand and region,
  for hotels open in both periods and for all hotels; hotels and rooms open, by kind of
  contract; and hotels signed.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Hotels | Weekly and monthly occupancy and rates by city (STR), mostly paid and quoted by the companies. New hotels due to open. The share of a country's hotels that belong to chains |
| Travel | Trips and spending over each public holiday, from the tourism ministry. Rail and air passenger numbers. Business events |
| Peers | Other chains' revenue per room, openings and signings |

- **What leads revenue.** Holiday figures. The companies' comments on recent weeks. Rail
  and air traffic. The pace of openings and signings.
- **Backtest.** Rooms times revenue per room times days against room revenue. The
  brand's total hotel revenue times the fee rate against fee revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- An owned or leased hotel has high fixed costs, so most of a change in revenue per room
  reaches profit.
- A fee business has few costs. Its profit follows the number of hotels and their
  revenue.
- A move from leased to franchised hotels lowers revenue growth and raises the margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Brands that own little can pay out most of what they earn.
- Leased hotels carry rent for years ahead. Some accounting rules show it as debt and
  interest, and others leave it in hotel costs. Count it once.
- Hotels need refurbishing every few years. That capex cannot be skipped for long.
- Franchisees pay for the brand's growth. They sign when a new hotel pays back quickly
  and when they can borrow.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Travel is among the first things cut in a downturn and among the fastest to return.
- New supply arrives two or three years after a good period, and holds rates down.
- Traps:
  - revenue per room up on rate while occupancy falls;
  - the like-for-like figure quoted while the figure for all hotels falls;
  - a brand growing by signing weak hotels;
  - signed hotels that never open, and closures left out of the count;
  - fee revenue that includes costs recharged to owners;
  - holiday figures where trips rise and spending per trip falls;
  - profit quoted before rent on leased hotels.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether demand holds, and whether new supply outruns it.
- How fast the brand can keep adding hotels, and of what quality.
- How far franchising can go in smaller cities.
- How much is paid out.

Yardstick: EV/EBITDA, with P/E beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

An owned hotel has 100 rooms, 70% full at a rate of 400: room revenue is 10.2m a year.
Costs are 8m and fixed, so profit is 2.2m. If revenue per room rises 5%, profit rises by
0.5m, up 23%.

A franchisor's hotels take 10bn in room revenue and pay it 5%: 500m. Rooms grow 8% and
revenue per room falls 2%, so fees grow 5.8%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
