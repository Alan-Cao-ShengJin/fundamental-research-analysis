# Whisky, cognac and other international spirits

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies make whisky, cognac, tequila, vodka, gin, rum and liqueurs and sell them
under brands in many countries. They ship to importers and distributors in each market,
who sell on to shops, bars and restaurants. Shops in airports are a channel of their
own.

The maker books a sale when it ships. What matters is what distributors sell on, and
what drinkers buy. Distributors build stock in good years and run it down in bad ones,
and a run-down can cut the maker's shipments for a year or more while drinkers buy the
same amount.

Aged spirits are laid down years before they are sold. The stock of ageing liquor is
most of the capital in the business, and part of it is funded by debt. Cognac makers buy
much of their young spirit from growers under contracts that run for years.

Demand follows incomes, eating and drinking out, and gifts and banquets in China. In
India most of what is sold is made locally, and each state sets its own tax and rules.
Trade measures and taxes on alcohol move prices in each market.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Shipments** by the maker, **sales by distributors**, often called depletions, and
  **sales to drinkers**, often called sell-out.
- Growth at **constant currency and scope** against reported growth.
- **Volume** against **price and mix**, which makers give as percentages.
- Sales **before** and **after** taxes on alcohol.
- Operating profit **before** and **after** one-off items.
- A financial year that ends in **March or June** against calendar-year peers.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes baijiu (see the
[baijiu card](baijiu.md)); it makes beer (see the [beer card](beer.md)); or it
distributes spirits it does not make (see the
[trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each division or brand, and each main market, separately, with history beside the
forecast. Makers give volume and price by brand and growth by market, and seldom both
together. Build revenue by region and use the brand figures as a check.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales to drinkers | Purchases by market: the US, China, India, Europe, airports and the rest | Growth by region at constant currency, as reported |
| Sales by distributors | Distributors' sales by market, where they are reported | The direction the company describes on calls |
| Distributors' stock | Shipments less distributors' sales, built up over the years | Assume shipments equal distributors' sales, and say so |
| Volume shipped | Distributors' sales plus or minus the change in their stock, by brand and region | Volume growth as reported |
| Price and mix | Price rises by market. The mix of brands, of ages and grades, and of markets | Price and mix as reported |
| Net sales | Last year's sales moved by volume, price and mix, then by currency and by businesses bought or sold | The growth the company guides. With none, the market's growth by region, less any cut in distributors' stock |
| Currency | Sales by currency against costs in the home currency, with hedges that delay the effect | The sensitivity the company gives |
| Tariffs and duties | Duties by market and date, and how much the maker absorbs in price | As disclosed |
| Cost of goods | The liquor, at what it cost when it was made or bought years ago, glass and packaging | Gross margin from history, moved with the mix |
| Advertising and promotion | A share of sales the company chooses, by brand | The share it guides. With none, its recent share of sales |
| Distribution and overheads | The company's own sales networks and head office, which are fixed | Their own growth |
| Operating profit | Sales less the rows above. It moves faster than sales because the costs are fixed | The margin the company guides. With none, the recent margin moved by mix and by the advertising share |
| Ageing stock | Liquor laid down and bought each year against liquor sold, and what the company has committed to buy | As reported |
| Capex | Distilleries and warehouses | The guided figure. With none, announced projects over their build years, plus other capex at its recent level |
| Below operating profit | One-off charges, interest, tax, partners' share and receivables sold | As reported |
| Debt and payout | Net debt against profit before depreciation, the limit the company has set, dividends and buybacks | The policy the company has stated |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Sales by division or brand and by region each quarter, with growth split
  into volume and price and mix, and comments on distributors' sales and stock. European
  makers give profit twice a year. Most guide growth and margin for the year.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| US | Monthly sales in the states that control liquor sales (NABCA), and shop scanner data, both mostly paid and quoted by brokers. The industry council's yearly figures |
| Exports | Cognac shipments by region and grade (BNIC). Scotch whisky exports (the Scotch Whisky Association). Tequila exports (the tequila regulator) |
| China | Customs imports of brandy and whisky, monthly. Reports of sales over the main festivals |
| India | Yearly volumes by state from the industry body, and each state's tax and licensing changes |
| Travel | Passenger traffic, and airport retailers' results |
| Rules | Tariffs, anti-dumping duties and taxes on alcohol, by market and date |

- **What leads revenue.** The gap between shipments and distributors' sales. Export and
  customs figures. Sales over festivals. The dates of trade measures.
- **Backtest.** Shipments against distributors' sales, added up over several years.
  Volume, price and mix, and currency against reported growth.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gross margin is high. Distribution and overheads are slow to cut, so profit falls
  faster than sales unless costs are taken out.
- For aged spirits the cost of the liquor reflects prices of years ago. Vodka and gin
  are made and sold within months.
- Margin differs by grade and by market, so a change in where the bottles go moves the
  margin as much as a price rise.
- Advertising is the easiest cost to cut. A cut lifts this year's margin and costs
  sales later.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Ageing stock is most of the capital. What can be sold in ten years is decided by what
  is laid down now.
- Purchases and distilling continue when sales fall, so stock and debt rise in a
  downturn.
- The payout is limited by debt as much as by profit.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- A boom leaves distributors overstocked, and the run-down takes years.
- Tariffs and anti-dumping duties can close a market or cut its margin within months.
- Traps:
  - shipments read as demand;
  - growth at constant currency quoted while reported sales fall;
  - a gain from the mix of markets read as pricing power;
  - advertising cut to hold the margin;
  - a dividend held while debt rises;
  - a P/E on depressed earnings read as dear, or on peak earnings read as cheap;
  - a year ending in March set beside a calendar year.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the downturn is distributors running down stock or people drinking less for
  good.
- When the US and China recover.
- What tariffs cost, and who pays.
- Whether drinkers go back to dearer bottles.
- How much debt the ageing stock can carry.

Yardstick: P/E and EV/EBITDA, read with net debt. On depressed earnings, investors look
to profit at a normal margin
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

Drinkers buy 100 cases a year. Distributors hold 30 and decide to hold 20, so they
order 90. The maker's shipments fall 10% while demand is flat. The next year demand
falls 3%, to 97, and distributors hold their stock: shipments are 97, up 8%.

Sales are 1,000 at a gross margin of 70%, which is 700. Advertising is 200, and
distribution and overheads 250. Profit is 250. Sales fall 10%. Gross profit is 630. If
advertising stays at a fifth of sales and the other costs stay at 250, profit is 200,
down 20%. If advertising is held at 200, profit is 180, down 28%.

A maker lays down liquor costing 60 each year and sells liquor that cost 50, so its
stock rises by 10. If sales fall and it sells only 40 while still laying down 60, stock
rises by 20, funded by debt.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
