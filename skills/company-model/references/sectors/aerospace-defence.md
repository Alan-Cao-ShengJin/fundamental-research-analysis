# Aerospace and defence

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

There are three businesses here, and most companies have more than one.

- **Commercial aircraft, engines and their parts.** Airlines and lessors order years
  ahead. Makers deliver against a backlog that can run for a decade, and suppliers'
  sales follow the makers' monthly production rate.
- **Aftermarket.** Spare parts and repairs for aircraft and engines in service. It
  follows hours flown and the age of the fleet. Engines are often sold at little or no
  profit and earn their return over decades of service, much of it under contracts paid
  by the hour flown.
- **Defence.** Aircraft, ships, missiles, electronics and services sold to governments
  under contracts. Some pay the contractor's cost plus a fee, and some fix the price.
  Programmes run for decades and follow defence budgets.

For commercial aircraft, the limit now is how fast the makers and their suppliers can
build, not demand. For defence, the budget and the government's buying plan decide. A
state-owned defence supplier takes its orders from the plan, and its customer reviews
the prices.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Orders**, **backlog** and **deliveries**. Orders over sales in a period is the
  book-to-bill ratio.
- **Firm** orders against options and letters of intent. In US defence, backlog that is
  **funded** against backlog that is not.
- **New equipment** against **aftermarket**, which earns far more.
- Revenue booked **at delivery** against revenue booked **as work is done**.
- **Programme accounting.** The margin on a long programme is an estimate over its whole
  life. When the estimate changes, the catch-up for all earlier years is booked at once.
- The company's own **adjusted** profit and cash flow.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is an airline or a lessor; it builds mainly
merchant ships (see the [shipbuilding card](shipbuilding.md)); or it makes general
machinery that happens to have a defence customer.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each programme or segment separately, with history beside the forecast.

A state defence supplier publishes neither units nor prices, so its revenue is built from
the top. Start from the company's own revenue target for the year, where it gives one.
Test it against the yearly limit it announces for purchases from sister companies, which
moves with planned output, and against the changes in stock, advances and receivables.
Expect uneven years at the start and end of a five-year plan, and when one aircraft type
gives way to the next.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliveries | Aircraft or engines by programme: the monthly production rate times the months, with the planned rate increases and what holds them back | The deliveries the company guides. With none, the production rate last reported, held |
| Price per unit | The contract price with its yearly increase, and the mix of models | Revenue over deliveries |
| Supplier's sales | The value of the company's parts on each aircraft times the maker's production rate, less or plus the maker's change in stock | Growth with the maker's production rate |
| Backlog | Opening backlog, plus orders, less deliveries and cancellations. Years of production covered | As reported |
| Aftermarket | The fleet in service times hours flown times spend per hour. Engine overhauls by the age of each engine type. Service contracts: hours flown times the rate. Yearly price rises on spare parts | Growth in hours flown plus price |
| Defence revenue | By programme: funded backlog and the rate it turns into sales, against the budget line for the programme. For a state supplier: the top-down build above | Growth with the budget for that kind of equipment, or the company's own target |
| Programme margin | The contract price less the estimated cost to finish. Early units cost far more than later ones. Charges when the estimate worsens | Segment margin from history, with charges named |
| Research | Paid by the company, apart from research paid by customers | Its share of revenue |
| Working capital | Advances from customers, stock built ahead of a rate increase, work done but not yet billed, and receivables | Their past ratios to revenue |
| Free cash flow | Profit turned into cash, after working capital and capex | The guided figure. With none, profit plus depreciation, less capex and the change in stock and customer advances |
| Pensions | Contributions to old pension plans, where they are large | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** The large aircraft makers publish orders and deliveries every month.
  Quarterly results give backlog, the book-to-bill ratio, sales split between new
  equipment and aftermarket, and any programme charges. Guidance covers deliveries and
  free cash flow. State-owned defence suppliers disclose less: watch the
  company's revenue target, the changes in advances, stock and receivables, and the
  yearly limits it announces for purchases from and sales to related companies.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Flying | Monthly air traffic and capacity (IATA). Airlines' scheduled flights |
| Fleet | Aircraft in service, on order and parked, by type (Cirium, quoted in the trade press) |
| Defence budgets | Each government's budget and, in the US, the request line by line for each programme. Contract awards, published daily in the US |
| Makers | Production rate plans, and suppliers' comments on them |
| State buyers | The yearly growth in the national defence budget, and the years of each five-year plan |

- **What leads revenue.** Production rate announcements. Orders. Hours flown lead the
  aftermarket, and engine overhauls follow with a lag. A budget request leads the
  contract, and the contract leads sales by one to three years. Advances from a state
  customer.
- **Backtest.** Deliveries times price against new-equipment revenue. Growth in hours
  flown against growth in the aftermarket.

## 4. Costs and margin

- The first aircraft of a new type cost far more to build than later ones. Margin rises
  as workers and suppliers learn, and falls again when the rate is changed.
- A fixed-price contract to develop something new often loses money, because the cost is
  not known when the price is set.
- The mix between new equipment and aftermarket moves the margin more than anything
  else.
- Defence margins are held within a range by the contract type and by the customer's
  right to review costs.
- A shortage at one supplier raises cost across the programme.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Customers pay advances, which fund part of the stock. When orders slow, that funding
  runs down. A state supplier may instead carry large receivables from its customer,
  paid late in the year.
- Raising the production rate needs stock and people before any extra delivery.
- A new aircraft or engine takes a decade and a great deal of money to develop. Some of
  it sits on the balance sheet.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Aircraft orders come in waves. The backlog smooths deliveries, though airlines defer
  when traffic falls.
- The aftermarket falls with flying, as in a pandemic, and recovers with it.
- Defence follows budgets, elections and wars. Export sales need government approval.
- Traps:
  - orders or backlog read as next year's sales;
  - options counted as firm;
  - deliveries bunched in the last quarter;
  - a catch-up from a changed programme estimate read as the running margin;
  - adjusted figures that leave out charges that recur;
  - cash flow lifted by advances;
  - a state customer cutting the price it pays;
  - sales to related parties.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How fast production can rise.
- How long the strong aftermarket lasts while new aircraft are short.
- Where defence budgets go.
- What fixed-price programmes will end up costing.
- How much profit becomes cash.

Yardstick: P/E, with free cash flow yield and the backlog beside it where the company
gives them ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker builds 50 aircraft a month at 50 each: 600 a year and revenue of 30,000. It
raises the rate to 60 from mid-year: 660 aircraft, up 10%.

An engine fleet of 1,000 flies 3,000 hours each and pays 100 an hour: 300m. If the fleet
grows 5%, hours 3% and the rate 4%, service revenue grows 12.5%.

A contract is worth 1,000 and was expected to cost 900. It is 40% done, so 400 of revenue
and 40 of profit have been booked. The cost estimate rises to 950. Profit to date should
be 20, so 20 is taken off this period at once, and the rest of the contract earns 5%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
