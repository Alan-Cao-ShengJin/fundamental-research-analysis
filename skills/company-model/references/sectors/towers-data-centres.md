# Towers and data centres

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies build shared infrastructure and rent it out on long contracts.

- **Towers.** Mobile operators rent space on a tower for their antennas. The rent rises
  each year by a fixed step or with inflation. A second operator on the same tower adds
  rent at almost no cost, and so does extra equipment from an existing tenant.
- **Data centres.** Customers rent space, power and cooling for their servers. Small
  customers rent by the cabinet and pay extra to connect to each other. The largest cloud
  and internet companies rent whole halls, measured by the power supplied, on contracts
  of five to fifteen years.

For towers, demand follows what the mobile operators spend on their networks: a new
network generation, new spectrum, more coverage. For data centres it follows the spending
of a handful of cloud companies, and since the arrival of AI the limit has often been
how much electricity a site can get.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Towers** against **tenancies**. Tenants per tower is the measure of sharing.
- **Growth in rent billed** on the same towers, which is the yearly step plus new
  tenants less tenants who leave, against reported revenue, which also moves with
  currency, costs passed through and accounting for rent over the life of a lease.
- Contracts **signed** against contracts that have **started to pay**. The gap is the
  backlog.
- Capacity **built**, capacity **leased** and capacity **in use and billing**.
- Power for the **servers** against the total a site draws.
- **Adjusted funds from operations (AFFO)**, the cash measure these companies quote,
  which each defines itself.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is a mobile operator (see the
[telecom operators card](telecom-operators.md)); it rents out computing by the hour and
owns the chips, which wear out in a few years; or it makes the cooling and power
equipment.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Tower companies report quarterly: towers, tenancies, growth in rent
  billed split into its parts, and tenants lost. Data-centre operators report contracts
  signed in the quarter, the backlog and when it starts, capacity in service and under
  construction, the share leased before completion, and price changes on renewal.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Customers | Mobile operators' capex plans and spectrum auctions. The large cloud companies' quarterly capex and guidance |
| Property consultants (CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield) | Twice-yearly reports on data-centre markets: capacity, vacancy, space taken up, capacity under construction and price per unit of power |
| Power | Electricity prices. Utilities' statements on how long new connections take |
| Rates | Government bond yields, which these long contracts are valued against |

- **What leads revenue.** Contracts signed and the backlog lead revenue by the time it
  takes to build and move in. Operators' and cloud companies' capex plans lead signings.
  Power connection dates decide deliveries.
- **Backtest.** Average tenancies times rent against tower revenue. Capacity billing
  times price against data-centre revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- A tower's costs are fixed, so nearly all of a second tenant's rent is profit.
- For a data centre, electricity is the largest running cost. Where the customer pays it
  directly, revenue is lower and the margin higher for the same profit.
- A new data centre earns little until customers have moved in. The return to watch is
  the income of a full site over what it cost to build.
- Ground rent on towers rises each year, and buying the land removes it.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Data centres take a great deal of capital before any rent. They are funded by debt,
  new shares and outside partners.
- A hall leased before it is built carries little letting risk. One built without a
  tenant carries a lot.
- Towers need little upkeep capex once built.
- A few customers pay most of the rent. One contract renewed on worse terms, or one
  operator merging with another, changes the forecast.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Tower leasing rises with each network generation and falls between them.
- Data centres are in a building boom. The risks are too much capacity in one market,
  customers building their own, and power arriving late.
- Where rents are fixed for many years, both are valued like long-dated bonds, and the
  shares move with interest rates. Where prices are falling at each renewal, that does
  not hold.
- Traps:
  - contracts signed read as revenue;
  - a backlog that depends on power not yet connected;
  - revenue that includes electricity passed through;
  - rent averaged over the lease, which runs ahead of cash early on;
  - AFFO that leaves out capex the sites really need;
  - growth quoted without the tenants a merger removed;
  - the share in use measured on capacity in service, not on all that is built;
  - revenue in weak currencies read at last year's exchange rate;
  - sites sold to a fund the company manages, with the gain read as recurring.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How long demand from AI lasts, and whether new sites will earn what the first ones do.
- Whether the largest customers build their own.
- What tower leasing looks like once the present network generation is built.
- Where interest rates go.
- How the building plan is paid for.

Yardstick: EV/EBITDA, with price to AFFO beside it; a large stake in a separately run
business is valued on its own and added
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company owns 100 towers with one tenant each paying 10: revenue is 1,000. Each tower
costs 4 to run, so the margin is 600. A second tenant joins 20 towers at 8 each, adding
160 of revenue and 10 of cost. Revenue rises 16% and the margin 25%, to 750.

A data centre of 100 units of power costs 1,000 to build. Fully let at 1.3 a unit a year
it earns 130, less running costs of 30, which is 100: a return of 10% on cost. If only
half is let in the first year and running costs do not fall, it earns 65 less 30, which
is 35, while interest on 600 of debt at 6% is 36.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
