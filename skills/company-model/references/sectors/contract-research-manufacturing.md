# Contract research and manufacturing for drug makers

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A drug developer pays an outside firm to do work it would otherwise do itself. There are
three kinds of work, charged in different ways.

- **Laboratory research**: chemistry, biology and safety studies before a drug reaches
  patients. It is charged by the project, or by the scientist per year.
- **Clinical trials**: running trials for the drug's owner over several years, either as
  a whole trial or by supplying staff to the customer's own trials, which earns less.
  Revenue comes as the work is done, and includes payments to hospitals that are passed
  through to the customer at no margin.
- **Development and manufacturing**: making the drug, from small batches for early trials
  to commercial supply. Customers often reserve capacity and commit to a minimum.

Demand is decided by the customer's research budget and by how its drugs progress. Large
drug makers spend steadily. Small biotechs spend what they have raised. Most drugs fail in
trials, and one that reaches the market needs far larger volumes than it did in testing,
so a manufacturer's revenue follows its customers' molecules through the stages. Politics
also decides: rules on which countries' suppliers may work on a drug, and tariffs.

Four words need fixing first. **Orders**, or bookings, are new contracts signed.
**Backlog** is contracted work not yet booked as revenue. **Book-to-bill** is net orders
divided by revenue. **Conversion** is the share of backlog that becomes revenue in a
period.

Signs the card does not fit: the company sells research tools, reagents or instruments
from a catalogue; it owns drugs and earns from their sales or royalties (see the
[innovative drugs card](pharma.md)); it makes generic drug ingredients for its own
account (see the [generic drugs card](generic-drugs.md)); or a stake in a drug developer is more than about
15% of its value, in which case that stake gets its own build.

## 2. The driver tree

There are three routes, and a group may need each for a different unit. Use the backlog
route for clinical trials and for any unit that reports a backlog. Use projects and
capacity for manufacturing. Use headcount for laboratory work charged by the scientist.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure varies widely. Clinical-trial firms report gross orders,
  cancellations, backlog and book-to-bill each quarter, and some give fee revenue without
  pass-through. Manufacturers report capacity in litres, a backlog or cumulative contract
  value (sometimes only the minimum customers must take), projects by stage and large
  contract wins. Some give revenue by customer region, the share from the largest drug
  makers and growth excluding one-off products.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Customers' spending | Large drug makers' research budgets in their reports. Biotech funding (venture rounds, listings and follow-on offerings) from data providers and the trade press |
| Pipelines | Drugs in development by stage from pipeline databases and trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov) |
| Peers | Other firms' orders, book-to-bill and comments on requests for proposals. Announced capacity |
| Policy | Laws on which suppliers may work on government-funded programmes. Tariffs on drugs and ingredients |

- **What leads revenue.** Biotech funding leads orders by several quarters. Orders lead
  revenue by the length of the backlog. Customers' trial results and approvals lead
  commercial volumes. New sites lead by their ramp.
- **Backtest.** Opening backlog times conversion against reported revenue. Projects times
  revenue per project against segment revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- In research and trials, people are most of the cost. The margin depends on how busy the
  scientists are and on price, and hiring ahead of orders lowers it.
- In manufacturing, depreciation, materials and staff are the cost. The margin depends on
  how full the plants are, and a new plant brings cost before revenue.
- Late-stage and commercial work earns more per project, and more steadily, than early
  work.
- Pass-through revenue carries no margin. Compare margins on fee revenue.
- Trial revenue is booked in step with costs incurred against the estimated total cost of
  the contract. A change in that estimate moves revenue, and can lead to restatement.
- With dollar revenue and home-currency costs, the margin moves with the exchange rate.
- Prices are cut when funding dries up and firms compete for fewer projects.
- Adjusted profit often leaves out share-based pay, currency effects and gains or losses
  on shares held in customers. Show reported and adjusted profit side by side.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Capacity is built years ahead of the revenue it earns, and sites abroad cost more.
- Customers may reserve capacity with prepayments or minimum commitments. These appear as
  contract liabilities.
- Work done but not yet billed, and receivables, are exposed to small biotechs running out
  of money.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The funding cycle: when biotechs cannot raise money, early-stage orders fall first.
- One product can be a large part of a year's revenue and then end, as pandemic products
  did, or when a customer's patent expires.
- Politics can move orders between countries. It does so slowly, because moving a drug's
  manufacture to a new supplier takes years.
- Traps:
  - backlogs defined differently: firm orders only or with options, a limited number of
    years, minimum against expected value;
  - gross orders against orders net of cancellations, and changes in how cancellations are
    counted;
  - revenue that includes pass-through, and a book-to-bill lifted by it while fee revenue
    falls;
  - backlog that still holds trials which are no longer active;
  - one large product inside commercial revenue;
  - adjusted profit that leaves out currency and investment results;
  - project counts where a few projects carry most of the value;
  - units sold or closed, so compare continuing operations;
  - sales to companies in the same group.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether politics will take customers away, and how fast customers can really move.
- Whether a recovery in biotech funding reaches orders.
- Whether demand for one class of drug lasts, and how much capacity is chasing it.
- Whether prices and margins hold as capacity is added.
- Whether new sites abroad earn their cost.

Yardstick: P/E, read with growth when growth is fast, and EV/EBITDA
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A firm opens the year with a backlog of 1,000. It signs 600 of orders and 50 are
cancelled, so net orders are 550. It converts 45% of the opening backlog and 20% of the
year's net orders: 450 + 110 = 560 of revenue. The closing backlog is 990 and book-to-bill
is 0.98, so revenue will not grow next year unless conversion speeds up.

All 560 is billed in dollars. Costs are worth 400 in dollars at last year's exchange rate
but are paid in the home currency. If the home currency rises 5%, costs become 420 and
profit falls from 160 to 140, down 12.5%, with no change in the business.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
