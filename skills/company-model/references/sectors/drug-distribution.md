# Drug distribution

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Distributors buy medicines from drug makers and deliver them to hospitals, pharmacies
and smaller distributors. Revenue is very large and the margin very thin. The lines of
work earn differently.

- **Direct sales to hospitals**, in China, earn the most, and hospitals take months to
  pay.
- **Sales to pharmacies and clinics** earn less and are paid sooner.
- **Sales to other distributors** earn least.
- **Medical devices** and **supply services run inside hospitals** earn more than
  drugs.
- Many groups also own **pharmacies** and **drug factories**, which belong on their own
  cards.

Spending on medicine grows with age and illness. Policy decides the rest. In China,
state tenders that cut a drug's price cut the revenue of everyone who handles it, and
rules limit the number of distributors between maker and hospital.

In the US three distributors handle most drugs. Their profit depends on what they earn
buying generic drugs, on price rises for branded drugs, on specialty drugs, and on
contracts with a few very large customers.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Revenue** against **gross profit**.
- The days hospitals take to **pay** against the days the distributor takes to pay drug
  makers.
- Receivables **on the balance sheet** against receivables **sold to banks**.
- **Operating profit** against profit after **interest**, and profit after **partners'
  share**, which is large where subsidiaries are part-owned.
- Growth from **volume** against the effect of **price** cuts and rises.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is mainly a pharmacy chain (see the
[pharmacies card](pharmacies.md)); it makes the drugs (see the
[generic drugs card](generic-drugs.md) and the [innovative drugs card](pharma.md)); or it
distributes goods other than medicines (see the
[trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue and margin by segment, and receivables in days. US distributors
  guide profit for the year and comment on the price of generic and branded drugs each
  quarter.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Drug market | The size of the hospital drug market, and each round of state tenders |
| Rules | Rules on the number of distributors in the chain, and on how fast hospitals must pay |
| US | Weekly prescriptions, list-price changes for branded drugs, price falls for generics and new specialty drugs (IQVIA, and the distributors' trade association) |
| Interest rates | Short-term borrowing rates |

- **What leads profit.** Tender results. Rules on payment by hospitals. Interest rates.
  In the US, list-price changes at the start of the year and contract renewals.
- **Backtest.** Receivable days times revenue times the borrowing rate against interest
  paid. Revenue by line times its margin against gross profit.

## 4. Costs and margin

- A distributor earns a few percent on revenue. The cost of carrying receivables is as
  important as the gross margin.
- Size wins better terms from drug makers and spreads the cost of warehouses.
- In China a price cut on a drug cuts the distributor's revenue and its profit on that
  drug by about the same share. In the US a cheap generic can earn the distributor more
  than the brand it replaces.
- Devices and services earn more and tie up more capital.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- In China the business is working capital. Hospitals pay in months, and the
  distributor borrows to bridge the gap. In the US suppliers fund it, and debt comes
  from acquisitions.
- Selling receivables to a bank moves them off the balance sheet without removing the
  cost.
- Growth has come from buying local distributors, which leaves goodwill and partners.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand for medicine is steady. Pricing and payment rules are what change a year.
- Traps:
  - revenue growth with no cash;
  - receivable days creeping up;
  - receivables sold to banks, left out of debt;
  - operating profit quoted where interest takes much of it;
  - group profit quoted where partners own a large share;
  - a tender's price cut treated as a one-off;
  - in the US, one customer's contract behind much of the revenue.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether services and devices lift the margin.
- Where interest rates go.
- How fast hospitals pay.
- What further price cuts do to revenue.
- In the US, what new high-priced drugs and rules on drug prices do to profit, and how
  long buybacks continue.

Yardstick: P/E, with dividend yield beside it for the large Chinese distributors; P/E on
adjusted earnings for US distributors
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A Chinese distributor has revenue of 100,000m, a gross margin of 8% and operating costs
of 5%: operating profit is 3,000m. Hospitals pay in 120 days, so receivables are about
32,900m. It borrows 60% of that at 3.5%, which costs 690m. If hospitals take 20 days
longer, it borrows another 3,300m and pays 115m more.

After interest of 690m and tax at 25%, profit is 1,733m. Partners own a third of the
subsidiaries that earn it, so shareholders are left with about 1,155m.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
