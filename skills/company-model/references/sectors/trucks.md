# Trucks and their engines

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- **Trucks.** Makers sell heavy, medium and light trucks through dealers to fleets and
  owner-drivers. The buyer earns a living from freight. They buy when freight volumes
  and rates cover the payments, when old trucks must be replaced, or when a rule,
  subsidy or tariff changes the sums.
- **Parts and service** on the trucks already on the road, and **financing or leasing**
  of the maker's own sales.
- **Engines.** A maker that fits its own engine earns more per truck and more parts
  revenue later. An independent engine maker sells to truck makers, and to machinery,
  marine and power-generation customers.
- **Where it sells.** Some makers sell from one home base and export through
  distributors. Others build and sell in several markets, and each of those is a home
  market with its own industry data.

Two words need fixing before any row is built. **Deliveries**, or factory sales, are the
maker's sales to dealers, and are when it books revenue. **Retail sales**, or
registrations, are dealers' sales to end buyers. Market share is quoted on either,
depending on the market and the source, so check which before applying a share to a row.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes buses or coaches (sold direct to
operators and by tender, so use the [bus card](buses.md)); it
assembles vehicles for another brand for a fee; it sells into one tender-driven niche
such as defence; or a finance arm, a listed stake or a non-vehicle business earns more
than about 15% of profit, in which case that unit gets its own card.

## 2. The driver tree

Each row is a row, or a block of rows, in the revenue build, with history beside the
forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry retail sales | Each market the company sells in, by weight class | Use the series that exists and state its basis |
| Split by fuel | Diesel, gas, battery-electric, where more than one fuel matters | One row |
| Company share | Share of each cell, giving the company's retail units | Take shares by cell from industry data by maker and scale them to the company's reported total |
| Dealer stock | Home deliveries less home retail sales, cumulated from a period when stock was normal. Only the change can be measured | Leave it out and let home deliveries equal retail |
| Home deliveries | Retail units plus the change in dealer stock. Where the company reports only total sales, home deliveries are total sales less exports | |
| Export units | For a maker exporting from home: by region, grown from the same period a year earlier, tested against the company's plan and the industry's exports | One row, at the frequency the company gives |
| Total deliveries | Home deliveries plus exports. This is what the company reports and books | |
| Orders and backlog | Orders less cancellations less deliveries, from the company or, where it reports none, from industry trackers | Leave it out |
| Revenue per truck | By market, or home against export, and by product line | Group average, labelled as such |
| Truck revenue | Total deliveries times revenue per truck, by line | |
| Parts and service | Trucks in service times spend per truck. Build the fleet by adding each year's deliveries and removing old trucks | The past ratio of parts revenue to truck revenue, labelled as such |
| Financing | Revenue: loan and lease book times yield, plus lease rentals. Profit: after funding cost, depreciation of leased trucks and credit losses | Segment profit only |
| Engines sold outside the group | Customers' truck output times fitment share. Other uses on their own rows | Leave them inside the segment that reports them, and say so |

Build the full tree for each weight class that matters to profit, and carry the others as
units times revenue per truck. Rows can run at different frequencies: where a monthly row
rests on a figure the company gives once a year, say so.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Reports give units and revenue, by product and by region where disclosed.
  Some makers report a single segment. Some publish a monthly production and sales
  bulletin. Industry size and the company's share are often given once a year only, and
  on retail sales.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly factory sales by maker from the vehicle association (CAAM), relayed by trade sites; some series include exports. Monthly registrations from insurance records. Sales by fuel. Customs exports by destination |
| North America | Monthly orders and retail sales by brand from industry trackers (ACT Research, FTR), relayed by the trade press. Backlog and cancellations sit in the trackers' paid data |
| Europe | Registrations by weight class and fuel from the manufacturers' association (ACEA), quarterly |
| Elsewhere | National associations, of uneven detail |

- **What leads revenue.** Freight volumes and rates. Used-truck prices. Order intake,
  which leads deliveries by the length of the backlog. The gap between gas and diesel
  prices where gas trucks matter. Announced dates for emission rules, subsidies and
  tariffs.
- **Backtest.** Industry sales times share against the company's reported units. Units
  times revenue per truck against segment revenue. Match the scope first: one series can
  include exports or light trucks that the other leaves out.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost: steel, bought-in components and, for electric trucks,
  the battery. Tariffs on imported parts and steel add to it.
- Labour and depreciation are largely fixed within a year, so profit moves much more
  than volume. Show the fixed and variable parts separately.
- Margin also depends on what is sold and where: export against home, heavy against
  light, electric and gas against diesel, own engine against bought-in. Carry a margin
  for each line and let the group margin follow the mix.
- Price competition shows first as dealer rebates and discounts. Revenue per truck falls
  before list prices do.
- Export revenue per truck in the reporting currency moves with the exchange rate.
- Parts, service and financing earn more per unit of revenue than new trucks, and are
  steadier.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Assembly capacity rarely limits sales. The supply of engines, batteries or key parts
  can, and so can the overseas service network.
- Capex is modest against sales and goes to new platforms, powertrains and overseas
  plants.
- Trouble shows first in working capital: receivables from dealers and overseas
  distributors, stock at the factory and with dealers, and guarantees on financed sales.
- A truck sold with a buy-back or residual-value guarantee is booked in one of two ways,
  depending on the accounting rules and the terms: as a lease, with revenue coming in
  over the term, or as a sale, with the guarantee disclosed as a commitment. Either way,
  used-truck prices decide what it costs.
- Where there is a finance arm, keep its loan book, leased trucks and funding apart from
  industrial cash.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand is mostly replacement. Trucks bought in one boom come up for replacement
  together.
- A new emission rule, a scrappage subsidy or a tariff pulls purchases forward before
  its date and leaves a gap after it. Test whether the latest strong period is such a
  pull-forward.
- Fuel switching can change the mix within a year, and with it which engine maker wins.
- Exports are concentrated in a few markets. One country's tariff, registration fee or
  currency can remove a large share of them.
- Traps:
  - deliveries against retail sales, and share quoted on one applied to the other;
  - factory sales that include exports against home registrations;
  - revenue per truck read as a list price, when it also carries mix, rebates and
    currency;
  - heavy shipments to dealers before a period end;
  - finance income reported inside the truck segment;
  - profit that rests on one-off items such as subsidies and disposal gains;
  - where the listed company is part of a larger group: group volumes against the
    listed company's, and engine sales to sister companies that are eliminated on
    consolidation.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How long the upcycle lasts and how deep the payback is.
- Whether a strong period is real demand or purchases pulled forward by a rule or
  tariff.
- Whether exports are a lasting second business or a window that closes as rivals follow
  and tariffs rise.
- Whether electric and gas trucks move profit away from the maker's own engines.
- Whether parts, service and financing make earnings steadier than in past cycles.

Yardstick: P/E on earnings through the cycle, P/B near a trough, and dividend yield for
a maker that pays out most of its profit ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

The home industry sells 600 heavy trucks to end buyers: 300 diesel, 180 gas and 120
electric. The company's shares are 20%, 30% and 10%, so its retail sales are 60 + 54 +
12 = 126. Dealers cut their stock by 6, so the company delivers 120 at home. Exports were
80 last year and grow 10% to 88, so total deliveries are 208.

Revenue per truck is 300 at home and 380 abroad, so revenue is 36,000 + 33,440 = 69,440.
Margins of 8% at home and 20% abroad give gross profit of 2,880 + 6,688 = 9,568, a group
margin of 13.8%.

If buyers switch 60 trucks from gas back to diesel, retail sales fall to 72 + 36 + 12 =
120 with the industry total unchanged. A single industry growth rate would have missed
it.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
