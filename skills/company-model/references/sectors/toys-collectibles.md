# Toys and collectibles

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies sell goods built on characters: figures, plush, blind boxes, trading
cards and toys. The characters are their own, or licensed from a studio or an artist
for a royalty. Collectibles sell to young adults as well as children. Children's toys
sell mostly around holidays.

They reach buyers through their own shops and vending machines, through their own apps
and shops on the large platforms, and through retailers and distributors. Sellers of
collectibles have their goods made by others. Large traditional toy makers own factories
and tooling.

Which characters catch on decides everything. A hit can multiply sales within a year,
and it can fade. Prices on resale apps show how wanted a character is, though they are
not the company's sales.

Numbers that get confused:

- Sales **by character** against sales by channel or region.
- **Own** characters against **licensed** ones, which pay a royalty.
- The **resale price** of a piece against its retail price.
- Sales **to retailers** against what retailers **sell on**.
- Growth from **existing shops** against growth from new ones.
- **Members** registered against members who buy.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes games or films from its characters as
its main business (see the [games card](games.md) and the
[film and television production card](film-tv-production.md)); it makes toys for other
brands under contract; or it retails other companies' toys (see the
[retail card](retail.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each main character, and each channel and region, separately, with history beside
the forecast.

A maker that sells through large retailers is built by brand and by region, quarter by
quarter, with the holiday season in the last quarter. Brand and product category are two
cuts of the same sales: forecast on the one the company gives growth for, and check with
the other.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Characters and brands | The sales of each main character, series or brand, where it stands in its life, and what is due next | Their share of sales, with the largest named |
| Kinds of product | Blind boxes, plush, figures and cards: units times price. A new kind of product can extend a character | Sales by kind as reported |

| Abroad | Shops and online sales by region | Revenue by region |
| Supply | The factory capacity the company has booked. In a hit, supply limits sales | The company's comments |
| Gross margin | Own characters earn more than licensed ones. The mix of products, and higher prices abroad. Freight, tariffs and, for a maker with its own plants, factory costs | Its recent level, moved with the mix |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese sellers of collectibles give revenue by character, by channel and
  by region twice a year, with shop numbers and members, and some give ranges for
  growth each quarter. US toy makers give billings by brand and category and net sales
  by region each quarter, with guidance for the year.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Online sales | Monthly estimates of the brand's sales on the main platforms, from data vendors |
| Resale | Prices on resale apps. A sign of demand, not a measure of sales |
| Apps and search | Download rankings of the brand's app by country, and search interest |
| Toy market | Toy sales by licence in Western markets (Circana) |
| Films | Release dates of films behind licensed characters |

- **What leads revenue.** How fast a new series sells out. Resale prices. App rankings
  abroad. Factory orders. Film releases.
- **Backtest.** Sales by character against revenue. Shops times sales for each.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gross margin is high on the company's own characters and lower on licensed ones.
- Shops are fixed costs, so a hit lifts profit much faster than revenue, and a fade
  cuts it as fast.
- Where goods are made by others, cost follows volume. A maker with its own factories
  carries fixed costs.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A seller of collectibles needs little plant. In a hit, cash builds quickly.
- Stock made for a character that has faded is worth little.
- Factory capacity has to be booked months ahead, so supply lags a hit and then
  overshoots.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- A character's sales rise, peak and fade. Few last for decades.
- Regulators set rules on blind boxes and on sales to children.
- Traps:
  - one character behind most of the profit;
  - a hit year carried forward;
  - resale prices read as lasting demand;
  - new shops abroad counted before they make money;
  - supply catching up and ending the scarcity that drove demand;
  - a licence that expires;
  - sales to retailers ahead of a holiday read as demand.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How long the hit lasts.
- Whether the company can produce the next one.
- How much growth is left abroad.
- Whether the characters can earn from films, games and parks.

Yardstick: P/E, read with growth
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company sells 1,000: one character is 400 and the rest 600. Gross margin is 65% and
shop and other costs are 450, so profit is 200.

The character triples, to 1,200, and the rest grows 20%, to 720. Revenue is 1,920, up
92%. Gross profit is 1,248, costs rise to 650, and profit is 598, nearly three times
the year before.

The next year the character falls 30%, to 840, and the rest grows 20%, to 864. Revenue
is 1,704, down 11%. Gross profit is 1,108, costs are 700, and profit is 408, down 32%.

The build holds the gross margin at 65% to keep the sums short. In a fade, stock
written down lowers it.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
