# Exchanges

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

An exchange runs the markets where shares, derivatives and commodities are traded, and the
clearing houses that settle them. It earns in five ways.

- **Trading and clearing fees**, charged on the value or number of contracts traded.
- **Listing fees**, paid by companies when they list and every year after.
- **Data and connection fees**, paid by brokers and data vendors.
- **Custody and settlement fees**, on the securities it holds for members.
- **Interest** on the collateral members post with its clearing houses, and on its own
  cash.

Trading volume decides most of it, and the exchange controls none of what moves it. Share
trading follows investors' confidence, market levels and new listings. Derivatives trading
follows the need to hedge: uncertainty about interest rates, commodity prices and
currencies. In some markets the regulator approves fees. In others the exchange sets them
and tells the regulator. Interest rates decide what the collateral earns.

Two things get confused. The **headline turnover** of the market against the turnover that
**pays the full fee**, since some products and flows pay less. **Interest earned** on
collateral against the part that is **passed back** to members.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is a broker (see the
[brokers card](brokers.md)); it sells indices or data as its
main business, on subscriptions; or it runs a venue for digital assets, where it also
earns the spread.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each market and product separately, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Listing | New listings times the initial fee, plus listed companies times the annual fee | Listing revenue moved with the number of listings |
| Data and connections | Subscribers and connected members times the fee, with price rises | Its own growth |

| Costs | Staff, technology and depreciation, mostly fixed. Licence fees paid to index owners move with volume | Costs grown at their own rate, or as guided |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Exchanges publish their own volumes daily and monthly, which gives most of
  a quarter's trading revenue before results. Quarterly results give revenue by segment,
  the average fee per contract, interest on collateral and the amount of collateral held.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Volumes | The exchange's own statistics, and other exchanges' for comparison |
| Listings | Applications filed and funds raised |
| Markets | Index levels, volatility measures and interest rates |
| Rules | Fee changes, new products, wider access between markets, and duties on trading, which go to the government and not the exchange |

- **What leads revenue.** Daily turnover. Interest rates. Listing applications. Fee
  announcements.
- **Backtest.** Volume times fee per unit against trading and clearing revenue.
  Collateral times the net yield against interest income.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Costs are mostly fixed, so most of an increase in volume reaches profit, and most of a
  fall comes out of it.
- Interest on collateral has no cost attached. It rises and falls with rates and with how
  much the exchange passes back.
- Fee cuts and rebates to attract volume lower the fee per unit.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Clearing houses must hold capital and contribute to default funds.
- Members' collateral sits on the balance sheet and is not the exchange's money.
- Exchanges generate cash, pay out most of their profit, and sometimes buy other
  exchanges or data businesses with debt.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Share turnover moves with the market cycle, and can halve or double within a year.
  Derivatives volume is steadier and rises when markets are uncertain.
- A policy change, such as wider access for outside investors or a new listing regime,
  can lift volume for good.
- Traps:
  - a record quarter's turnover multiplied by four;
  - interest on collateral treated as permanent when rates are high;
  - headline turnover applied to the full fee when part of it pays less;
  - trading duties counted as the exchange's revenue;
  - gains on the exchange's own investments inside revenue.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether turnover has moved to a lastingly higher level.
- What a fall in interest rates takes away.
- Whether fees will be cut.
- Whether data and technology revenue can grow without volume.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A market trades 200bn a day for 245 days, 49,000bn a year. The exchange takes 0.01% in
trading and clearing fees, which is 4.9bn. Other revenue is 5.1bn, costs are 3bn and
profit is 7bn.

If daily turnover rises 30%, fees rise by 1.47bn and costs do not move. Profit rises 21%.

It holds 200bn of members' cash collateral and keeps a spread of 1% a year on it, which is
2bn of the other revenue. If rates fall and the spread is 0.5%, that falls by 1bn, a
seventh of profit.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
