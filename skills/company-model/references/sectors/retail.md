# Retail

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A retailer sells goods made by others, and some under its own label, through shops and
online. The formats differ in how they earn.

- **Supermarkets, convenience and discount chains** buy goods and resell them at a
  margin.
- **Membership stores** earn a large part of their profit from the yearly fee.
- **Department stores and malls run by a retailer** often let brands run counters and
  keep a share of their sales, so revenue is a commission and not the full sale.
- **Franchise chains** supply goods to shops owned by others, so their revenue is
  wholesale.
- **Grocers** often add a wholesale arm that supplies independent shops and caterers,
  sell fuel, and earn from advertising, customer data and insurance.

Shoppers decide demand: how many come, and how much each spends. Both follow incomes,
prices and what rivals and online sellers offer. A chain grows by opening shops and by
selling more in the shops it has.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Same-store sales growth**, which covers shops open for a year or more, against total
  growth, which includes new shops.
- **Sales at the till** against **revenue**, where part is commission or wholesale.
- **Visits** against **spend per visit**.
- **Gross margin**, which may include rebates and fees from suppliers.
- Profit **before** and **after** rent, which accounting rules treat in different ways.
- Shops the company **owns** against shops under **franchise**.
- A year of **53 weeks** against one of 52, and sales **with** and **without** fuel.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns the brands it sells (see the
[beauty brands card](beauty-personal-care.md) and the
[sportswear and apparel card](sportswear-apparel.md)); it is an online marketplace (see
the [e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)); it runs
restaurants (see the [restaurants card](restaurants.md)); it is a pharmacy chain (see the
[pharmacies card](pharmacies.md)); it sells duty-free goods to travellers (see the
[travel retail card](travel-retail.md)); or it owns the mall and collects
rent (see the [landlords card](reits-landlords.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Same-store sales growth and shop numbers each quarter, and sales by
  format and category. Some large chains report sales every month.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Official data | Monthly retail sales by category and by format. Consumer prices, food prices above all |
| Spending | Card-spending data and footfall counts, where published |
| Rivals | Their same-store sales and opening plans. The online share of retail |
| Share | Monthly grocery market shares and price inflation from shopper panels (Worldpanel, NielsenIQ) |
| Costs | Rents and minimum wages |

- **What leads profit.** Monthly retail sales. Rivals' same-store sales. Food prices,
  which lift a supermarket's sales in money terms. The opening plan. Holiday dates.
- **Backtest.** Average shops times sales per shop against revenue. Same-store growth
  plus the contribution of new shops against total growth.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Rent and staff are fixed for each shop. A few points of same-store sales, up or down,
  move a shop's profit by several times as much.
- Gross margin is a choice as well as a result: a retailer can give it up to win
  shoppers.
- Size brings better terms from suppliers and cheaper logistics.
- Online orders carry picking and delivery costs that shop sales do not.
- A membership fee is almost all profit.
- Wages are the largest cost a grocer controls. Each year it has to find savings to
  match the rise in pay.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A retailer is usually paid by shoppers before it pays suppliers. That funds growth,
  and drains cash when sales shrink.
- New shops need capex and lose money at first. Closing one costs lease penalties and
  write-offs.
- Leases are long, fixed commitments.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Sales follow consumer spending. The longer shift is towards online and discount
  formats.
- Traps:
  - total growth from new shops while existing ones shrink;
  - same-store figures that leave out closed or refitted shops;
  - a change between booking the full sale and booking a commission;
  - supplier rebates and one-off income inside gross margin;
  - profit quoted before rent;
  - a gain from selling shops and leasing them back;
  - new shops that take sales from the company's own nearby shops;
  - franchise openings paid for with the franchisor's loans.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether same-store sales recover.
- Whether a new format works and can be rolled out.
- How many more shops the market can take, and what a new one earns.
- Whether margin is spent on lower prices.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it; EV to sales where margins are thin
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A chain has 100 shops selling 10 each: revenue is 1,000. Gross margin is 25%, so gross
profit is 250. Shop costs are 200 and fixed, and central costs are 30. Profit is 20.

Same-store sales fall 3%. Gross profit falls by 7.5 and profit to 12.5, down 37.5%.

It opens 10 shops, which sell 60% of a mature shop's sales in their first year: 60 of
revenue and 15 of gross profit. They cost 20 to run and 2 to open, so they lose 7.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
