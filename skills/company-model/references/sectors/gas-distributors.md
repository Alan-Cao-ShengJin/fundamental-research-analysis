# City gas distributors

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A city gas distributor holds the sole right to pipe gas in each city it serves, usually
for decades. Most of the listed ones operate in China. It earns in five ways.

- **Retail gas.** It buys gas from the national producers and importers and sells it to
  households, shops, factories and vehicle filling stations. It keeps the margin per
  cubic metre.
- **Connection fees.** A one-off fee for each new home or business joined to the
  network.
- **Wholesale gas.** Gas resold outside its own cities, at a thin margin that swings
  with spot prices.
- **Energy for businesses.** Heat, steam, cooling and power from plants it builds at
  factories and industrial parks.
- **Services to households**: appliances, safety products and insurance.

Some also own liquefied gas terminals and plants, bottled gas businesses or stakes in
gas fields. Build those as separate units. For some distributors they earn a large part
of profit.

Factories and businesses use most of the gas. Their demand follows industrial activity,
the price of gas against coal, fuel oil and electricity, and rules that push them off
coal. Household use follows the number of homes connected and the weather. New
connections follow the homes completed each year, and the older districts still to be
joined.

The margin depends on what the gas costs and how fast a rise can be passed on. Factory
prices move quickly. Household prices need the local government's approval, which can
take months or years.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Volume** that includes joint ventures and associates, against the volume of the
  companies consolidated. The margin the company quotes times total volume is not its
  gross profit.
- The **margin per cubic metre** the company states, against gross profit over volume.
  Cost of sales holds more than gas.
- **Homes connected** in total, **new connections** in the period, the homes that
  **could be** connected, and the share that are.
- New connections in **new homes** against those in **existing homes**.
- **Core profit**, before currency gains and losses on foreign debt and changes in the
  value of hedges, against reported profit.

Signs the card does not fit: a regulator sets the company's revenue from the value of its
network (see the [regulated networks card](regulated-networks.md)); it mainly imports or
trades gas; or it runs long-distance pipelines for a transport tariff.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Results twice a year, and for some a quarterly operating update.
  Disclosure varies. Most give retail volume and new connections, and guide each year on
  volume growth, the margin, connections and the dividend. Some add volume by type of
  customer, the margin per cubic metre, connections split between new and existing
  homes, and gross profit by business. Others give only each segment's profit before
  tax, and the margin has to come from what management says.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Gas use | National gas consumption, monthly (National Development and Reform Commission, National Energy Administration) |
| Purchase cost | The benchmark price at the city gate. The national producers' contract terms for the year, set each spring. Spot prices for imported gas and for gas delivered by truck (Shanghai Petroleum and Natural Gas Exchange) |
| Household prices | Local governments' notices that pass a cost rise on to households |
| Connections | Housing completions (National Bureau of Statistics) |
| Demand | Industrial output. Prices of coal and fuel oil. Winter temperatures |

- **What leads profit.** The producers' yearly contract terms lead purchase cost. Local
  price approvals lead the margin. Housing starts lead completions, and so connections,
  by two or three years. Oil prices lead the cost of imported contract gas by months.
- **Backtest.** Consolidated volume times the stated margin against gross profit on gas
  sales, with the gap explained. New connections times the fee against connection
  revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gas is most of the cost, and the margin is a small part of the selling price. A rise in
  purchase cost that is not passed on takes a large part of profit.
- Households pay the lowest price and earn the distributor the least. Factories and
  businesses earn more, and can leave for another fuel if gas becomes dear.
- Connection fees earn a far higher margin than selling gas, and fall when fewer homes
  are built.
- Energy projects and household services are how distributors try to replace connection
  fees. Each has its own margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Once a city's pipes are laid, capex is modest and free cash flow is positive.
- Many customers pay in advance, so the business holds customers' money.
- Old pipes have to be replaced on a timetable the government sets after accidents.
- Debt in foreign currency moves reported profit with the exchange rate.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Volume follows industry and the weather. A warm winter lowers it.
- Policy decides the margin: how purchase prices are set, when household prices may
  rise, and how much may be charged for a connection.
- Traps:
  - the stated margin times total volume read as consolidated profit;
  - connection fees read as lasting income;
  - volume growth that is low-margin wholesale;
  - a year of profit on reselling imported gas read as normal;
  - currency and hedge gains inside reported profit;
  - the share of households with pass-through quoted by city count and not by volume;
  - concessions that expire, or cities where a rival pipes gas directly to large users.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether household prices will be allowed to catch up with costs.
- What replaces connection fees.
- Whether factories keep choosing gas.
- How much of the cash is paid out.
- What the parent does: asset sales to the listed company, or an offer to buy it out.

Yardstick: P/E and dividend yield
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A distributor reports volume of 1,000 at a margin of 0.50. Joint ventures sell 300 of
that. Its own gross profit on gas is 700 times 0.50, which is 350. Dividing 350 by 1,000
gives 0.35, which is not the margin.

It connects 100 homes and earns 1.5 on each: 150. If new homes fall 30%, it loses 45. At
a margin of 0.50 that takes 90 more of its own volume to replace, growth of 13%.

Its purchase cost rises by 0.10 and it cannot yet raise the price on the 200 it sells to
households. It loses 20, about 6% of its gross profit on gas.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
