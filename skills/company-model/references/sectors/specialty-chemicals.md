# Specialty chemicals and industrial gases

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A specialty chemical is bought for what it does in the customer's product, not to a
standard specification: coatings, adhesives, additives, catalysts, flavours and
fragrances, crop protection, construction chemicals, engineering plastics, and the
chemicals used to make chips and screens. The customer has usually tested and approved
it, and it is a small part of the customer's own cost, so switching supplier is slow.

**Industrial gases** work differently and need their own rows. Oxygen, nitrogen and
hydrogen are supplied in three ways: from a plant built at the customer's site under a
contract of ten to twenty years with a minimum payment; as liquid delivered by truck;
and in cylinders.

Demand follows what the customers make: buildings, cars, electronics, consumer goods,
crops. On top of that a company gains or loses share through new products, new plants,
and rules that favour one chemistry over another. In China, local makers replacing
imports is a driver of its own.

Raw materials mostly come from oil and gas. Their cost is passed on to customers, by a
formula in the contract or by negotiation, with a lag of one to three quarters.

Numbers that get confused:

- Growth from **volume**, from **price**, from **mix**, from **currency** and from
  **acquisitions**. Companies show these as a bridge.
- A **percentage margin** against a **margin per unit**. When raw materials rise and are
  passed on, revenue rises, the percentage margin falls and the profit per unit is the
  same.
- **Capacity** against **output** against **sales**.
- Profit **before** the items the company calls exceptional.

Signs the card does not fit: the product's price follows industry capacity and the cost
of its feedstock, whatever the company calls it (see the
[commodity chemicals card](commodity-chemicals.md)); the company distributes chemicals
it does not make (see the [trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)); or
it makes drugs.

Build each segment or product line separately, with history beside the forecast.

If it reports by region and not by the way the gas is supplied, build each region from
its bridge of volume, price and cost passed through, and use the supply split as a

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Western companies give the growth bridge by segment each quarter, with
  the margin and comments on raw materials. Chinese listed chemical makers publish
  output, sales volume and average price of their main products, and the prices of their
  main raw materials, every quarter. Annual reports give capacity by product and
  projects under construction.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Customers' industries | Building starts and completions, vehicle output, chip production, crop prices and planted area, factory surveys |
| Raw materials | Price series for the main inputs (ICIS and Platts; in China, Baiinfo and SCI99) |
| Trade | Customs imports and exports by product code, in volume and value, which show local makers replacing imports and the price of exports |
| Rivals | Their capacity plans and start dates |

- **What leads profit.** Raw material prices move the margin the opposite way first and
  then pass through. Customers running stocks down or building them up. The customers'
  own output. New plants starting. Approvals won at large customers.
- **Backtest.** Volume times price against segment revenue. The change in input prices,
  after the lag, against the change in gross margin.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Raw materials are the largest cost. When they rise fast the margin is squeezed for a
  few quarters and then recovers. When they fall the reverse happens, until customers
  ask for the saving.
- Plants, research and sales staff are fixed, so volume moves profit by more than it
  moves revenue.
- A gas plant at a customer's site is paid at least the contract minimum however little
  the customer takes, so its return is steady. Gas delivered by truck is a local
  business: the company with the most customers near its plant has the lowest cost.
- A new plant brings its full depreciation from the first day and its sales only as
  customers approve the product.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A plant takes two or three years to build, and customers' approval can take as long
  again.
- Stock and receivables grow when input prices rise, so cash flow lags profit in those
  years.
- Many companies in the sector grow by buying others, and carry goodwill.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows the industrial cycle, and customers' stock changes make it swing
  further.
- A specialty can turn into a commodity when rivals add capacity. New Chinese capacity
  has done this to many products.
- Tariffs, anti-dumping duties and environmental shutdowns change who can sell where.
- Traps:
  - price rises that only pass on raw materials, read as pricing power;
  - a percentage margin that moved because revenue did;
  - gains on stock bought before input prices rose;
  - a new plant counted at full output from its first year;
  - exceptional costs that come back every year;
  - one large customer or one export market;
  - subsidies and tax refunds inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the margin is truly steady through a cycle.
- When volume recovers.
- Whether prices hold when raw materials fall.
- What new plants and acquisitions earn.
- In China, how much further local makers can replace imports.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company sells 100 units at 10: revenue is 1,000. Raw materials are 5 a unit, so the
margin is 5 a unit and 500 in all. Fixed costs are 300 and profit is 200.

Raw materials rise 20%, to 6. The company raises its price by 1, but only after two
quarters. In the first year it loses 1 a unit on half the volume: profit falls by 50,
to 150. In the second year the price is 11 and the margin per unit is back at 5.
Revenue is up 10%, the percentage margin has fallen from 50% to 45%, and profit is 200
again.

A gas company has plants costing 1,000 in its backlog. Past plants have brought 0.2 of
yearly sales, before energy, for each 1 spent. Once all are running the backlog adds
about 200 of sales a year.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
