# Steel, aluminium, cement and other basic materials

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies turn bulk raw materials into standard materials in large plants.

- **Steel**, from iron ore and coking coal in a blast furnace, or from scrap and
  electricity in an electric furnace.
- **Aluminium**, from alumina and a great deal of electricity.
- **Cement**, from limestone and coal. Clinker is the half-finished product.
- **Glass** and **paper**, from soda ash or pulp, and energy.

Buyers are builders, machinery and car makers, appliance makers and packagers. The
products are standard, so the market sets the price. Steel and aluminium are priced
nationally or on world exchanges. Cement and glass cost too much to move far, so each
region has its own price, set by local demand and by how many producers share it.

Demand follows building, both property and public works, then manufacturing and exports.
Supply is shaped by policy as much as by companies: limits on capacity and output,
environmental rules, the price of carbon, and duties on trade.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Capacity**, **output** and **sales**.
- The price **with** and **without** sales tax, and the **benchmark** against the price
  **realised** on the company's own mix of products.
- The **spread**, which is price less raw materials, against **gross profit per tonne**,
  which is also after energy, staff and depreciation.
- Raw materials and power the company **supplies itself** against those it **buys**. A
  steelmaker with its own mine, or a smelter with its own power plant, earns the
  supplier's profit as well.
- **Clinker** against **cement**.

Signs the card does not fit: the company mines the ore and sells it (see the
[metal miners card](metal-miners.md)); it rolls or shapes bought metal for a processing
fee, where the metal price passes through and the fee is the business; or it sells
branded building products through dealers, where the brand and the sales network decide
the price.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each product separately, and each region where prices differ by region, with
history beside the forecast. A company that sells a half-finished product as well as the
finished one, such as alumina beside aluminium, builds each as its own product and takes
out what it uses itself.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output | Capacity times the rate of use, held back by output limits, maintenance, winter shutdowns and cuts the producers agree | Output growth |
| Sales volume | Output less the change in stock, by product and region, with exports apart | Sales volume as reported |
| Price | The benchmark for the product and region, adjusted for the company's mix, without sales tax. Contract and spot sales apart where both exist. Metal sold molten to nearby customers is priced a little below the benchmark for ingot | The realised price as a ratio to the benchmark, from history |
| Raw materials per tonne | The amount of each input used per tonne, from the company's own figures, times its price. The self-supplied part at cost and the rest at the market price, with inputs from part-owned suppliers counted as bought. Where the company gives no usage figures, use the industry's standard amounts per tonne and say so. Stock delays the effect by weeks | The industry's published spread, adjusted by the company's past gap to it |
| Energy per tonne | Power or fuel used per tonne times its price, by source where plants differ: the company's own coal-fired power, power from the grid, and hydropower, which can be rationed in dry months. The cost of carbon permits | Its past share of cost, moved with the fuel price |
| Processing cost per tonne | Staff, upkeep and depreciation. Fixed in total, so it falls per tonne as the rate of use rises | Cost per tonne from history |
| Gross profit per tonne | Price less raw materials, energy and processing | The industry's published margin, adjusted by the company's past gap to it |
| Other products | Crushed stone and concrete for a cement maker, by-products, and further processing | Segment profit |
| Selling, freight and overheads | Per tonne | Their share of revenue |
| Partners | The share of profit from plants not consolidated, and the partners' share of those that are | As reported |
| Capex | Upkeep, environmental upgrades, capacity bought from others and plants abroad | The guided figure. With none, announced projects over their build years, plus other capex at its recent level |
| Interest, tax and payout | Debt times its cost. The tax rate. The dividend policy and buybacks | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Output, sales volume and average price each quarter or half-year. Cement
  makers often give the cost per tonne and its make-up. Annual reports give capacity by
  plant and how much ore, alumina or power the company supplies itself.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly output of steel, cement, aluminium and glass (National Bureau of Statistics). Steel output and stocks every ten days (China Iron and Steel Association). Weekly prices, producers' margins, stocks and furnace use (Mysteel, SMM). Regional cement prices and shipments weekly (Digital Cement). Building starts and completions, public-works spending, customs exports. Coal prices at the mine and the port |
| United States | Weekly steel output and rate of use (American Iron and Steel Institute). Steel and scrap prices from price reporters. Cement shipments (USGS) |
| World | Monthly steel output by country (World Steel Association). Aluminium prices and stocks (LME, SHFE), and alumina prices |

- **What leads profit.** Weekly prices and margins give most of a quarter before it is
  reported. Building starts and public funding lead demand. Announcements on output
  limits. Stocks at producers and traders.
- **Backtest.** Sales volume times gross profit per tonne against gross profit. The
  realised price against the benchmark.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Raw materials and energy are most of the cost. For steel and cement the spread is
  small against the price, so profit per tonne moves far more than the price does. An
  aluminium smelter with cheap power of its own can earn a wide margin, which moves with
  the metal price.
- Processing cost is fixed, so the rate of use matters as much as the spread.
- For aluminium, power is the largest cost. Where a smelter gets its power decides
  whether it makes money at a given price.
- For cement, the number of producers in a region and whether they hold back output
  decide the price.
- When raw material prices change, stock bought earlier brings a gain or a loss for a
  few weeks.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Where capacity is capped, as for steel and aluminium in China, growth comes from
  buying capacity, upgrading plants or building abroad.
- A mature producer spends on upkeep and environmental rules, and can pay out much of
  its profit or buy rivals.
- In a weak market, customers take longer to pay. Cement makers' receivables show it
  first.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Steel, cement and glass follow the building cycle, and profit swings hard. Aluminium
  also follows power networks, vehicles and packaging, and the limits on its supply.
- Policy on supply can lift margins quickly, and its end can drop them as fast.
- Duties and export rebates move steel and aluminium between countries.
- Building stops in winter in cold regions, so the quarters are unequal.
- Traps:
  - a peak spread treated as normal;
  - sales volume that includes material bought and resold;
  - prices with tax set against prices without;
  - one region's benchmark applied to another;
  - gains on stock inside profit;
  - capacity read as permitted output;
  - profit that belongs to the company's own mine or power plant, read as the margin on
    making the material;
  - plants bought from the parent.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- When building demand stops falling.
- Whether limits on supply hold.
- Where raw material prices go.
- Whether the industry consolidates.
- How much cash is paid out.

Yardstick: P/B against return on equity, with enterprise value per tonne of capacity
against the cost of building it, and EV/EBITDA; P/E and dividend yield when profit is
steady ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A steelmaker sells 10m tonnes at 4,000: revenue is 40bn. Raw materials are 2,600 a tonne,
energy 400 and processing 700, so gross profit is 300 a tonne and 3bn in all.

The price falls 5%, by 200, and raw materials fall 4%, by 104. Gross profit is 204 a
tonne, down 32%.

In another case the price holds and output falls 10%. The 7bn of processing cost is
spread over 9m tonnes, 778 a tonne. Gross profit is 222 a tonne and 2bn in all, down a
third.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
