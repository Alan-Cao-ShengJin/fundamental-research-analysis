# Pharmacy chains

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Pharmacy chains sell prescription drugs, over-the-counter medicines, health supplements
and devices through shops they own or franchise, and online. In some countries they
also sell cosmetics and everyday goods, which can earn more than the medicines.

Spending on medicine grows with age and illness. Who pays decides what a pharmacy
earns.

- **Patients** pay for over-the-counter products and supplements, which earn the highest
  margins. In many countries they also pay for most prescriptions themselves, at prices
  the government caps and lets rise once a year.
- **Public insurance** pays for many prescriptions, at prices it sets. In China it
  decides which pharmacies it will pay and how much patients can spend from their own
  accounts. Prescriptions are slowly moving from hospitals to pharmacies.
- **Private insurers and their agents**, in the US above all, set what a pharmacy is
  paid for each prescription.

Flu and other outbreaks lift sales for a season and make the next year's comparison
hard.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Same-store sales growth** against growth from new and bought shops.
- Shops the chain **owns** against **franchised** shops, to which it only sells goods.
- **Gross margin by category**: prescription drugs earn least and own-label products
  most.
- **Visits** against **spend per visit**.
- Sales paid by **insurance** against sales paid by the customer.
- Revenue **before** and **after** sales taxes, where margins are quoted on the first.
- Profit **with** and **without** the accounting rules for leases.

Signs the card does not fit: the company's main business is delivering drugs to
hospitals and other sellers (see the [drug distribution card](drug-distribution.md), and
build a pharmacy arm here); it makes the drugs (see the
[generic drugs card](generic-drugs.md) and the [innovative drugs card](pharma.md)); or
it is a general retailer (see the [retail card](retail.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each region, and owned and franchised shops, separately, with history beside the
forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shops | Opening shops, plus opened, bought and franchised, less closed, by the year each opened | The net openings the company guides. With none, the recent pace of openings, purchases and closures |
| Same-store sales | Visits times spend per visit, by category. The effect of insurance rules, of any regulated price rise, and of the previous year's outbreaks. One class of drug can be a tenth of sales | The figure the company reports, on its trend |
| New shops | A new shop takes a year or two to break even | A share of a mature shop's sales in the first year |
| Franchise | Franchised shops times the goods each buys from the chain | Wholesale revenue growth |
| Gross margin | The mix of categories, the share of own-label products, makers' rebates, what insurers pay for prescriptions, and the share sold wholesale | Its recent level, lowered as prescription drugs grow |
| Shop costs | Rent, pharmacists and other staff, mostly fixed for each shop | Their share of sales |
| Online | Orders through the chain's own app, delivery apps and by post, with platform fees and delivery cost. Where orders are filled from shops, treat online as a share of shop sales and as a cost | Its share of sales |
| Shops bought | The price paid for each chain bought, and the goodwill | Announced deals only |
| Central costs | Warehouses, delivery to shops and head office | Their share of sales |
| Working capital | Stock in days, payables, and money owed by insurance funds | Their past ratios to sales |
| Capex and leases | The cost of a new shop, and rent commitments | The guided figure. With none, new shops times the cost to open one, plus upkeep at its recent level |
| Debt and interest | Borrowing and its cost, which takes a large part of profit where rates are high | As reported |
| Partners and payout | Partners' share of subsidiaries. Dividends | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Shop numbers, openings and closures each quarter. Some give same-store
  growth, sales by category and the share of sales paid by insurance.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacy sales | Monthly estimates of retail pharmacy sales from data vendors, mostly paid and quoted by brokers. The number of pharmacies in the country |
| Insurance rules | Notices on personal accounts, on which pharmacies may dispense insured prescriptions, on comparing prices across shops, and on what insurers pay |
| Illness | The weekly flu index from the disease-control centre |
| Rivals | Other chains' openings, closures and same-store sales |

- **What leads revenue.** The flu index. Insurance rules as each province adopts them.
  Monthly pharmacy sales. The pace of openings and closures.
- **Backtest.** Average shops times sales per shop against revenue. Same-store growth
  plus the contribution of new shops against total growth.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Rent and staff are fixed, so same-store sales move a shop's profit by several times as
  much.
- More prescription drugs mean more sales and a lower gross margin. Own-label and
  exclusive products work the other way.
- Size wins better terms from drug makers and spreads the cost of warehouses.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Chains have grown by buying shops, and carry goodwill that has to be written down
  when those shops weaken.
- A pharmacy is paid at the till for most sales and pays suppliers later. Insurance
  funds pay more slowly.
- A new shop needs little capex and a year or two of losses.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand for medicine is steady. Insurance and pricing rules are what change a year.
- Where too many pharmacies have opened, shops close and the survivors gain.
- Traps:
  - more shops with falling same-store sales;
  - growth that was bought, and the goodwill with it;
  - a year lifted by an outbreak used as the base;
  - a rule change treated as temporary;
  - sales to franchisees counted as retail;
  - a gross margin held up by rebates that makers can withdraw.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How fast prescriptions move out of hospitals, and who gets them.
- Whether the chains consolidate after closures.
- What price comparison and online selling do to margins.
- What insurers will pay.
- Outside China, how many more shops the market can take, and what a new one earns.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A chain has 10,000 shops selling 1.5m each: revenue is 15,000m. Gross margin is 35%. Shop
costs are 26% of sales and central costs 4%, and both are fixed. Profit is 750m.

Same-store sales fall 3%. Revenue falls 450m and gross profit 157.5m. Profit falls to
592.5m, down 21%.

At another chain prescription drugs earn 15% and everything else 43%. A rise in
prescriptions from 30% of sales to 35% lowers its gross margin from 34.6% to 33.2%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
