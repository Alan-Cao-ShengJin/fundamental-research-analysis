# Display panels

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Panel makers turn glass into screens for televisions, monitors, laptops, phones and
cars. There are two products, and they are built differently.

- **LCD panels** are close to a standard product, made by a handful of companies and
  sold by size. The price is published twice a month and follows how full the
  industry's fabs are.
- **OLED panels** come in three kinds. Small ones for phones are sold by the unit to a
  few brands: each is made for one model, the yield decides the cost, and a maker's
  share of a model can change every year. Large ones for televisions and monitors are
  sold by area. Screens for cars are sold under contracts that run for the life of a
  car model.

A fab is built for one size of glass sheet. Its cost is mostly depreciation, which is
fixed.

Demand, measured in area, is the number of devices sold times the size of the screen.
Televisions keep getting larger, so area grows when units do not. Buyers are television
and device makers. They stock up before promotions and when prices are rising, and stop
when prices fall.

Supply is the number of fabs and how hard they are run. The makers now cut their running
rates to hold the price.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Area** against **units**.
- The price of a **panel** of a given size against revenue **per unit of area**.
- **Capacity** in glass sheets against capacity in area.
- The **industry's** share of capacity in use against the **company's**.
- **Cash cost** against full cost, which adds depreciation.
- Profit of a **fab** against the part that belongs to shareholders, where a local
  government co-owns it.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes parts for panels, such as glass,
polarising film or driver chips; it assembles televisions (see the
[consumer electronics brands card](consumer-electronics-brands.md) and the
[contract manufacturing card](contract-manufacturing.md)); or it makes other parts (see
the [electronic components card](electronic-components.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese makers give area produced, sold and in stock for LCD and OLED
  once a year, with revenue and margin for the display segment, and little by fab.
  Taiwanese makers publish revenue every month, and area shipped and price per unit of
  area each quarter. Korean makers give area, price per unit of area and the mix by use
  each quarter.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Prices | Panel prices by size, twice a month (TrendForce, Omdia, Sigmaintell) |
| Supply | The share of capacity in use by maker, and capacity plans (Omdia, TrendForce, Counterpoint) |
| Demand | Shipments of televisions, monitors, laptops and phones, and average screen size |
| Buyers | Television makers' purchase plans and stock |
| Rules | Subsidies for replacing old appliances, tariffs on televisions and panels |

- **What leads profit.** The panel price. Television makers' stock and the calendar of
  promotions. The makers' cuts to running rates. The ramp of new fabs. Subsidy and
  tariff dates.
- **Backtest.** Area sold times price per unit of area against revenue. Price less cash
  cost against gross margin.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Depreciation is fixed, so the panel price and the share of capacity in use decide
  profit.
- When the price falls below cash cost, makers cut running rates.
- When a fab finishes being written off, its profit rises with no change in price.
- An OLED line loses money until its yield and its orders are both high.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A fab costs a great deal. In China local governments paid for much of each one, and
  the listed company buys out their stakes later.
- Building of LCD fabs has largely stopped. Capex is now for OLED and for larger glass.
- Stock builds at makers and at buyers before a price turn.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The price moves in cycles of a year or two, set by buyers' stock. With fewer makers
  the swings have narrowed.
- Subsidies for buyers pull demand forward.
- A maker that sells to phone brands earns most in the second half, when new models
  launch, and may depend on one customer.
- Traps:
  - a forecast on price alone, when area and mix also move;
  - units counted while screen size changes;
  - a margin forecast that leaves out a new line's depreciation, or the end of an old
    line's;
  - grants inside profit;
  - partners' share of a fab left out;
  - write-downs treated as one-off in a falling market;
  - an OLED line counted at full yield.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the makers keep holding back output.
- How much profit the end of depreciation releases, and when.
- Whether the OLED lines ever earn their cost.
- How long screens keep getting larger.
- What is paid out once building stops.

Yardstick: P/B through the cycle, with EV/EBITDA beside it because depreciation is so
large; P/E once profit is steady
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker's fabs can make 100 units of area. It runs at 80% and sells 80 at a price of 10:
revenue is 800. Cash cost is 6 a unit, 480 in all, and depreciation is 250. Gross profit
is 70.

The price rises 10%, to 11. Revenue is 880 and gross profit 150, more than double.

A fab that carried 60 of depreciation finishes being written off. At the old price of 10
gross profit is 130 and not 70.

If area sold rises 8% and revenue rises 1%, the price per unit of area fell 6.5%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
