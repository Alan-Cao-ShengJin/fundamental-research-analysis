# Packaged food and drink

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies make branded food and drink: dairy, soft drinks, bottled water, snacks,
noodles, sauces and seasonings, frozen food. Makers of household and personal products
sold through the same shops, such as tissue, detergent and nappies, are built the same
way. They sell through several channels.

- **Distributors**, who supply small shops and restaurants. In China distributors often
  pay before the goods ship.
- **Supermarket and convenience chains**, supplied directly, which pay later and ask for
  fees and promotions.
- **Online shops**, discount snack chains and membership stores, which have grown at the
  expense of the others.
- **Restaurants and caterers**, for sauces and frozen food.

In a mature market people eat and drink about the same amount each year, so volume grows
slowly and can fall in a weak year. Revenue grows through price, through dearer
products, through new products, and by reaching more shops. In poorer countries volume
still grows with incomes. Hot weather helps drinks. Eating out helps sauces and frozen
food.

The maker books a sale when goods leave for the distributor. What shoppers buy can be
more or less than that for several quarters, and the gap is stock in the channel.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Shipments** to distributors against **sales to shoppers**.
- Growth from **volume**, from **price** and from **mix**.
- The **list price** against the price after rebates and promotions. Some companies take
  promotions off revenue and others book them as a cost.
- **Advance payments** from distributors, which sit on the balance sheet and show how
  keen they are to stock up.
- Growth from the company's **own business** against acquisitions and currency.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes beer (see the [beer card](beer.md)) or
spirits (see the [baijiu card](baijiu.md) and the [spirits card](spirits.md)); it runs
restaurants (see the [restaurants card](restaurants.md)); it is a retailer (see the
[retail card](retail.md)); or it processes farm commodities at a spread.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by product, region and channel, quarterly for companies listed in
  mainland China, with the number of distributors and advances from them. Western
  companies split growth into volume and price and mix each quarter.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Retail trackers | Sales and share by category and brand in shops and online (NielsenIQ, Kantar). Mostly paid, and quoted by the companies |
| Official data | Monthly output of dairy and soft drinks. Monthly retail sales of food and drink, and restaurants' revenue |
| Inputs | Prices of raw milk, sugar, grain, palm oil, plastic resin, aluminium and paper |
| Channels | Store counts of the discount chains and membership stores. Online sales estimates by brand |

- **What leads profit.** Input prices lead gross margin by one or two quarters. Stock at
  distributors. Announced price rises. Advances from distributors at the year end. The
  weather in summer.
- **Backtest.** Volume times price against revenue by category. Lagged input prices
  against gross margin.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Raw materials and packaging are most of the cost of goods. When they fall, margin
  rises until rivals start to promote harder.
- Advertising and promotion are the largest cost after that, and the easiest to cut in a
  bad year. A cut lifts this year's profit and costs share later.
- A price rise sticks when the category leader makes it and others follow.
- A company with its own trucks, sales force and coolers in shops has a cost that new
  entrants cannot match quickly.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- For most branded makers, plants are small against the profit they support, and the
  business produces cash and pays much of it out. Dairy and drinks plants are heavier.
- Distributors who pay in advance fund the maker. Supermarket chains do the opposite.
- New channels buy in bulk and ask for lower prices, which moves margin from the maker
  to the channel.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand holds up better than most in a weak economy, though shoppers trade down to
  cheaper products and eat out less.
- A food safety scare can halve a brand's sales in a quarter.
- Traps:
  - shipments ahead of what shoppers buy, before a price rise or a year end;
  - a price rise given back in promotions;
  - a higher average price that is only mix;
  - a margin lifted by cheap inputs, read as pricing power;
  - advertising cut to meet a profit target;
  - advances from distributors falling, measured on the same date each year;
  - growth that was bought.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether volume grows again.
- Whether shoppers keep moving to dearer products or trade down.
- Where input costs go.
- What the new channels do to price and margin.
- How much is paid out.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it; dividend yield for a mature company
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker sells 100 units at 10: revenue is 1,000. Materials and packaging are 5 a unit and
other factory costs 1, so gross profit is 400. Selling costs are 250 and profit is 150.

Inputs rise 10%, by 0.5 a unit. Profit falls to 100, down a third, unless the price rises
5%.

If volume falls 5% instead and the other factory costs of 100 are fixed, gross profit is
375 and profit 125, down 17%.

Shoppers buy 100 units, and distributors add 5 to their stock: the maker ships 105. Next
year shoppers buy 3% more, 103, and distributors run their stock down by 5. The maker
ships 98, down 7%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
