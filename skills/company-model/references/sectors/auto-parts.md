# Auto parts

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A parts maker supplies components for vehicles: glass, seats, lights, chassis and body
parts, cooling systems, electronics. There are two markets.

- **Car makers.** The supplier is chosen for a model two or three years before it is
  launched and supplies it for the model's life, usually five to seven years. The price
  falls by an agreed percentage each year. Volume is whatever the car maker builds.
- **The replacement market.** Parts for cars already on the road, sold through
  distributors and workshops. Demand follows the number of cars in use and how far they
  are driven. Brands matter more here, and prices hold better.

For supply to car makers, three things decide revenue: how many vehicles each customer
builds, the value of the company's parts on each vehicle, and the price. The value per
vehicle rises when cars carry more of what the company makes, such as larger glass roofs
or more electronics, and when the company wins more of each model.

Numbers that get confused:

- Vehicles **built** against vehicles **sold**. Parts follow production.
- **Awards won**, which are an estimate of sales over a model's whole life, against
  revenue. An award is worth only what the model sells.
- **Value per vehicle**, which companies define over different sets of vehicles.
- **Growth above the market**, which is the company's growth less the growth in vehicle
  production.
- Revenue from **tooling** paid for by the customer, against revenue from parts.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes tyres (see the [tyres card](tyres.md));
it makes batteries (see the [batteries card](batteries.md)); it makes engines for trucks (see the
[trucks card](trucks.md)); it makes chips; or it distributes parts it does not make (see
the [trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each product line and customer region separately, with history beside the
forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customers' production | Vehicles built by each main customer, by model where the company's parts are on only some | Vehicle production in the region |
| Value per vehicle | The value of the company's parts on each vehicle, by product. It rises with new products and richer versions | Revenue over customers' production |
| Share of each model | Whether the company supplies all of a model or shares it | Leave it inside value per vehicle |
| New models | Awards won, by launch date, time to full volume and expected volume. Models that end | The growth above the market the company guides. With none, its past growth above customers' production |
| Price | The yearly cut in the contract, less what is recovered for raw materials | The net price change from history |
| Revenue from car makers | Production times value per vehicle times share, by customer | Vehicle production growth plus growth above the market |
| Replacement market | Cars in use times how often the part is replaced, times share, times price. Stock at distributors | Its own growth |
| Raw materials | Steel, aluminium, plastics, rubber or copper: the amount used times the price, and the share that contracts pass on, after a lag | Gross margin, moved with the main input's price |
| Factory costs | Staff and depreciation. A new plant loses money until it fills | Costs grown with capacity |
| Tariffs and freight | Where each product is made against where it is sold | Their past share of revenue |
| Research and overheads | Engineers and selling costs | Their share of revenue |
| Capex | Plants built to serve models already won, at home and near customers abroad | The guided figure. With none, announced projects over their build years, plus other capex at its recent level |
| Receivables | What car makers owe and how long they take to pay | Receivable days |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by product and by region, the share of sales to the largest
  customers, awards won in the period, and plants in service and under construction.
  Some give value per vehicle. Few give volume by customer.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Vehicle production | Monthly output by maker (national manufacturers' associations). The customers' own monthly sales. Forecasts by model from data firms (S&P Global Mobility, MarkLines) |
| Raw materials | Prices of steel, aluminium, rubber, plastics and copper |
| Trade | Customs exports by product. Tariff and anti-dumping rulings |
| Replacement market | Cars in use and their age |

- **What leads revenue.** The customers' monthly sales and production plans. Launch dates
  of models the company supplies. Raw material prices, which reach the margin a quarter
  or two later. Tariff decisions. Freight rates for exporters.
- **Backtest.** Customers' production times value per vehicle against revenue from car
  makers.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are more than half the cost. The price falls every year by contract, so the
  supplier has to find the same saving in purchasing and in the factory just to hold its
  margin.
- When raw materials rise, only part of the rise is recovered, and late.
- Factory costs are fixed, so the margin moves with the customers' production.
- A product with few rivals, or one the supplier designs itself, keeps its price better
  than a part made to the customer's drawing.
- Replacement parts earn more than parts sold to car makers.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A plant is built before the model it serves is launched, and near the customer.
  Plants abroad are often built to get behind a tariff wall.
- Car makers pay slowly, and in some markets with bills that take months to become
  cash. A weak car maker may not pay at all.
- Tooling is paid for by the supplier and recovered from the customer over the life of
  the model, or at the start.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Vehicle production swings more than vehicle sales, because makers and dealers build
  and cut stock.
- Customers rise and fall. A supplier tied to a maker that is losing share loses with
  it.
- The move to electric cars adds value per vehicle for some parts and ends others.
- Traps:
  - awards read as revenue already secured;
  - one customer, or one successful model, behind most of the growth;
  - the yearly price cut left out of the forecast;
  - a margin lifted by cheap raw materials, read as lasting;
  - a plant abroad counted at full output in its first year;
  - receivables from a car maker in trouble;
  - a tariff that changes which plant can serve which market.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether value per vehicle keeps rising faster than prices fall.
- How much depends on a few customers.
- What plants abroad earn, and how exposed they are to tariffs.
- Whether new products outside cars are real.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A supplier's customers build 1,000 cars with 5 of its parts on each: revenue is 5,000.

Next year production grows 3%. The contract price falls 2%. A new product adds 0.5 a car
on 40% of the cars, which is 0.2 across all of them. Value per vehicle is 4.9 plus 0.2,
which is 5.1. Revenue is 1,030 times 5.1, or 5,253, up 5%: 3 points from production, 2
points lost to price and 4 gained from the new product.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
