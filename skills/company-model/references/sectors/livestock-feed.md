# Livestock farming and feed

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies produce food at the start of the chain.

- **Hog farmers** sell pigs for slaughter, by weight, at the market price of the day.
  Some also sell piglets.
- **Poultry farmers** sell live chickens and chicks. Producers that process the birds
  and sell meat are on the [poultry and meat processing card](meat-processing.md).
- **Feed makers** sell feed to farmers. The price follows the cost of grain, and the
  maker keeps a margin per tonne.

People eat about the same amount of meat each year. The price is set by supply, and
supply moves in a cycle. For pigs, the number of breeding sows decides how many pigs
reach the market about ten months later. When prices are high, farmers add sows; ten
months on there are too many pigs and prices fall; farmers cut sows; and prices rise
again. Disease can remove a large part of the herd in months. Chickens have the same
kind of cycle, set by the breeding flocks.

A farmer is a price taker. What it controls is how many animals it sells and what each
costs to raise. Feed is most of that cost.

Some farmers own their farms. Others supply piglets or chicks and feed to contract
farmers and pay a fee for each animal raised, which needs less capital for each animal.

Numbers that get confused:

- Animals **sold for slaughter**, **piglets** sold, and **breeding stock** sold.
- **Head** sold against **weight** sold.
- The **market price** against what the company realised, which differs by region and
  weight.
- **Full cost** per kilogram against cash cost, and a reported cost that already
  includes write-downs.
- Cost quoted per **jin**, which is half a kilogram, against prices per kilogram.
- **Breeding sows** in the herd against sows actually producing.
- Profit **before** and **after** changes in the value of animals on the balance sheet.

Signs the card does not fit: the company sells branded packaged meat or food (see the
[packaged food and drink card](packaged-food-drink.md)); it sells seed (see the
[seeds card](seeds.md)); it makes fertiliser or crop chemicals (see the
[commodity chemicals card](commodity-chemicals.md)); or it trades and processes crops at
a spread (see the [trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)).

forecast.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese hog and chicken farmers publish sales every month: head sold,
  average price and revenue. On calls they give cost per kilogram and its target.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Herd | The monthly count of breeding sows from the agriculture ministry, and private surveys of sows, slaughter weights and frozen stock (Yongyi, Mysteel) |
| Prices | Daily and weekly prices of hogs, piglets, chicks and chicken. Hog futures |
| Feed | Prices of maize and soybean meal, spot and futures |
| Policy | The target range for the national sow herd, and state buying and selling of frozen pork |
| Poultry | For white-feathered broilers, breeding birds imported and in stock, from the industry association. For local yellow-feathered breeds, the breeding flocks and chick prices |
| Abroad | Quarterly herd reports and monthly supply and demand estimates (USDA) |

- **What leads profit.** The sow herd leads supply by about ten months. Piglet prices
  lead hog prices. Feed prices lead cost by a quarter. Rising slaughter weights mean
  farmers are holding pigs back, which is supply still to come.
- **Backtest.** The monthly sales announcements against revenue. The sow herd of ten
  months ago against animals sold.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Cost per kilogram changes slowly and the price changes fast, so profit swings from
  large to negative within a year.
- Feed is most of the cost. The amount of feed needed for each kilogram gained, and the
  number of piglets each sow rears, separate a low-cost farmer from the rest.
- A low-cost farmer makes money for most of the cycle. A high-cost one makes money only
  at the top.
- A feed maker's margin per tonne is steady. Its volume follows its customers' herds.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Building farms and herds takes a great deal of capital, mostly borrowed in the good
  years.
- At the bottom of a cycle a farmer loses cash on every animal. Those with the most debt
  have to sell sows, which is how the cycle turns.
- Animals on the balance sheet are marked down when prices fall.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The hog cycle runs three to four years. Governments try to damp it by steering the
  size of the sow herd and by buying and selling frozen pork.
- Disease can change supply faster than the breeding cycle.
- Traps:
  - a profit per animal at the peak multiplied by planned volume;
  - volume targets that ignore the price;
  - a cost figure that leaves out overheads and interest;
  - piglet sales mixed with hog sales;
  - gains on the value of animals inside profit;
  - grants and insurance payouts;
  - debt that falls due at the bottom of the cycle.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where the cycle stands.
- Who has the lowest cost, and how much lower it can go.
- Whether policy holds the herd down.
- Whether a farmer that has stopped building starts to pay out cash.

Yardstick: P/B, with market value per animal sold beside it, read against the cycle
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A farmer has 50,000 sows. Each rears 24 piglets a year and 90% survive: about 1.08m pigs
reach the market ten months later.

This year it sells 1m pigs, from last year's smaller herd, at 120 kilograms and a price
of 16: revenue is 1,920m. Cost is 14 a kilogram, 1,680m in all. Profit is 240m, or 240 a
pig.

If the price falls to 14, profit is nil. If it rises to 18, profit doubles to 480m.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
