# Oil and gas producers

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A producer finds oil and gas, develops the fields and sells what comes out: crude oil,
natural gas and the liquids that come with gas. Fields are often shared with partners
and with the host government.

- **Pure producers** do only this, onshore, offshore or in shale.
- **Integrated companies** also refine oil, make chemicals, run filling stations and
  sell gas. Build each division apart, and take out what one division sells to another.
  Refining and chemicals go on the [commodity chemicals card](commodity-chemicals.md),
  and pipelines and gas terminals on the [pipelines card](pipelines-lng.md). Fuel
  marketing is the volume sold times a margin per unit, which the government may set. A
  gas sales division resells the company's own gas and gas it imports. Where it imports
  on oil-linked contracts and sells at regulated prices, it loses money when oil is high.

The company is a price taker. The oil price is set by world demand, by what the producer
countries agree to pump, and by how fast shale output responds. Gas is priced region by
region: at a trading hub, by a contract linked to oil, or by a government. What the
company decides is how much to drill and where.

Numbers that get confused:

- Output from the fields the company **takes part in**, against its **own share** after
  partners, royalties and the government's part. Under a production-sharing contract the
  company's share falls as the price rises.
- **Barrels of oil equivalent**, which add gas to oil by energy content. A barrel of gas
  on that measure usually sells for much less than a barrel of oil.
- The **benchmark** price against the price **realised**, after the discount for quality
  and location and after hedges.
- **Operating cost** per barrel against **all-in cost**, which adds depreciation and
  taxes on production. Companies define both differently.
- **Proved reserves** against wider estimates of what is in the ground.
- **Units**: barrels against tonnes for oil, cubic feet against cubic metres for gas,
  and dollars against the home currency. Convert before comparing.

Signs the card does not fit: the company only refines or sells fuel; it sells services
to producers (see the [oil services card](oil-services.md)); or it owns only royalty
rights, which have no costs or capex, so volume and price are the whole build.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Quarterly: production by region and product, realised prices and unit
  costs. Yearly: reserves, and a plan with the production target and capex budget. Shale
  producers add wells drilled and completed and their hedges.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Prices | Benchmark crude prices (Brent, WTI, Dubai) and the discounts for other grades. Gas prices at the main hubs and, where regulated, the official price |
| Supply and demand | Monthly reports from the producer group (OPEC) and the International Energy Agency. Weekly US output and stocks (EIA). National output and imports from statistics offices and customs |
| Activity | Rigs at work, weekly (Baker Hughes) |
| Rules | Notices on windfall levies, royalties and gas prices |

- **What leads profit.** The oil price reaches the producing division's profit in the
  same quarter. Refining and gas imports follow later, through stocks and contracts.
  Rigs at work lead shale output by about half a year. Project start dates. Meetings of
  the producer countries. Service costs follow the oil price with a lag.
- **Backtest.** The company's share of volume times the realised price against the
  producing division's revenue. The realised price against the benchmark.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Most costs do not move with the price, so profit per barrel moves by more than the
  price does.
- Taxes that rise with the price take part of a gain and give back part of a fall.
- Depreciation is the largest cost that is not cash. Cash cost and all-in cost answer
  different questions: whether a field keeps running, and whether it was worth building.
- A company whose output is mostly gas earns less per barrel of oil equivalent than one
  that is mostly oil.
- Costs are often in local currency and revenue in dollars, so a weak home currency
  lowers cost per barrel.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Fields decline. A producer has to spend each year just to hold output level, and has to
  replace the reserves it produces.
- Offshore and other large projects take years from decision to first oil. Shale wells
  take months, and decline fast.
- Closing a field costs money at the end of its life, with nothing coming in.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The oil price moves in long cycles and in sudden shocks. Forecast with a stated price,
  and show the result at others.
- Governments take more when prices are high, and state-owned producers may be told to
  raise output whatever the return.
- Over the long run, demand for oil may peak. Treat the timing as a scenario.
- Traps:
  - barrels of oil equivalent that hide a gas-heavy mix;
  - output of the whole field read as the company's share;
  - growth bought with acquisitions;
  - hedges made at old prices that run out;
  - reserves added because the price assumed went up;
  - capex cut today, which is output lost in later years;
  - a high price assumed to last for ever;
  - gains on fields sold inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where the oil price goes.
- How long the reserves last and what it costs to replace them.
- Whether growth in output is worth more than paying the cash out.
- What the payout is when prices are low.
- For state producers, whether output targets come before returns.

Yardstick: for integrated companies, P/E, dividend yield and free cash flow yield at a
stated oil price; for pure producers, price to net asset value and EV/EBITDA
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A producer's fields give 100 this year. They decline 10% and new wells add 15, so next
year they give 105.

The benchmark is 70 and the discount 4. It has sold 30% ahead at a benchmark of 60. It
realises 66 on 70% and 56 on 30%, an average of 63. Operating cost is 12 a barrel,
production taxes 6 and depreciation 15, so profit is 30 a barrel.

If the benchmark falls to 60, the realised price is 56, down 11%. Production taxes fall
to 5. Profit is 24 a barrel, down 20%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
