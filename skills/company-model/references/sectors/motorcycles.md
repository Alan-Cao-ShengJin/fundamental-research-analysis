# Motorcycles, powersports and small engines

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- **Commuter motorcycles and scooters**: cheap, sold in volume, mostly in developing
  countries. Buyers need transport, so demand follows incomes, fuel prices and credit.
- **Leisure motorcycles** of larger engine size: fewer units at several times the price.
  Buyers want them, so demand follows spare income, new models and rules on where bikes
  may be ridden.
- **Off-road and utility vehicles**, sold mainly in North America and Europe through
  dealers who hold stock.
- **Small engines and outdoor power equipment** such as mowers and generators, sold to
  other manufacturers and to large retailers.
- **Manufacturing for other brands**, under contract.

Most makers sell through distributors and dealers at home and abroad. Some own their
distribution in a market and carry the stock themselves. Where a lender finances the
dealers' stock, the maker is paid at once but usually promises to buy stock back if a
dealer fails.

Three pairs of numbers get confused. **Shipments** to dealers against **registrations**
by riders. **Units** against **value**, since commuter bikes dominate the count and large
bikes the revenue. **Own-brand** sales against **contract** production for others.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes mainly electric bicycles or kick
scooters; it makes parts only; it is a car maker with a small motorcycle arm; or a boat,
finance or other unit is more than about 15% of revenue or profit, in which case that
unit gets its own build.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each product line and market separately, with history beside the forecast. For
off-road vehicles the classes are the vehicle types, not engine sizes.

There are two routes. Where units are published, build from industry units and share.
Where the maker publishes neither units nor prices, as many off-road makers do, build
each segment's sales in money, as last year's sales moved by four things, each on its
own row and added together: the change in retail sales in units, where the company gives
it; the change in dealer stock, since shipments are retail sales plus or minus it; price
and mix, net of promotions; and currency. Restate history for any business sold.

A stated change in dealer stock has to be turned into points of sales. Estimate the
stock, from the days of sales the company quotes or from the dealer borrowing it
discloses, apply the percentage change, and divide by sales. The retail figure usually
covers one region and one product group. Build the rest, such as parts, other regions
and sales to businesses, on their own growth.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry sales | Units by engine size class, at home and exported, on one stated basis | Total units |
| Company share | Share of each size class, at home and in exports, giving units | Unit growth by line |
| New models | Launch dates and the size class each model enters | Leave it out |
| Contract production | Units agreed with each brand customer, from the customer's own plans | Growth from the same period a year earlier |
| Dealer stock | Shipments less registrations, in units where both are published | The company's own statement of the change in dealer stock, in per cent or days of sales |
| Revenue per unit | By line and size class, with price in the currency of sale, mix and the exchange rate apart. Promotions paid to dealers and buyers come off revenue | Line revenue divided by units, or the company's own statement of the change in price |
| Vehicle revenue | Units times revenue per unit, by line | Revenue by line |
| Parts and accessories | Vehicles in use, from sales in earlier years, times spend per vehicle | Its own past growth |
| Engines and power equipment | Units to each customer or channel times price, with retailers' stock in mind | Revenue growth |
| Margin by line | Scale, mix, materials, freight, tariffs and currency | Reported margin by line |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure varies. Some makers publish monthly dispatches. Others give
  units and revenue by product once or twice a year, with the export share and the main
  contract customers. Makers selling through stock-holding dealers often report dealer
  stock and retail sales.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly production, sales and exports by maker and engine size from the motorcycle chamber of commerce (CCCM), relayed by trade sites. Customs exports by destination |
| India and South-east Asia | Monthly dispatches from the manufacturers' association and registrations from the vehicle registry |
| Europe and North America | Registrations from the industry associations, some of which are not public. Makers' own estimates of industry retail sales in their annual reports. Makers' reports of dealer stock and retail sales. Dealer surveys. Large retailers' comments on outdoor equipment |

- **What leads revenue.** Monthly sales and exports by size class. Dealer stock. Retailers'
  orders, placed a season ahead. Model launches. Tariff and emission-rule dates. Exchange
  rates. Storm seasons for generators.
- **Backtest.** Industry sales times share against the company's units. Units times
  revenue per unit against line revenue. On the second route, the four factors against
  the company's own explanation of its sales change.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost: aluminium, steel and bought-in parts. Margin improves
  with scale.
- Large bikes and own-brand sales earn more than commuter bikes and contract work, so mix
  moves the margin more than volume does.
- An exporter bills in foreign currency and pays costs at home, so the exchange rate moves
  both revenue and margin, and leaves gains or losses on money owed by customers. A maker
  that sells at home and imports parts has the opposite exposure, on its costs.
- Tariffs fall on finished vehicles for an exporter and on imported parts for a maker
  that assembles at home. They are shared between the maker, the importer and the buyer,
  and can later be refunded. Check who is bearing them.
- Building a brand abroad means dealers, marketing and warranty, which arrive before the
  sales.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Capex is moderate. Plants abroad are built to get round tariffs.
- A maker that owns its distribution abroad carries the stock, and the risk of clearing
  it.
- Receivables from distributors lengthen when a market weakens. Where a lender finances
  dealers, the risk shows instead as a promise to buy back unsold stock.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Leisure and off-road demand follows consumer credit and confidence, and dealers'
  stock swings amplify it.
- City riding bans, licence limits and new emission rules can open or close a market.
- A tariff or an anti-dumping duty can reset an export market within a year.
- Outdoor equipment follows housing and the weather. Seasonal vehicles are ordered by
  dealers before the season, so one quarter carries most of the year.
- Traps:
  - units read as value;
  - shipments read as riders' demand;
  - contract production counted as brand strength;
  - export revenue growth that is the exchange rate;
  - one contract customer carrying a line;
  - the group's figures against the listed company's.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the home market for large bikes is maturing.
- Whether exports keep growing under tariffs.
- Whether the company is becoming a brand or remains a contract maker.
- Whether the margin holds against currency and tariffs.

Yardstick: P/E, on normal earnings or with EV/EBITDA beside it when the year is a loss
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker sells 100 commuter bikes at 5 and 20 large bikes at 30. Revenue is 500 + 600 =
1,100. Margins of 10% and 25% give gross profit of 50 + 150 = 200.

Next year it sells 90 commuter bikes and 30 large ones. Units are unchanged at 120.
Revenue is 450 + 900 = 1,350, up 23%, and gross profit is 45 + 225 = 270, up 35%.

On the second route, a segment sold 1,000 last year. Retail sales fall 3%, dealers cut
their stock by an amount equal to 5% of sales, and price and mix add 2%. Sales fall about
6%, twice the fall in what riders bought.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
