# Consumer finance, leasing and other non-bank lenders

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies lend without taking deposits. They fund themselves with bank loans, bonds
and by selling packages of their loans. They earn in three ways.

- **Lending from their own balance sheet** to consumers and small businesses: instalment
  loans, cash loans, car loans, pay-later credit. They earn the rate charged less their
  cost of funds and their loan losses.
- **Arranging loans that banks fund.** The company finds and scores the borrower and
  collects the payments, for a fee. It may or may not guarantee the loan.
- **Leasing** equipment, vehicles, aircraft or ships, mostly to companies and public
  bodies. A finance lease is a loan in all but name. In an operating lease the company
  keeps the asset and its value at the end. Renting equipment out for short periods with
  operators and services is an operating business, not a lease.

Borrowers decide demand. For consumers, the ability to repay follows jobs and incomes.
For companies and public bodies, it follows their investment plans and their own
finances. Lenders to the company decide how much it can grow and at what cost.
Regulators set the highest rate that may be charged, who may lend, and how much the
company may borrow against its capital. Any of these can change the business in a year.

Four numbers get confused. Loans **originated** in the period against the balance
**outstanding**. Loans **on** the balance sheet against loans arranged for others but
still **guaranteed**. The **rate charged** against the **spread** kept after funding.
Loans **overdue** by a number of days against loans **written off**.

Signs the card does not fit: the lender takes deposits (see the [banks card](banks.md));
it processes payments and lends only on the side; or it is a pawnbroker or a
micro-lender too small for this detail. A lending group may also own operating
businesses, such as equipment rental or hospitals. Build each of those on its own card,
or under [when no card fits](index.md#when-no-card-fits), and value the group as a sum
of the parts.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each product separately, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loans originated | New and repeat borrowers times average loan size | Origination growth |
| Balance outstanding | Opening balance, plus loans originated, less repayments over the loans' life, less write-offs. On the balance sheet and arranged for others apart | Balance growth |
| Rate charged | By product, under the legal cap where one applies, and repricing with the benchmark lending rate where loans float | Interest income over average balance |
| Cost of funds | By source and currency, times the mix | Interest cost over average borrowing |
| Net interest income | Average own balance times the rate charged less the cost of funds | Its past ratio to the balance |
| Fees | For loans arranged for others: loans arranged times the fee rate, less the cost of any guarantee given. For the lender's own borrowers: service fees as a share of new lending | Fee revenue over new lending, from history |
| Lease income | Finance leases as loans. Operating leases as assets times the rental rate times the share on lease, less depreciation, with the value at the end of each lease | Lease income over average lease assets |
| Loan losses | By the period loans were made in: each period's lending times its expected loss, as overdue loans become write-offs, less recoveries | Losses as a share of average balance |
| Operating costs | The cost of finding each borrower, servicing, collection and technology | Costs as a share of the balance |
| Capital and funding | Borrowing against capital, the limit the rules set on it, and when each source of funding falls due | As reported |
| Profit for ordinary shareholders | Net profit, less minority interests in listed subsidiaries, less payments on perpetual securities | |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Some report quarterly and others twice a year: loans originated, the
  balance by type of borrower, the share arranged without bearing risk, the yield and
  cost of funds, overdue rates at set numbers of days, and provisions. Many show
  how each period's loans have performed over time. Reports on packaged loans sold to
  investors give monthly repayment and default figures.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Credit | The central bank's figures on consumer credit. Banks' card and consumer loan arrears |
| Borrowers | Unemployment and income data. Used-car and equipment prices, for secured lending and leasing |
| Funding | Bond yields and the spreads paid on packaged loans |
| Rules | Rate caps, licensing, limits on borrowing, and rules on arranging loans for banks |

- **What leads profit.** Loans that miss their first payment. Early overdue rates.
  Funding spreads. Regulatory notices. Unemployment.
- **Backtest.** Average balance times the spread against net interest income. Each
  period's lending times its loss rate against write-offs a year or more later.

## 4. Costs and margin

- What is left for shareholders is the rate charged, less the cost of funds, less loan
  losses, less running costs. Each is a large number, so a small change in any one is a
  large change in profit.
- Losses arrive months after a loan is made. A lender growing fast shows low loss ratios
  because most of its loans are new.
- Funding costs rise, and funding can vanish, just when losses rise.
- A rate cap cuts the rate charged at once, and forces the lender towards safer borrowers.
- The cost of finding a borrower is paid at the start. A repeat borrower costs far less.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Growth is limited by capital and by the borrowing allowed against it.
- Loans last longer than much of the funding behind them, so funding must be renewed.
- Guarantees on loans arranged for others are liabilities, whether or not they are on the
  balance sheet.
- In an operating lease, the asset's value at the end is an estimate that the used market
  can prove wrong.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- The credit cycle: losses are low while jobs are plentiful and money is easy, then rise
  together across lenders.
- Rules change abruptly: caps on rates, limits on arranging loans for banks, and
  licensing.
- Traps:
  - a low overdue rate that is the result of fast growth;
  - loans arranged for others described as free of risk when they are guaranteed;
  - revenue booked at the start on loans that will be collected over years;
  - loans extended or restructured so that they are not counted as overdue;
  - provisions compared with overdue loans and not with expected losses;
  - fee rates defined differently from company to company.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How the loans will perform in a downturn.
- What regulators will do next.
- Whether funding will stay available.
- Whether the return on equity lasts once growth slows.

Yardstick: P/B against return on equity, with P/E beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A lender has loans of 100, funded by borrowing of 80 and capital of 20. It charges 18%
and pays 6% on what it borrows, so net interest income is 18 less 4.8, which is 13.2.
Loan losses are 6 and running costs 4. Profit before tax is 3.2, a 16% return on capital.

If losses rise by 2 points of the balance, profit falls to 1.2, down 62%.

A lender that doubles its lending each year, with losses arriving a year after each loan
is made, reports about half the loss rate its loans will finally show.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
