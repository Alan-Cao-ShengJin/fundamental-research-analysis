# Batteries

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- **EV batteries** (cells, modules and packs) go to carmakers under supply agreements
  tied to particular vehicle models. Demand is the customers' EV output, times the
  battery in each vehicle, times the maker's share of each model. The carmaker decides:
  which supplier, which chemistry, whether to add a second supplier, whether to make its
  own cells.
- **Storage batteries** (cells and complete systems) go to system integrators, project
  developers and utilities, project by project. Demand follows grid and data-centre
  storage installations, which follow power-market rules, tenders and subsidies.
- **Other lines**: battery materials and recycling, stakes in mines, technology licences
  and royalties, and small cells for tools and electronics.
- **Where it sells.** A maker can sell at home, export, and build plants abroad. Tariffs,
  local-content rules and tax credits differ by market and decide which makers can sell
  there.

A battery is counted three times, and the three numbers differ. **Production** is what
the plants make. **Shipments**, or sales volume, are what leaves for customers, and are
when revenue is booked. **Installations** are the batteries in vehicles registered in the
period, counted by outside trackers. Shipments also include exports, storage batteries
and stock building at customers, so they are usually the largest of the three in a
growing market.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes battery materials or equipment and no
cells; it assembles packs or storage systems from bought-in cells; it is a carmaker's
battery arm selling mostly inside its group; or materials, mining or licences earn more
than about 15% of profit (of revenue, where profit by line is not disclosed), in which
case that unit gets its own build.

Classify a cell by its use, not its shape: any cell sold for vehicles belongs in the EV
rows.

## 2. The driver tree

Each row is a row, or a block of rows, in the revenue build, with history beside the
forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EV output | Customers' or the market's EV production, by market, battery-electric and plug-in hybrid separately | Market totals |
| Battery per vehicle | kWh per vehicle, for each type | Installations divided by vehicles |
| EV battery demand | Vehicles times kWh, by market. This is the market's installations | The trackers' installation totals |
| Company share | By customer where a few carmakers are most of the volume: the customer's output times the maker's share of its supply. By market and chemistry where the maker sells to many | Share by market from the trackers |
| EV shipments | Company installations, plus exports the tracker misses, plus the change in stock at customers | The past ratio of shipments to installations, labelled as such |
| Storage shipments | Named contracts and backlog with delivery dates, or market shipments times share. A backlog can be cancelled, so test how firm it is | Growth from the same period a year earlier |
| Price per Wh | EV and storage separately, and home against overseas where disclosed. It moves with materials costs through the price formula, with a lag | Segment revenue divided by volume |
| Battery revenue | Shipments times price, by line | |
| Profit per Wh | Price less materials, less processing cost and depreciation per Wh | Segment gross profit divided by volume |
| Capacity | By plant, with start dates and ramp. Shipments cannot exceed what the plants can make. Lines converted from EV to storage count where they are now | The company's total capacity and utilisation |
| Other lines | Materials and recycling, mining, licences: each on its own row | One row |

Forecast profit per Wh, and let the margin percentage follow. Most supply contracts set
the price by a formula: materials at published prices averaged over an earlier period,
plus a processing fee. The formula and its lag are seldom disclosed, so estimate the lag
from how the price per Wh has followed metal prices in the past. The margin percentage
then moves with metal prices even when the business has not changed.

Some makers disclose neither volume nor revenue by line. Estimate volume from the
installation trackers, from capacity times utilisation, or from production tax credits
received divided by the credit per kWh. Price per Wh is then revenue divided by that
volume, checked against cell price indices. Label both as estimates.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure varies widely. Some makers give sales volume for EV and storage
  batteries, segment revenue and margin, capacity in GWh, capacity under construction and
  revenue at home and abroad. Others give only group revenue and profit, with capacity
  and utilisation in money terms. Quarterly volumes often come only from the results
  call. Some makers report profit with and without production tax credits.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Monthly production, sales, installations and exports by maker and chemistry from the battery industry alliance (CABIA), relayed by trade sites. EV production and registrations |
| Global | Monthly EV battery installations by maker from research trackers (SNE Research is the usual one). Storage shipment rankings from several trackers, whose scopes differ and do not reconcile |
| Europe and North America | EV registrations by model, the makers' own disclosures and announced plants |

- **Input prices.** Lithium carbonate and hydroxide, nickel, cobalt, cathode materials
  and cell price indices.
- **What leads revenue.** Customers' EV production plans and model launches. Materials
  prices, which lead the battery price by the lag in the formula. Storage tender awards
  and order announcements. Dates when incentives, tariffs or credits start and end.
- **Backtest.** Installations times share against reported sales volume, with the gap
  explained by exports, storage and stock. Volume times price against segment revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost: cathode (lithium with nickel and cobalt, or with iron
  phosphate), anode, electrolyte, separator and foils.
- Because the price follows materials costs, revenue per Wh follows metal prices while
  profit per Wh is steadier. A higher margin percentage after metals fall is not pricing
  power.
- When metals fall quickly, stock bought at the old price is sold at the new one, and
  inventory is written down. When they rise, the reverse helps.
- Depreciation is heavy, so cost per Wh depends on how full the plants are. A new plant
  starts with low yield.
- Overseas plants cost more to run. Tax credits or grants per kWh can be a large part of
  reported profit, so show profit with and without them. Credits earned in a joint
  venture are shared with the partner, through minority interests where the venture is
  consolidated.
- Where plants and customers are abroad, revenue and cost per Wh in the reporting
  currency move with exchange rates.
- Warranties run for years and customer rebates are accrued. Additions to and releases
  from these provisions move profit.
- A storage system carries bought-in equipment that a cell does not, so compare storage
  and EV profit per Wh as well as by margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A plant takes one to two years to build, and costs more and takes longer abroad. Count
  the lines that are producing, not the capacity announced.
- Many makers carry large supplier payables and customer prepayments. Check how much of
  the cash balance they explain, because a fall in volume reverses them.
- Customers may commit to minimum volumes. Compensation paid when they buy less can
  appear as revenue or other income, and is not repeatable.
- Stakes in mines and materials makers bring equity income, and impairments when metal
  prices fall.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Capacity is built ahead of demand. When it overshoots, prices fall until weaker makers
  stop adding.
- The metal price cycle moves revenue and inventory results without changing volume.
- Policy sets much of the demand: EV purchase incentives and their end dates, tariffs,
  local-content rules, tax credits, export controls, and the rules that pay for storage.
  An incentive that ends pulls purchases forward and leaves a gap.
- Chemistry and cell design change within a few years and move share between makers.
- Customers add second suppliers and build their own cells. A few customers can be most
  of the volume.
- Traps:
  - production, shipments and installations mixed in one series;
  - totals that include storage against EV-only figures;
  - revenue falling while volume rises, which is the price formula at work;
  - the margin percentage read as pricing power;
  - tax credits and grants inside profit;
  - provisions for rebates and warranties released into profit;
  - joint-venture volumes that are not consolidated;
  - announced capacity counted as operating;
  - one-off compensation from customers.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether overcapacity ends, and whether profit per Wh holds.
- Whether the leader keeps its share and its premium as rivals and carmakers' own cells
  catch up.
- Whether overseas plants and licences grow despite tariffs and local-content rules.
- Whether storage demand from grids and data centres lasts, and at what profit.
- Whether a new chemistry changes who leads.

Yardstick: forward P/E, read with growth in a growth phase, and P/B in a downturn. Where
earnings are thin or rest on tax credits, P/E misleads and investors use EV/EBITDA or
P/B ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

Customers build 10 million EVs with 50 kWh each, so the market installs 500 GWh. At a 40%
share the company's batteries are in 200 GWh of them. Customers add 10 GWh of stock, so
EV shipments are 210 GWh. Storage shipments are 60 GWh, making 270 GWh in total.

At a price of 0.60 per Wh, revenue is 162bn. Materials cost 0.42 and processing with
depreciation 0.08, so profit is 0.10 per Wh: 27bn, a margin of 16.7%.

Metals then fall. Materials cost 0.32 and the formula takes the price to 0.50. Revenue
falls 17% to 135bn, profit per Wh is unchanged, gross profit is still 27bn and the margin
is 20%. Holding the old 16.7% margin on the lower revenue would have cut gross profit by
4.5bn for no reason.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
