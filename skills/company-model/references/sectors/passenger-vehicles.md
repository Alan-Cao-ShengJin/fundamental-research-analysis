# Passenger vehicles

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A car maker designs and builds cars and sells them under its own brands, through dealers
or its own shops. Around the cars it may have other units:

- **Joint ventures** with foreign brands, common in China, which appear as a share of
  profit and not as revenue.
- **A finance arm** that lends to buyers and dealers and leases cars. Build it on the
  [non-bank lenders card](non-bank-lenders.md), with the value of leased cars when they
  come back.
- **Parts, batteries or chips** made in-house and sold to other makers. Batteries go on
  the [batteries card](batteries.md).
- **Parts and service** for cars already sold, software, and emission credits sold to
  other makers.

Households decide demand. They follow incomes, confidence and the cost of credit, and
they replace cars on a cycle of years. Governments move the timing with purchase taxes,
trade-in subsidies and limits on registrations. Two shifts run through everything: from
petrol to electric and plug-in cars, and in China from foreign brands to local ones.
Makers fight for share with price cuts, and exports face tariffs.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Wholesale**, which is cars sold to dealers. **Retail**, which is cars sold to
  buyers. **Registrations**, which is cars insured or licensed. The gap between the
  first two is the change in dealers' stock.
- A group's **total** sales, which count joint ventures, against the sales of the brands
  it **consolidates**.
- **Revenue per car**, which is after the dealer's margin, discounts and tax, against the
  sticker price.
- **Orders**, which may carry a refundable deposit, against deliveries.
- **Gross margin** against **profit per car**.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes heavy trucks or buses (see the
[trucks card](trucks.md) and the [buses card](buses.md)); it supplies parts (see the
[auto parts card](auto-parts.md)); it is a dealer group; it makes a few thousand luxury
cars a year, where the order book and price matter more than the market; or it has not
yet reached volume, where the cash it has left matters most.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each brand and market separately, or each region where that is how the company
reports, with history beside the forecast. Many Western makers publish each quarter how
profit moved with volume, mix, price and cost. Build their forecast in the same four
steps.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese makers publish sales every month, by brand, with electric cars and
  exports shown apart. Results give revenue, margin and often profit per car. Newer
  electric makers report deliveries and a vehicle margin each quarter. Most set a yearly
  sales target and list the models due.
- **Industry**, which differs by market:

| Market | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| China | Weekly and monthly retail and wholesale sales by maker, engine type and exports (China Passenger Car Association). Monthly sales (China Association of Automobile Manufacturers). Weekly insurance registrations by brand. A monthly index of dealers' stock (China Automobile Dealers Association). Prices of battery materials. Customs exports by country |
| United States | Monthly or quarterly sales by maker, the yearly selling rate, days of stock at dealers and discounts per car (Wards, Cox Automotive) |
| Europe and Japan | Monthly registrations by maker and by country (ACEA, national agencies) |

- **What leads profit.** Weekly registrations. Orders for a new model. Dealers' stock.
  Discounts. Battery material prices, which reach cost after some months. The dates when
  tax breaks and subsidies end. Tariff decisions.
- **Backtest.** The monthly sales announcements against the units in the results. Units
  times revenue per car against vehicle revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Materials are most of the cost of a car. For an electric car the battery is the
  largest single part.
- Factories, tooling and research are fixed. Each car carries less of them as volume
  rises, and below a certain volume the maker loses money on every car.
- A price cut takes effect at once. Savings from suppliers arrive over the following
  quarters.
- A maker that builds its own batteries and parts keeps the supplier's margin and
  carries the supplier's fixed costs.
- Warranty and recalls are provisions, and they can be raised years after the sale.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Many makers are funded by their suppliers: they are paid for cars before they pay for
  parts. When sales fall, that cash flows out.
- Where research is added to the balance sheet, it lifts profit now and is written off
  over later years.
- An electric-car programme that is cut back brings write-downs on plants and supplier
  contracts.
- A new plant abroad takes two or three years and loses money while it ramps.
- Dealers carry the stock. When they are losing money, the maker ends up paying through
  rebates.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Car sales follow the economy and credit. A subsidy or tax break pulls sales into one
  year and out of the next.
- In a price war every maker's revenue per car falls, and the weakest stop.
- Tariffs can close an export market in a quarter.
- Traps:
  - wholesale read as retail while dealers' stock builds;
  - the group's units read as the consolidated brands';
  - revenue per car rising with the mix while like-for-like prices fall;
  - research costs moved to the balance sheet;
  - profit that rests on subsidies or on credits sold;
  - supplier payables that work like debt;
  - orders with refundable deposits read as sales;
  - a joint venture whose profit is fading.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the maker holds its share and its profit per car as prices fall.
- How much exports and plants abroad can add, and what tariffs take away.
- Which makers survive.
- What driving software is worth.
- How fast joint-venture profit disappears.

Yardstick: P/E; EV to sales for an electric-car maker still losing money; a sum of the
parts where joint ventures, a finance arm or listed subsidiaries matter
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker sells 1,000 cars at 100: revenue is 100,000. Materials and other costs that move
with volume are 80 a car. Fixed costs are 15,000. Profit is 5,000, or 5 a car.

It cuts prices 3%. Profit falls to 2,000, down 60%. If the cut also lifts volume 10%, to
1,100 cars, profit is 1,100 times 17 less 15,000, which is 3,700. At the new price it
needs 883 cars to break even.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
