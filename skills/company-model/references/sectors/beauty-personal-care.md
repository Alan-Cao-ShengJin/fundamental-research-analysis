# Beauty brands

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies own brands of skincare, cosmetics, fragrance and hair care. They reach
buyers through several channels, each with its own economics.

- **Online shops on the large platforms.** The brand books the retail price, and pays
  the platform a commission and, above all, pays for traffic.
- **Live-streaming hosts**, who take a commission and often a fee for the slot.
- **Own shops, and counters the brand runs itself.** The brand books the retail price and
  pays rent and staff. Where a department store buys the goods, the brand books a
  wholesale price.
- **Distributors, beauty chains and pharmacies.** The brand books a wholesale price.
- **Airport and duty-free shops**, which matter most to expensive brands.

A beauty brand lives on a few hero products that buyers come back for, on new products,
and on what it costs to win a buyer online. Sales bunch around shopping festivals.
Demand follows spare income and fashion, and buyers move between foreign and local
brands.

Chinese brands sell mostly online and are built from sales on the platforms. Global
groups that own expensive brands sell mostly wholesale, to department stores, beauty
chains and duty-free shops, and report by category and region. Build those on the
second table in section 2.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Sales on a platform**, which are before returns and discounts, against **revenue**.
- **Retail sales** against the brand's revenue, where a distributor stands between.
- **Sales to** distributors and duty-free shops against what they **sell on**.
- **Gross margin**, which is high everywhere, against profit after **selling costs**,
  which is what compares across channels.
- Growth at **constant currency and scope** against reported growth.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes products for other brands under
contract; it sells injected products through clinics, which are regulated as drugs or
devices; it is a luxury house with a beauty division (see the
[luxury goods card](luxury-goods.md), and build the division here); or it retails other
companies' brands (see the [retail card](retail.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese companies give revenue by brand and channel twice a year, with
  the online share. Global groups give sales and operating profit by category and by
  region each quarter, with growth at constant currency, and little by brand or channel.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Online sales | Monthly estimates of each brand's sales on the main platforms, from data vendors. They are estimates, are before returns, and cover live-streaming platforms less well |
| Official data | Monthly retail sales of cosmetics |
| Festivals | The platforms' brand rankings during each shopping festival |
| Duty-free | Monthly duty-free sales where published, such as Hainan's and Korea's |
| New products | The regulator's register of new products |
| Peers | Other brands' results and marketing spending |

- **What leads revenue.** Monthly online estimates, most of all in festival months. A
  new product's first weeks. Duty-free sales. Peers' results.
- **Backtest.** Online estimates less returns against online revenue. Marketing as a
  share of revenue against growth, over several years.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gross margin is high. The large cost is selling: traffic, hosts, commissions, rent
  and staff.
- Online, a brand can buy growth with marketing. The return on that spending shows
  whether the brand is strong or only loud.
- Expensive brands earn more for each sale and depend more on travellers.
- Many brands have their products made by others, so plant is small. The large global
  groups make most of their own.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little plant. Stock is the capital, and it ages.
- A brand with a hit collects cash faster than it can spend it.
- Distributors and duty-free shops can hold months of stock.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand follows confidence and fashion. An ingredient or a look can sell for two years
  and then stop.
- Regulators approve new products and police what brands may claim.
- Traps:
  - online sales before returns read as revenue;
  - growth bought with marketing whose return is falling;
  - one product behind most of the profit;
  - a festival that pulls sales forward from the months around it;
  - distributors' and duty-free shops' stock read as demand;
  - growth that was bought with a brand.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the brand holds without heavy marketing.
- Whether a hero product can be widened into a range.
- How the share of local and foreign brands moves.
- What rising traffic costs do to the margin.
- How much growth is left abroad.

Yardstick: P/E, with enterprise value to operating profit beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A brand sells 1,000 online before returns. 30% is returned, so revenue is 700. Gross
margin is 70%, which is 490. Marketing is 40% of revenue, 280. Platform fees are 5% of
revenue, 35, and other costs 75. Profit is 100. If traffic costs 10% more, marketing is
308 and profit 72, down 28%.

One product is 40% of sales. If it falls 20% while the rest grows 10%, revenue falls 2%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
