# Duty-free and travel retail

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Travel retailers sell perfume and cosmetics, liquor, tobacco, luxury goods and food to
travellers, free of duty and tax. They trade in airports, in downtown shops, at borders,
on islands with their own rules such as Hainan, and online for collection.

Few licences are granted. Airport shops are won by tender. The airport takes a share of
sales with a guaranteed minimum, or a fixed rent plus a share of sales.

Revenue is travellers, times the share who buy, times what each buyer spends. It follows
passenger numbers, the travellers' nationality, exchange rates, the gap between the
duty-free price and the price at home, and the allowance each traveller is given. Online
sellers and resellers who buy in bulk compete for the same shoppers.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Travellers** against **shoppers**, and shoppers against what each **spends**.
  Published figures may count visits and not people.
- Duty-free sales in a **place** against the **company's** share of them.
- The airport's **share of sales**, its **guaranteed minimum** and any **fixed rent**.
- Sales to **travellers** against sales to **resellers**, which earn far less.
- **Duty-free** sales against sales of goods on which duty has been paid.

Signs the card does not fit: the company is an ordinary retailer (see the
[retail card](retail.md)); it owns the airport (see the
[transport infrastructure card](transport-infrastructure.md)); or it owns the brands it
sells.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each place and each kind of shop separately, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Travellers | Passengers by airport, or visitors to the island or city, by nationality | Growth in passengers or visitors, as published |
| Share who buy | Shoppers over travellers, counted the same way each year: visitors to an island, departing passengers at an airport | Its past ratio |
| Spend per buyer | Items times price. It follows the allowance, the discount offered, the mix of goods and the gap to prices at home | Sales over shoppers, from the published figures |
| Sales by place | Travellers times the share who buy times spend, with new shops by opening date | Sales moved with traveller numbers |
| Company share | The company's share of each place's duty-free sales, against other licence holders. Where it is not published, the subsidiary's revenue over the place's published sales. At an airport, the shops the company holds | Hold the latest share |
| Online and resellers | Orders placed online, and bulk sales to resellers at a lower margin | As the company describes |
| Revenue | Sales by place times the company's share, plus goods sold with duty paid | Revenue growth as reported |
| Gross margin | The mix of goods, the depth of discounts, suppliers' terms, and the currency goods are bought in against the currency they are sold in | Its recent level, moved with discounts and currency |
| Airport fees | For each contract: the share of sales and the guaranteed minimum, or the fixed rent plus a share, and the date it is re-tendered. The airport company's own announcements give the terms. A contract lost at tender takes its sales with it | Their past share of airport sales |
| Shop costs | Staff, delivery, and rent or depreciation for downtown shops | Their share of revenue |
| Partners | The share of each subsidiary's profit that belongs to an airport or a local partner | As reported |
| Stock | Stock in days, bought months ahead, and goods written down as they age | As reported |
| Capex | New shopping complexes and shops | The guided figure. With none, its recent share of sales |
| Interest | Interest earned on net cash | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue and margin each quarter. Results by subsidiary, which stand for
  places, once a year. Few give travellers, shoppers or spend for their own shops.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Hainan | Duty-free sales, shopper visits and items sold, from customs, for each period and each holiday |
| Airports | Monthly international passengers by airport |
| Korea | Monthly duty-free sales to locals and to foreigners (the duty-free association) |
| Travel | Flights scheduled, visa rules and exchange rates |
| Policy | Allowances, new licences, rules for downtown shops and tax refunds on departure |
| Brands | Beauty and luxury groups' comments on their sales to travel retailers |

- **What leads revenue.** Monthly sales where they are published. Holiday figures.
  International passengers. Policy announcements. Exchange rates, which decide where
  travellers shop.
- **Backtest.** Travellers times the share who buy times spend against sales by place.
  The company's sales against the published sales for the place.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Gross margin follows the mix of goods and how deep the discounts are.
- At an airport the fee is the largest cost. When traffic falls, the guaranteed minimum
  or the fixed rent takes a larger share of sales. A fixed rent can appear in the
  accounts as a lease, with depreciation and interest.
- A downtown or island shop the company owns has more fixed costs and no airport fee.
- Buying in bulk earns better terms from the brands.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Stock is large and bought months ahead.
- Shopping complexes are heavy capex. Airport shops are not.
- The licence is the asset. New licences and new rivals change a place's economics.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Sales follow travel, and travel follows incomes, visas and exchange rates. They peak
  around the main holidays.
- Policy sets the allowance, the licences and what may be sold where.
- Traps:
  - a place's sales growth read as the company's, while rivals take share;
  - travellers returning while spend for each one falls;
  - a margin lifted by the exchange rate;
  - a guaranteed minimum left out of a weak year;
  - revenue from resellers read as demand from travellers;
  - a policy change priced in before its rules are written.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether spend for each traveller recovers.
- How much shoppers buy abroad or online instead.
- What new rules and licences do.
- What the next airport contracts cost.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A place receives 10m travellers. A fifth buy, spending 5,000 each: sales are 10bn. The
company holds 80%, which is 8bn.

Travellers grow 10%, the share who buy falls to 18% and spend falls to 4,500. Sales are
8.91bn, down 11% with more visitors. If the company's share slips to 75%, its sales are
6.68bn, down by a sixth.

At an airport it sells 1,000 and pays 35% of sales or a minimum of 300. In a weak year
it sells 700 and pays the minimum, which is 43% of sales.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
