# Consumer electronics brands

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

- A **brand owner** designs devices and accessories and sells them to consumers under its
  own name. Most of the making is contracted out, though some brands run their own plants.
- It sells in two ways. As a **seller on online marketplaces** and its **own website**,
  it books the retail price and pays the platform for each sale. As a **wholesaler** to
  shops, online retailers and distributors, it books the wholesale price, less the
  promotion money and rebates it gives them. Selling to an online retailer that resells
  the goods is wholesale.
- Some products are bought by **businesses** for their staff and offices, on technology
  budgets.
- It usually sells several **categories** at different stages: an established one that
  grows slowly, and newer ones that grow fast until rivals copy them.

The buyer decides demand, and does so by category: how many households or workplaces own
the product, how often they replace it, and whether a new feature gives a reason to
upgrade. An accessory follows sales of the device it attaches to.
Sales bunch around the big online promotions and the holiday quarter. Platforms decide
how visible the brand is and what it pays. Governments decide the tariff.

Three pairs of numbers get confused. The **retail value** sold on a platform against the
company's **revenue**, which is net of returns and discounts. Gross margin against the
margin **after platform fees and advertising**, which sit in selling costs. **Sales to
shops and distributors** against **what consumers bought** from them.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes devices or components for other brands
(see the [contract manufacturing card](contract-manufacturing.md) and the
[electronic components card](electronic-components.md));
it sells large home appliances, where installation and the property market matter (see
the [home appliances card](home-appliances.md)); or it trades unbranded goods across
borders.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each category separately, and each region and channel where the company reports
them, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Category market | Units or value sold in each category, from how many buyers own the product and how often they replace it, or from sales of the device it attaches to | Category growth from trackers, labelled as such |
| Company share | Share of the category from trackers. A rank on a platform shows direction, not a number | Category revenue growth |
| New products | Launch dates, and the category and price point each enters | Leave it out |
| Units and price | Units times average price, with product mix, promotion depth and currency apart | Revenue by category |
| Revenue by channel | Marketplaces, own website, shops and distributors | Online against offline, or one row |
| Channel stock | For wholesale: sales to retailers less what they sold on | The company's comments on retailers' stock |
| Revenue by region | In local currency, then translated | Reported growth only |
| Gross margin | By category: bought-in product cost, components, freight, tariffs, mix and currency | Category margin as reported |
| Selling costs | For marketplace sales: platform commissions and delivery fees as a share of those sales, and advertising. For wholesale: promotion money and rebates, which come off revenue | Selling costs as a share of revenue |
| Research cost | Staff and projects, by category where given | A share of revenue |
| Stock | Days of stock, including goods at sea and in platform warehouses, and write-downs of old models | Stock days |

Judge a category on its margin after selling costs. A product sold through a marketplace
can show a high gross margin and little profit. Build by category, and use the regional
split as a check where the two are reported separately.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure varies. Many brands give revenue and gross margin by category
  and by region once or twice a year, and the share of sales through each platform. Few
  give units or prices. Some give stock in detail.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Platforms | Bestseller ranks, and estimates of sales by product from marketplace trackers. Results of the big promotion days |
| Shipments | Unit shipments by brand for phones, wearables and other counted categories (IDC, Counterpoint). Shop sales panels in Europe and North America (Circana, GfK) |
| Costs | Container freight indices. Tariff schedules. Component prices |
| Trade | Customs exports by product and destination |

- **What leads revenue.** Platform ranks and promotion results. Launches and reviews.
  Changes in platform fees. Freight rates. Tariff announcements. Rivals' price cuts.
- **Backtest.** Category market times share against category revenue. Units times price
  against revenue where units are given.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Product cost is bought-in manufacturing, components, freight and tariffs. Freight and
  tariffs can each move the margin by points in a year.
- A brand that bills abroad and buys at home has its revenue and margin moved by the
  exchange rate.
- For a marketplace seller, platform fees and advertising rise with sales and do not
  shrink as the brand grows. They are the main reason a high gross margin leaves a modest
  profit. A wholesaler's equivalent is the promotion money it gives retailers.
- A tariff is shared between the brand, the retailer and the buyer. Check who is bearing
  it, and whether the brand has moved production.
- A new category needs research and marketing before it has sales.
- Old models are written down when a new one arrives.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A brand that contracts out its making needs little capex. Its money is in stock.
- The supply chain is long, so stock is built months before the holiday quarter. A
  misjudged season is cleared at a loss.
- A recall, most often for battery safety, brings refunds and provisions.
- Cash held in investments can produce gains that sit inside profit. Keep them apart.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Products have short lives. A hit fades, and a category turns into a price fight once it
  is copied.
- Dependence on one platform or a few large retailers is a risk: a rule change, a
  suspended account or a lost customer can stop sales.
- A shortage at a supplier can cap sales in a quarter.
- Tariffs, rules on small parcels, safety rules and bans on security grounds can close or
  reprice a market.
- Freight and exchange rates swing the margin without any change in demand.
- Traps:
  - platform retail value read as revenue;
  - gross margin read as profitability;
  - sales to distributors read as consumer demand;
  - growth from a new, lower-margin category read as improvement;
  - stock at sea and in platform warehouses left out;
  - returns and recall costs still to come;
  - investment gains and subsidies inside profit;
  - currency gains reported below operating profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the brand can hold its price against cheaper copies.
- Whether it can reduce its dependence on one platform.
- Whether new categories succeed before the research spending tells.
- Whether the margin holds against tariffs, freight and currency.

Yardstick: P/E, read with growth while growth is fast
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A brand sells 1,000 a year, 400 of it in one export market. Gross profit is 450. Platform
fees and advertising take 230, research 80 and administration 40, leaving operating
profit of 100.

The goods sold in that market cost 220 to buy. A new 20% tariff on that cost adds 44. If
the brand absorbs it, operating profit falls 44% to 56. To hold its profit it has to
raise prices in that market by 11% and lose no sales.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
