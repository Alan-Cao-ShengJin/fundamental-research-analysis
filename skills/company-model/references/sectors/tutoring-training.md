# Tutoring and training

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies sell courses: tutoring for school pupils where the rules allow it, test
preparation, training for civil-service and professional exams, language and skills
courses, and advice on studying abroad. Courses are sold by the term or by the exam, in
centres or online. Some also sell learning devices and apps.

Learners pay before the course. Some courses are refunded if the learner fails the
exam. Revenue on those is booked after the result, or over the course after an estimate
of refunds, depending on the company. Some classes are paid for only on passing.

Demand follows the number of people in each age group, the number sitting each exam, the
number of posts or places on offer, and the job market: when jobs are scarce, more
people sit civil-service exams and stay in education. Rules decide what may be taught
for profit, when, and how fees may be collected in advance.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Cash collected** against revenue. Fees paid in advance sit on the balance sheet.
- **Enrolments** against **learners**: one learner can buy several courses.
- Courses that are **refunded on failure** against ordinary ones.
- The **list price** against what is collected after discounts.
- Revenue from **teaching** against revenue from selling goods or devices.
- Profit **with** and **without** share-based pay.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns schools or colleges that students attend
for years (see the [schools and colleges card](schools-colleges.md)); the product is
software sold to schools (see the [software card](software.md)); or most of its revenue
now comes from selling goods online (see the
[e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each kind of course separately, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People sitting the exam | The number who apply for each exam and the number of posts or places offered, or the size of the age group | Growth in applicants and in posts, as published |
| Learners | The share of them who buy a course, and the company's share of those. The share of past learners who sign up again | Enrolment growth as reported. Where no enrolments are given, forecast revenue by kind of course |
| Price | The fee for each course, the mix of dear and cheap courses, discounts and rivals' prices | Revenue over enrolments |
| Capacity | Centres times classrooms times the share of seats filled, and the number of teachers. Online classes have no such limit | Centres as reported |
| Cash collected | Enrolments times price | The change in fees paid in advance, plus revenue |
| Refunds | The share of refundable fees returned, from pass rates, and when the revenue is booked. The refund liability on the balance sheet, beside fees paid in advance | The change in the refund liability |
| Revenue | Opening fees in advance, plus cash collected, less refunds, less the closing balance | Revenue growth by line |
| Teaching costs | Teachers' pay, which moves with classes where teachers are paid by the class and is fixed where they are salaried, and centre rent | Their share of revenue |
| Selling costs | The cost of winning each new learner | Its share of revenue |
| Content and overheads | Course development, technology and administration | Their share of revenue |
| Other businesses | Devices, apps and goods sold online, each on its own rows | Segment profit |
| Share-based pay | The yearly charge | As reported |
| Cash and payout | Cash, most of it paid in by learners in advance, and money placed in funds and shares. Capex on centres, dividends and buybacks | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by line each quarter, or twice a year for companies listed in Hong
  Kong, fees paid in advance, and the number of centres. Some give enrolments or paying
  learners, and others only active users.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Exams | The number who apply for the civil-service, postgraduate and teaching exams each year, and the number of posts or places offered |
| Population | Births by year, and the number sitting the university entrance exam |
| Jobs | Graduate numbers and youth unemployment |
| Online | App downloads and users for online courses |
| Rules | Notices on tutoring, on fees collected in advance and on advertising. These matter more than any other series |

- **What leads revenue.** Exam dates and the number of applicants. The change in fees
  paid in advance. Centre openings. Draft rules.
- **Backtest.** Opening advances plus cash collected less revenue and refunds against
  the closing balance. Enrolments times price against cash collected.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Where teachers are salaried, their pay and the centres' rent are both fixed, and the
  margin falls quickly when enrolments do. Where they are paid by the class, only the
  rent is fixed.
- These companies spend heavily to find learners. The share who sign up again decides
  whether that pays.
- A course refunded on failure sells at a high price and earns only on those who pass.
- Online courses cost less to deliver and more to sell.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little capital. Learners pay in advance, so cash runs ahead of
  profit while enrolments grow and behind it when they fall.
- Regulators may require fees paid in advance to be held in a supervised account.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand for exam training rises when jobs are scarce.
- Policy has closed whole lines of business at a stroke.
- Traps:
  - revenue booked on courses that may be refunded;
  - cash collected read as revenue;
  - a peak season's quarter multiplied by four;
  - growth bought with selling costs that do not come back;
  - cash counted as free when it is owed to learners as teaching or refunds;
  - profit quoted without share-based pay;
  - a new business line, such as selling goods, hiding a fall in teaching.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- What the rules will allow.
- How many people sit each exam as the population and the job market change.
- Whether AI tools replace paid courses.
- Whether the cash is paid out.

Yardstick: P/E ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company sells 60,000 ordinary course places at 3,000 and 40,000 refundable places at
8,000. Cash collected is 500m. The ordinary courses are booked as they are taught: 180m.
Of the refundable ones, 70% of learners fail and are repaid, so the company keeps 96m
and books it after the results. Revenue is 276m on 500m collected. Some companies book
the refundable courses over the term instead, after taking off the refunds they expect.

It spends 800 to win each of the 100,000 places, which is 80m, or 29% of revenue.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
