# Video games

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Game companies earn from what players spend. There are two models, and they are built
differently.

- **Free games with purchases inside**, on phones and computers. Revenue is active
  users, times the share who pay, times what each payer spends. A game runs for years
  as a service, kept alive with events and new content. It reaches players through app
  stores and other channels that keep a share of what players spend.
- **Games sold for a price**, on consoles and computers. Revenue is units sold times
  price. A new title sells most in its first weeks. Older titles keep selling for years
  at lower prices, helped by online stores and discounts. The cost of making a game is
  held on the balance sheet until launch and charged as the game sells, most of it in
  the first year.

Many paid games also earn for years from spending inside them, and at some publishers
that is most of the income. Build it like a free game, on the players of each title.
Beside these come subscriptions, advertising inside games and licensing of characters.

Players decide, and what they choose is hard to predict. A few games earn most of a
company's revenue. Regulators matter too: in China each game needs approval before
launch, and rules limit what minors may play and spend.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Gross billings** or bookings, which is what players paid, against **revenue**, which
  is booked over the life of what they bought and may be stated before or after the
  channel's share. The gap sits in deferred revenue.
- **Monthly active users**, **paying users** and **spend per paying user**.
- Revenue from **existing** games against revenue from **new** ones.
- **New titles** against the **catalogue** of older ones. Each company draws the line
  itself.
- Units **shipped to retailers** against units **sold to players**, and **digital**
  against **physical** copies.
- Development cost **charged** in the year against development cost **added to the
  balance sheet**.

Signs the card does not fit: most revenue is advertising or online shopping (see the
[e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)); the company
streams music or video (see the [streaming card](streaming.md)); it makes films or runs
cinemas (see the [film and television production card](film-tv-production.md) and the
[cinemas card](cinemas.md)); or it makes the console, in which case build the hardware
on the [consumer electronics brands card](consumer-electronics-brands.md).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by business each quarter. Deferred revenue, from which billings
  can be worked out. Some give active and paying users. Most list the games due and
  their expected timing. Japanese publishers of paid games give units by title, units of new
  titles and of the catalogue, the digital share, and a unit target for the year. US
  publishers give bookings by kind and by platform, and few units.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Game rankings | Daily rankings of games by spending and downloads on the app stores, and estimates of each game's revenue (Sensor Tower and others). They do not see every channel |
| Approvals | The monthly list of games approved in China |
| Market size | Monthly or quarterly game industry revenue from industry bodies, and sales charts by country (Circana in the US, Famitsu in Japan) |
| Players | Numbers playing each game at once on computer platforms |
| Consoles | Consoles sold and in use, from the console makers |
| Reviews | Review scores before launch |
| Rules | Notices on minors, content and the fees app stores may charge |

- **What leads revenue.** A game's ranking and downloads. Sign-ups and advance orders
  before a launch. Approvals. The change in deferred revenue. The schedule of new
  content. Review scores.
- **Backtest.** Active users times the share who pay times spend against billings.
  Opening deferred revenue plus billings less revenue against the closing balance.
  Units times price against revenue from paid games.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Development staff are a fixed cost. Channel fees move with revenue. Marketing is a
  choice, and is highest at launch.
- A successful free game has little extra cost, so most of its revenue beyond the
  channel fee is profit.
- For a paid game most of the development cost is charged in the launch year, so that
  year has both high revenue and a high charge. Catalogue sales carry little cost
  beyond the store's fee and any royalties.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Game companies need little fixed capital. Many hold a lot of cash, often with large
  stakes in other companies. Those that bought studios with debt do not.
- A big game takes three to five years to make and is often late.
- Players pay before the revenue is booked, in free games and for content inside paid
  ones, so cash runs ahead of profit when billings grow. In paid games, cash also goes
  out for years before a title earns anything.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Results are lumpy, because they depend on a few titles.
- Rules can stop approvals, limit play or change what channels may charge.
- Traps:
  - a launch quarter multiplied by four;
  - an app-store ranking read as total revenue when other channels are large;
  - revenue released from deferred balances while billings fall;
  - one game behind most of the profit;
  - development cost on the balance sheet growing faster than releases;
  - copies shipped to retailers read as copies sold;
  - a title that slips from one financial year into the next;
  - investment gains inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How strong the coming games are, and when they launch.
- How long the oldest games and the catalogue keep earning.
- How much growth comes from abroad.
- What regulators and app stores do next.

Yardstick: P/E, read with the games due. Where charges on studios bought have taken
earnings to a loss, investors look to bookings and cash flow
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A free game has 10m active users. 5% pay, and spend 200 a month: billings are 100m a
month, 1,200m a year. Channels keep 30% on half of it, 180m. Marketing takes 15%, another
180m.

A new free game takes 300m in its first quarter and fades 30% a quarter: 300m, 210m,
147m and 103m, which is 760m in its first year. If a third of each quarter's billings is
booked later, first-quarter revenue is 200m and deferred revenue rises by 100m.

A publisher sells 5m copies of a new title at 50 and 20m catalogue copies at 15: revenue
is 550m. The new title's development cost of 150m is charged in the year. Before store
fees and marketing, that leaves 400m. The next year has no new title: 24m catalogue
copies at 14 give 336m, down 39%. No development is charged, so 336m is left, down
16%.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
