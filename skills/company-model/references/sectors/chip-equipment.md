# Chip equipment

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies make the tools that fabs use to make chips: lithography, etching,
deposition, cleaning, polishing, inspection, testing and packaging. A tool is ordered
months ahead and booked as revenue when it ships or when the customer accepts it,
depending on the product and the company. Makers then earn from servicing and supplying
parts for the tools already installed.

The customers' capital spending decides demand: how many fabs the
foundries and memory makers build and how much of each kind of tool a fab needs. That
spending follows how full the customers' fabs are, the move to newer processes, and
government grants. In China, two more things matter: local makers are replacing imported
tools from a low base, and export rules limit what foreign makers may sell. The same
rule costs a foreign maker sales and hands a local maker share.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Orders**, **shipments** and **revenue**. A tool can ship months before the customer
  accepts it.
- **Backlog**, and **customers' prepayments** and **tools shipped but not yet
  accepted**, which sit on the balance sheet and lead revenue.
- Sales of **tools** against **service and parts**.
- Customers' **spending on tools** against their total capex, which includes buildings.
- The share of sales to **China**, which rules can change.

Signs the card does not fit: the company runs fabs (see the
[foundries card](foundries.md) and the [memory card](memory.md)); it designs chips; it
makes the wafers, gases and chemicals that fabs use up (see the
[chip materials card](chip-materials.md)); or it makes general industrial equipment with
a small sale to chip makers.

## 2. The driver tree

forecast.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Western makers report orders or backlog, revenue from tools and from
  service, the share of sales by region, and guidance each quarter. Chinese makers give
  less, and few report orders in figures. Watch customers' prepayments and stock on the
  balance sheet, revenue by kind of tool in the annual report, and what they say about
  orders on calls.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Tool spending | Forecasts of spending on fab equipment, quarterly billings by region with China among them, monthly billings by makers in Japan, and fab construction plans (SEMI and the Japanese equipment association) |
| Customers | The capex budgets of the large foundries and memory makers, given each quarter |
| China | Customs imports of chip-making equipment by type and country, monthly. Tender results published by Chinese fabs |
| Rules | Export controls by tool, customer and date |

- **What leads revenue.** Customers' capex guidance. Orders and backlog. Imports of
  tools into China. Prepayments. The start of a fab's construction, which leads tool
  deliveries by a year or two.
- **Backtest.** Customers' spending times the two shares against orders, or against
  revenue a few quarters later where no orders are given.

## 4. Costs and margin

- The leading tool makers earn high gross margins, because few can make what they make.
- Research is large and must continue through a downturn.
- Service and parts are steadier than tools and earn well.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Tool makers assemble more than they manufacture, so capex is modest.
- Stock is large: tools take months to build and can wait months for acceptance.
- Customers prepay part of the price, which funds some of that stock.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Spending on tools swings more than chip sales: fabs stop buying when they are not
  full.
- Customers in China buy ahead of each new export rule, which pulls orders forward.
- Traps:
  - orders pulled forward, read as the new level;
  - sales to a region that the rules may close;
  - revenue booked at shipment compared with revenue booked at acceptance;
  - backlog that customers can delay;
  - service revenue assumed safe when fabs cut what they run;
  - a few years of share gains from replacing imports, extended without limit;
  - grants inside profit.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where spending on tools goes next year.
- How long China keeps buying at this rate, and how fast local makers take share.
- What the next export rule covers.
- Who gains when the way chips are made changes.

Yardstick: forward P/E, read with growth and with where the cycle stands
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

Fabs spend 1,000 on tools. A fifth goes on etching, and the company has 10% of that: 20
of orders, shipped over the following year.

Next year spending falls 10%, to 900. The company's share of etching rises to 15%. Its
orders are 900 times 20% times 15%, which is 27, up 35% in a falling market.

A maker that reports no orders ends the year with customers' prepayments of 30. If
customers prepay a third of the price, that stands for about 90 of orders in hand. It
also holds tools at customers awaiting acceptance at a cost of 50. At a gross margin of
40% they become about 83 of revenue when accepted.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
