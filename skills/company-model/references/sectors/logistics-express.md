# Logistics and express delivery

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Logistics companies move goods for others. The businesses under that name earn in
different ways.

- **Express parcel networks** collect, sort, carry and deliver parcels for a price per
  parcel. Some run the whole network themselves. In the franchise model common in China,
  the listed company runs the sorting centres and long-distance trucks, and independent
  outlets collect and deliver. It then books only its own fee per parcel.
- **Freight forwarders** buy space on ships and aircraft and resell it. Most of their
  revenue is passed on to the carrier. What they keep is the gross profit per container
  or per tonne.
- **Contract logistics** runs warehouses and distribution for customers under contracts.
- **Road freight** carries part-loads through a network of terminals, or full loads at
  the spot rate.

Parcel volume follows online shopping, and has grown faster than its value as parcels
get smaller and cheaper. Freight follows industrial output and trade. In every part of
the business, competition on price decides how much of the volume growth becomes profit.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Volume**, **revenue per parcel** and **cost per parcel**.
- A franchise network's **fee per parcel** against the price the sender pays.
- A forwarder's **revenue** against its **gross profit**, also called net revenue.
- **Yield**, with and without the fuel surcharge.
- The **operating ratio**, which is operating cost over revenue.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns the ships, aircraft or railway that do the
carrying (see the [shipping card](shipping.md), the [airlines card](airlines.md) and the
[railways card](railways.md)); it runs a platform that matches shippers with truckers
and carries nothing (see the
[e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)); or it owns
warehouses and lets them (see the [landlords card](reits-landlords.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Chinese express companies listed at home publish volume and revenue per
  parcel every month. Others report them each quarter, with cost per parcel split
  between transport and sorting. Global parcel carriers report average daily volume and
  revenue per piece. Forwarders report volume and gross profit per unit. Road carriers
  report tonnage, yield and the operating ratio.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Parcels | Monthly industry volume, revenue and revenue per parcel, by region, and the share of the largest carriers (State Post Bureau in China). Online retail sales |
| Prices | Parcel prices in the main shipping towns, reported in the trade press. Statements by the regulator on pricing |
| Freight | Sea and air freight rates. Indices of freight volume and truck rates (Cass, DAT, the American Trucking Associations) |
| Costs | Diesel prices. Rules on couriers' pay and social insurance |

- **What leads profit.** Monthly volume and revenue per parcel give most of a quarter.
  Price moves in the main shipping towns. The peak shopping season. Diesel. Freight
  rates lead a forwarder's gross profit.
- **Backtest.** Volume times revenue per parcel against express revenue. Forwarding
  volume times gross profit per unit against gross profit.

## 4. Costs and margin

- A parcel network is a fixed cost. Each parcel costs less as volume fills the trucks and
  the sorting lines. In a price war those savings go to customers.
- For a company that delivers itself, couriers are the largest cost.
- A franchise network depends on its outlets. When they lose money, the network gives
  back fees to keep them alive.
- A forwarder's costs are staff. Its profit is gross profit less staff, so the ratio of
  the two is the measure to watch.
- Fuel is passed on through surcharges, with a lag.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Sorting centres and trucks are built ahead of volume, in waves. Free cash flow rises
  when a wave ends.
- A franchise network needs far less capital than one that owns the last leg.
- A forwarder pays the carrier before its customer pays it, so working capital grows
  with freight rates.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Parcel volume grows with online shopping in most years. Price is what swings.
- Freight is cyclical, and follows stocks of goods as well as sales.
- Regulators set rules on prices below cost and on couriers' pay.
- Traps:
  - share gained by cutting price;
  - a fall in revenue per parcel that is lighter parcels, read as a price cut, or the
    reverse;
  - a network's profit taken from its outlets;
  - a forwarder's revenue read as growth when freight rates rose;
  - a year of very wide forwarding margins read as normal;
  - a change in what is counted as revenue;
  - volume that depends on one online platform.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether price competition has ended, and who keeps the gain.
- Whether the industry consolidates.
- When capex falls and cash is paid out.
- What new businesses abroad or in other kinds of freight earn.

Yardstick: P/E, before the one-off costs of acquisitions and read with them, with
EV/EBITDA beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A network handles 10bn parcels. Its fee is 1.40 a parcel and its cost 1.00, so gross
profit is 0.40 a parcel and 4bn in all. Overheads are 1bn and profit is 3bn.

Next year volume grows 20%. The fee falls by 0.05 and the cost by 0.03. Gross profit is
0.38 on 12bn parcels, 4.56bn. Overheads grow 10%. Profit is 3.46bn, up 15% on volume up
20%.

A forwarder ships 1m containers. It charges 2,000 and pays 1,700, keeping 300: gross
profit is 300m on revenue of 2,000m. Staff cost 200m and profit is 100m. Freight rates
jump and it charges 3,700 and pays 3,300. Revenue is up 85%, gross profit is 400m, and
profit doubles to 200m.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
