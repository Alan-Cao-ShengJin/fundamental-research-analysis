# Property management

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

A property manager looks after buildings it does not own: security, cleaning, repairs and
running the estate, for the owners of homes, offices, malls and public buildings. The fee
is charged per unit of floor area each month, or as a contract price. Around it sit extra
services.

- **Services to developers**: staffing their sales offices and inspecting homes before
  handover.
- **Services to residents**: home services, retail, and agency on homes and parking
  spaces.
- **Running malls and offices** for their owners, for a share of the rent or the income.
  For some managers this earns more than everything else together, and it needs its own
  build.

New buildings decide growth. A manager owned by a developer is handed that developer's
new estates. Others win contracts by bidding, or buy smaller managers. Owners can vote a
manager out. Fees on homes are hard to raise, and in some places the local government
guides them, while wages rise every year.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Contracted area**, which includes buildings not yet handed over, against **managed
  area**, which earns fees now.
- A contract where the manager **keeps the whole fee** and bears the costs, against one
  where it takes a **fixed share** as commission and the rest belongs to the owners. The
  same building gives very different revenue and margin.
- A mall the manager **runs for a fee**, against one it **leases and sublets**, where it
  books the rent and bears the risk.
- Revenue from the **parent developer** against revenue from others.
- **Core profit**, which the dividend is often set on, against reported profit.

Signs the card does not fit: the company owns the buildings (see the
[landlords card](reits-landlords.md)); it builds and sells homes (see the
[developers card](property-developers.md)); or it earns commissions on sales and leases,
or runs workplaces for companies under outsourcing contracts (see the
[property agents and services card](property-agents-services.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Listed managers in Hong Kong and mainland China report twice a year:
  managed and contracted area by source and building type, the average fee, revenue and
  gross margin by line, the share of fees collected, and receivables by age with related
  parties shown apart. Managers of malls give the number of malls open and due to open,
  and the malls' retail sales.
- **Industry:**

- **Backtest.** Average managed area times the fee times twelve against basic management
  revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Staff are most of the cost. With fees flat and wages rising, the margin falls unless
  each employee covers more floor area.
- Margins differ widely by line. Running malls for a fee earns the highest margin where
  a manager does it. Among estate services, work for developers earns most and is
  shrinking where developers are. Contracts won from outside the parent usually earn
  less than the parent's own estates.
- A manager that keeps the whole fee bears the cost risk. One that takes a fixed share
  reports little revenue at a high margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little capital. Many residents pay fees in advance and some pay
  late, so a healthy manager holds net cash and still carries receivables.
- The risk sits with related parties. A parent short of cash can pay its manager late,
  or draw on the manager's cash through deposits, loans or purchases.
- Money collected for owners, such as repair funds, is not the company's.
- Buying other managers creates goodwill, which is written down if the contracts are
  lost.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Management fees are paid in good years and bad. Growth is what follows the property
  cycle, through completions and the parent's health.
- Rules that make it easier for owners to change manager, or that guide fees, lower
  growth and margin.
- Traps:
  - contracted area counted as earning fees;
  - growth from acquisitions read as growth won;
  - a margin held up by services sold to the parent on generous terms;
  - receivables from the parent, and cash lent or pledged to it;
  - one-off agency income on parking spaces or homes read as recurring;
  - a shift between the two kinds of contract read as a change in the business;
  - contracts with local governments that pay late;
  - loss-making contracts dropped, which lifts the margin and cuts the area.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether the cash is safe from the parent.
- Whether the company can grow without the parent's new estates.
- Whether the margin holds as wages rise and fees do not.
- Whether services to residents are a real business.
- How much of the cash is paid out.

Yardstick: P/E, with net cash and the dividend yield beside it; a manager with a large
mall business may be valued part by part
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A manager looks after 100 of floor area at a fee of 2 a month: revenue is 2,400 a year.
Staff and subcontract costs are 2,040, so gross profit is 360, a margin of 15%. If wages
rise 5% and nothing else changes, costs rise by 102 and gross profit falls 28%.

Its contracted area is 130. The 30 not yet handed over arrives over two years, which
gives growth of about 15% a year before any new contract is won.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
