# Poultry and meat processing

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies turn animals into meat and sell it by weight. There are two kinds.

- **Integrated chicken producers** own the breeding flocks, hatcheries, feed mills and
  processing plants. The birds are raised on the company's own farms or by farmers
  under contract, who are paid a fee. The meat is sold fresh to shops and restaurants,
  as prepared products at a higher and steadier margin, and abroad.
- **Meat packers** buy cattle or pigs, slaughter them and sell the meat. They earn the
  gap between the price of meat and the price of the animal.

People eat about the same amount of meat each year, with chicken slowly gaining. The
price is set by supply. For chicken, the number of chicks placed decides how much meat
arrives about two months later. The parent flocks behind them take about half a year to
change, and the grandparent stock behind those more than a year. For beef the herd takes
years to change. Disease can remove supply in weeks and close export markets.

Feed is the largest cost of raising a bird. For a producer that raises birds on its own
farms, feed and other raw materials are well over half of all costs. For one that pays
contract farmers and runs large processing plants, feed can be under half.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Birds** or head processed against **weight** of meat sold.
- The **market price** of each cut against the price the company **realises**, which
  depends on its mix and on contracts that follow the market late.
- **Fresh** meat against **prepared** products and exports.
- **Capacity**, in birds or head a day, against the share in use.
- Results **with** and **without** legal settlements and plant closures.
- **Segment** sales, which include meat passed from one unit to another, against group
  revenue.

Signs the card does not fit: the company raises animals and sells them live at the
market price (see the [livestock and feed card](livestock-feed.md)); branded packaged
food is most of its profit (see the
[packaged food and drink card](packaged-food-drink.md)); or it sells feed to outside
farmers (see the [livestock and feed card](livestock-feed.md)).

Build each region and each kind of product separately, with history beside the forecast.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |

| Industry supply | The breeding flock, eggs set and chicks placed, bird weights, frozen stock and imports. For packers, the herd and animals coming to slaughter | The published production forecast |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Sales, cost and profit by region each quarter, with the effect of volume,
  price and currency on sales. Once a year: capacity, what is spent on feed and what a
  move in feed prices costs. Chinese chicken producers publish sales volume and revenue
  each month, for chicken and for prepared products, before sales between the two are
  taken out.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Supply | In the US, the weekly count of eggs set and chicks placed, the monthly breeding flock and slaughter figures (USDA). In China, the breeding stock brought in and renewed, and chick prices, from the poultry association |
| Prices | Market prices by cut (USDA market news in the US, daily price reporters in China) |
| Feed | Maize and soybean meal prices, spot and futures |
| Trade | Export volumes, and bans after outbreaks of disease |
| Other meats | Prices of beef and pork, which move demand for chicken |
| Forecasts | The monthly supply and demand estimates (USDA) |

- **What leads profit.** Chicks placed lead supply by about two months, parent flocks by
  about half a year, and grandparent stock by more than a year. Feed prices lead cost by
  a quarter. The price of beef and pork. Outbreaks of disease.
- **Backtest.** The volume and price effects against the change in revenue. Lagged feed
  prices against cost for each unit.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Price moves fast and cost moves slowly, so profit swings from high to nothing within a
  year or two.
- Prepared and branded products earn a steadier margin than fresh meat.
- A packer's profit is the spread. It widens when animals are plentiful and narrows
  when they are scarce.
- Plants are fixed costs, so the share of capacity in use matters.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Plants are heavy fixed assets. Where farmers under contract own the farms, the
  producer needs less capital.
- Birds are raised in weeks, so the chicken cycle is shorter than the hog or cattle
  cycle.
- Live birds are held at cost under some accounting rules and at market value under
  others.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Supply responds to good margins within months, and the margin then falls.
- Disease and trade rules can change prices overnight.
- Traps:
  - a peak margin multiplied by planned volume;
  - volume growth into a falling price;
  - a feed cost forecast that ignores hedges;
  - the market price applied to meat sold on contract;
  - legal settlements treated as one-off when they recur;
  - an export market assumed open;
  - results abroad moved by the exchange rate.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where the cycle stands, and how fast supply is growing.
- Whether dear beef and pork keep demand for chicken strong.
- Where feed costs go.
- How much of the profit now comes from steadier prepared products.

Yardstick: EV/EBITDA and P/E on a margin averaged over the cycle; P/B beside them for
Chinese producers ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A producer processes 1m birds a week at 3 kilograms live weight, and three-quarters of
that becomes meat: 2.25m kilograms a week, 117m a year. At a price of 12 a kilogram,
revenue is 1,404m. Feed costs 6 for each kilogram of meat and other costs 4.5, so cost
is 1,228.5m and profit 175.5m.

The price falls 8%, to 11.04. Revenue is 1,291.7m and profit 63.2m, down 64%. A 10% rise
in feed prices would take off another 70.2m.

A packer buys a pig for 1,800, sells its meat and by-products for 2,000 and spends 120
processing it: 80 a head. If the price of pigs rises 3% and meat does not follow at
once, it earns 26 a head.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
