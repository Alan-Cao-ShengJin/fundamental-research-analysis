# Hospitals and healthcare services

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies are paid to treat or test patients: general hospitals, chains of
specialist hospitals and clinics for eyes, teeth, fertility, cancer or rehabilitation,
health check-up centres, and independent laboratories that run tests for hospitals.

Who pays shapes the business.

- **Public insurance** pays for most necessary care in China and in other public
  systems, at prices it sets. More and more it
  pays a fixed sum per case, whatever the hospital spends.
- **Patients themselves** pay for elective care such as laser eye surgery, dental
  implants and cosmetic work. This behaves like consumer spending and follows
  confidence.
- **Private insurers** pay private hospitals in many countries and bargain over prices.
  In the US a hospital's mix of payers decides what it earns for the same work. A
  regulator can also set a fixed price for each case at private hospitals.
- **Patients from abroad** travel for treatment and pay more.

Demand grows with ageing and with more people choosing elective treatment. Supply is
limited by doctors and, where hospitals are full, by beds. A new hospital takes years to
fill and loses money until it does.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Outpatient visits** against **admissions**, and revenue per visit or per case.
- Beds **registered** against beds **in use**.
- **Mature** hospitals against **new** ones, which pull the average down.
- Hospitals the company **consolidates** against those held in outside funds or by
  partners until they are profitable.
- Revenue **billed** against revenue after the insurer's deductions and year-end
  settlement.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes drugs or devices; it runs pharmacies (see
the [pharmacies card](pharmacies.md)); or it is a
health insurer, which collects premiums and pays claims and is built as an insurer.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Visits, operations, the number of hospitals and revenue by service line,
  twice a year or quarterly. US operators give admissions, revenue per admission and the
  payer mix.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Activity | National figures for hospital visits and discharges, public and private apart |
| Insurance | The public insurance fund's income and spending. Notices on paying per case and on prices for medical services. In the US, the yearly update to public payment rates |
| Elective demand | Consumer confidence. Marriage and birth figures for fertility and maternity care |
| Costs | Wages for nurses and doctors, and the cost of temporary staff |
| Peers | Other chains' same-unit growth and prices |
| Other countries | Each health ministry's figures, insurers' price talks with hospitals, and rules on what private hospitals may charge |

- **What leads revenue.** National visit figures. Draft insurance rules. The summer and
  holiday peaks for elective surgery. The schedule of new openings. Peers' prices.
- **Backtest.** Visits times revenue per visit against revenue from mature units. Each
  past opening's ramp against the one assumed.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Staff are the largest cost and change slowly, so a hospital's margin rises as it
  fills.
- A mature specialist hospital can earn a high margin. A chain's reported margin
  depends on how many new hospitals are still filling.
- When the insurer pays a fixed sum per case, the hospital keeps what it saves and bears
  what it overspends.
- Elective services need advertising, and compete on price when demand is weak.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- A new hospital needs capital and several years of losses before it earns.
- Chains often grow by buying hospitals, sometimes from funds linked to the company,
  and carry large goodwill.
- Doctors are kept with a share in their hospital, which shows as partners' share of
  profit.
- Public insurers pay late, and audit and claw back what they judge to be overcharged.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Insured care is steady. Elective care paid by patients rises and falls with
  confidence.
- Policy sets prices and what is covered, and tightens when the insurance fund is short.
- Traps:
  - growth from buying hospitals read as growth in the existing ones;
  - new hospitals' losses kept outside the listed company;
  - goodwill that has not been tested by a downturn;
  - revenue per visit pushed up in ways regulators then stop;
  - deductions by the insurer at the year end;
  - a fall in revenue that is cheaper supplies passed on, with profit unchanged;
  - one-off testing or epidemic revenue, and the receivables left behind.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- When patients' own spending recovers.
- What paying per case does to revenue.
- How fast new hospitals fill, and what they earn.
- Whether goodwill is safe.
- Whether prices for elective treatment hold.

Yardstick: EV/EBITDA and P/E
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A chain has 100 mature hospitals, each with revenue of 50 and a margin of 20%: revenue
5,000 and profit 1,000. It has 20 new hospitals, each with revenue of 15 and a loss of 3:
revenue 300 and a loss of 60. The chain's margin is 17.7%, against 20% for the mature
hospitals.

At the mature hospitals visits grow 6% and revenue per visit falls 2%, so revenue grows
3.9%.

A new hospital loses 5 in its first year and 2 in its second, earns 1 in its third and 8
by its fifth.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
