# Testing, inspection and certification

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies test products and materials in laboratories, inspect goods, sites and
equipment, and certify that products and systems meet a standard. Clients pay for each
test, each day of inspection or each audit.

The segments differ in clients and in margin:

- consumer goods tested for exporters and retailers;
- food, water, soil and air tested for companies and governments;
- buildings, plants and vehicles inspected;
- cargoes of commodities checked as they are traded;
- laboratory work for drug and device makers;
- audits of management systems.

Rules and standards decide demand: each new rule creates tests. So do trade volumes,
clients' new products, government budgets for food and the environment, and companies
closing their own laboratories. The market grows a little faster than the economy and
swings less.

In China there are tens of thousands of small testing institutions. Standard tests are
won on price, and government clients pay slowly.

Numbers that get confused:

- The company's **own growth** against growth from acquisitions.
- **Laboratories** opened against how fully they are used.
- Revenue **per laboratory** and **per employee**.
- **Segment** margins, which differ widely, against the group's.
- **Capex** on laboratories against depreciation.
- **Reports issued** against revenue.
- **Adjusted** profit, which leaves out charges on acquisitions, against reported
  profit.

Signs the card does not fit: the company runs clinical laboratories for hospitals (see
the [hospitals card](hospitals-healthcare-services.md)); it does research for drug
makers under contract (see the
[contract research and manufacturing card](contract-research-manufacturing.md)); it
makes testing equipment; or it sells its people's time on technology projects (see the
[IT services card](it-services.md)).

## 2. The driver tree

Build each segment or division separately, with history beside the forecast. A global
group that gives growth and margin by division, and little on laboratories, is built
from those two.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segment market | The size and growth of each segment, from the regulator's statistics and the rules in force | Growth of the industry's revenue, or the large companies' own growth in that segment |
| Own growth | The company's growth by segment: volume from clients' activity, price, and new services | Segment growth on its trend |
| Laboratories | The number of laboratories and sites. New ones by opening date, which take two to three years to reach normal use and margin | Revenue over laboratories, or over fixed assets |
| Staff | Staff times revenue per employee, as a check on the segment build | Skip it where staff numbers come only once a year |
| Revenue | The company's own growth, plus acquisitions, plus currency, by segment | |
| Staff cost | The largest cost, with the yearly pay rise | Its share of revenue |
| Laboratory costs | Depreciation of equipment, leases and supplies. They are fixed, so use decides the margin | Their share of revenue, raised when new laboratories open |
| Segment margin | By segment or division: price, mix, productivity and how fully laboratories are used, with the drag from new ones. Where costs are not split by division, forecast each division's margin directly | The margin as reported |
| Overheads | Selling, administration and research | Their share of revenue |
| Acquisitions | Revenue bought, the price paid and the goodwill it leaves | Announced deals only |
| Receivables | In days, with bad-debt charges. Government clients and builders pay slowly | Their past days |
| Capex | New laboratories and equipment, as a share of revenue | The guided figure, or its past share |
| Currency, tax and payout | Revenue abroad, the tax rate and the dividend | As reported |
| Debt, leases and partners | Net debt and interest, lease charges, and partners' share of profit | As reported |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** The large Western companies give growth by division each quarter, split
  into their own growth, acquisitions and currency, and margins twice a year. Chinese
  companies give revenue and gross margin by segment twice a year, with the number of
  laboratories, staff and receivables. In China the first quarter is the weakest and the
  last the strongest.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Market | In China, the market regulator's yearly statistics on testing institutions: their number, revenue, reports issued and staff. Elsewhere, the large companies' own growth |
| Rules | New standards and compulsory tests, and one-off national surveys |
| Trade | Export volumes and commodity trade |
| Clients | Research spending and product launches in electronics, cars and drugs |
| Peers | The results of the large global and Chinese testing companies |

- **What leads revenue.** The dates new rules take effect. Government tenders for
  surveys. Exports. Laboratories opened, which lead revenue by a year or two. New
  accreditations.
- **Backtest.** Segment growth against the industry's statistics. Revenue per laboratory
  and per employee. Capex against the rise in depreciation.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Staff and laboratories are mostly fixed costs, so the margin follows how fully the
  laboratories are used.
- A new laboratory loses money at first and pulls the group's margin down.
- Standard tests that many can run are cut in price. Tests that need a rare
  accreditation hold their price.
- A falsified report or a lost accreditation can close a laboratory.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Laboratories and their equipment are capex. At a company that owns many laboratories,
  fixed assets are large against revenue. At the global groups, where inspection and
  audits need no laboratory, capex is a few percent of revenue.
- Growth by acquisition leaves goodwill.
- Receivables are long where clients are governments or builders.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand is steady. One-off government programmes, such as a national survey or mass
  testing in an epidemic, lift a year and then leave.
- Traps:
  - growth that was bought, read as the company's own;
  - a margin forecast without the drag from new laboratories;
  - a one-off programme left in the base;
  - receivables growing faster than revenue;
  - goodwill on acquisitions that have not earned it;
  - a change in the mix of segments read as a change in margin within them.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether margins recover as new laboratories fill.
- How far price competition goes in standard tests.
- What acquisitions earn.
- Whether new fields, such as chips, batteries and medical work, become large.

Yardstick: P/E, with EV/EBITDA beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A company has 100 laboratories, each with revenue of 10: revenue is 1,000. Staff cost 450,
laboratory costs 300 and overheads 100, so profit is 150.

It opens 10 laboratories. In their first year each brings in 3 and costs 8: revenue of 30
against costs of 80, a loss of 50. Group revenue is 1,030, up 3%, and profit is 100,
down a third.

By their third year each brings in 10 and costs 8.5. They add 15, and group profit is 165.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
