# Baijiu

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Baijiu makers sell branded Chinese liquor at prices far above its cost. Products sit in
tiers, from mass-market to very expensive.

The maker sells to distributors, who pay in advance. It sets the price it charges them
and decides how much each may buy. Some makers sell through a sales company owned with
their distributors, so the largest customer in the accounts can be that company.
Distributors then sell on at a wholesale price the market sets, which moves every day.
Many makers also sell direct to consumers at a higher price.

Buyers drink at business dinners, banquets and weddings, give bottles as gifts, and some
buy to keep. Demand follows business activity, incomes and the festival calendar.
Government rules on official entertaining can cut the top tiers sharply.

The gap between the wholesale price and the maker's price is the distributor's profit.
When the wholesale price falls towards the maker's price, distributors stop buying, and
the maker's own shipments follow.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Shipments** to distributors against bottles actually **opened**.
- The **maker's price**, the **wholesale price** and the **retail price**. List prices
  include value-added tax and revenue does not.
- Volume in **tonnes**, as reported, against prices quoted for a **bottle**.
- **Revenue** against **cash received**. Advances from distributors sit on the balance
  sheet, and they are seasonal, so compare the same date each year.
- Growth from **volume**, from a **price rise**, and from selling more **direct** or
  more of the dearer products.
- Liquor **made** this year, much of which will be sold years later, against liquor
  **sold**.

Signs the card does not fit: the company makes whisky, cognac or other spirits sold
through importers in many countries (see the [spirits card](spirits.md)); it makes beer
(see the [beer card](beer.md)); or it distributes liquor it does not make (see the
[trading and distribution card](trading-distribution.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Revenue by product tier, channel and region, twice a year and at some
  makers each quarter. Tonnes made, sold and in stock by tier, once a year. The number
  of distributors, advances from them each quarter, and often a revenue target for the
  year.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Wholesale prices | Daily wholesale prices of the benchmark products, from trade price trackers |
| Channel | Brokers' and trade media's surveys of distributors' stock and of sales at festivals. Treat them as reports |
| Official data | Baijiu output. Restaurants' revenue. Notices on official entertaining |
| Peers | Other makers' results, targets and price changes |

- **What leads revenue.** The wholesale price and distributors' stock lead shipments.
  Advances at each quarter end. Sales over the main festivals. The company's own target.
- **Backtest.** Volume times the maker's price against revenue by tier. Revenue with
  value-added tax, plus the change in advances and less the change in bills received,
  against cash collected.

## 4. Costs and margin

- The liquor costs little to make, so gross margin on the top tiers is very high and
  hardly moves.
- Tax on sales and selling costs are the costs that matter. Tax follows what is made
  as well as what is sold, so its share can jump in a weak year. Selling costs fall
  more slowly than revenue.
- So profit moves faster than revenue, in both directions.
- Lower tiers earn much less, so a shift down the tiers lowers the margin.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The best products are aged for years. What can be sold now was decided by what was
  made then.
- Distributors' advances mean a maker holds a great deal of cash and pays out much of
  its profit.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand at the top follows business activity and official rules. A downturn is followed
  by years of distributors working off stock.
- Makers can hold reported revenue for a time by pressing distributors to keep buying.
  The wholesale price and advances show when that is happening.
- Traps:
  - shipments read as demand;
  - revenue held up while advances fall and the wholesale price sinks to the maker's
    price;
  - a price rise announced while the wholesale price is falling;
  - growth from selling direct, read as a price rise;
  - one product behind most of the profit;
  - output running ahead of sales, with finished stock building;
  - a change in when tax is paid.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- When distributors' stock is worked off and the wholesale price steadies.
- Whether demand for the top tiers is lower for good.
- Whether the payout holds while profit falls.
- Whether the leading brands keep taking share.

Yardstick: P/E, with dividend yield beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A maker ships 100 bottles to distributors at 1,000 each: revenue is 100,000. The
wholesale price is 1,300, so a distributor earns 300 a bottle.

The wholesale price falls to 1,050. After its own costs the distributor earns almost
nothing and stops paying in advance. The maker cuts shipments to 90 to protect the price.
Revenue falls 10%.

In another year the maker moves 10 of its 100 bottles from distributors to its own
channel, at 1,400. Revenue rises 4% with no more bottles sold.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
