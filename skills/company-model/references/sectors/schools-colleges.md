# Private schools and colleges

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies own schools that charge tuition: private universities and vocational
colleges and, where the rules allow, private and international schools for children.
Each student pays tuition and often boarding. In China fees are collected at the start
of the school year and booked over it. Elsewhere they are often paid by instalment. A
student stays for the length of the course, so most of next year's revenue is already
enrolled.

Families pay, out of what they are willing to spend to get ahead. The number of young
people in each age group sets the size of the market, and it is known years in advance.
The share who go on to college, the places at public schools, and student loans or
grants decide the rest.

Rules decide almost everything else. In China the authorities set how many students each
school may admit, review fees, and decide how a school may be owned and whether it may
pay dividends.

Elsewhere, private universities set their own fees and compete for students. They admit
students two or more times a year, teach a growing share online, and lose part of each
class before it finishes.

Numbers that get confused:

- Students **enrolled**, the school's **capacity**, and the number it is **allowed** to
  admit.
- The **new intake** against all students, which includes those from earlier years.
- The **list fee** against the average actually collected, after scholarships.
- The **school year** against the company's financial year.
- **Cash collected** against revenue. Fees paid in advance sit on the balance sheet.
- A **for-profit** school against a **not-for-profit** one, which may not pay
  dividends.

Signs the card does not fit: the company sells courses by the term or by the exam (see
the [tutoring and training card](tutoring-training.md)); the product is software sold to
schools (see the [software card](software.md)); or it publishes books.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each school, or each country, separately, with history beside the forecast. A
group that reports by country is built by country, and quarter by quarter where intakes
fall in different quarters.

| Row | Built from | If the data is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capacity | Places in classrooms and dormitories, with new campuses by opening date | Capacity as stated. Skip it where much of the teaching is online |
| Enrolment | The number admitted at each intake, within any quota, plus students continuing from earlier years, at the share who stay | Student growth |
| Fees | Tuition and boarding per student, with any rise approved or filed for the new intake, less scholarships | Revenue per student, with the price and mix effect the company reports |
| Revenue | Students times fees, booked over the school year, plus canteens, training and other services | Revenue growth |
| Fees paid in advance | Opening balance, plus cash collected, less revenue booked | As reported |
| Teaching costs | Teachers, from the number of students per teacher and pay | Cost per student |
| Campus costs | Running costs and depreciation, and fees paid to a public partner where a college is being made independent | Their past level, grown with campuses |
| Recruitment and overheads | The cost of recruiting students, and administration | Their share of revenue |
| Bad debts | Fees billed and not collected, where students pay by instalment | Their past share of revenue |
| Schools bought | The price, the students and fees they bring, and the goodwill | Announced deals only |
| Capex | New campuses and dormitories | The guided figure. With none, announced projects over their build years, plus other capex at its recent level |
| Debt, leases and interest | Borrowing for campuses, rent on leased campuses, and their cost | As reported |
| Tax | The tax a school pays once it registers as for-profit, and the cost of changing its registration | As reported |
| Currency | Results of schools abroad, translated | The split the company gives |
| Payout | Dividends, where the structure allows them, and buybacks | The policy the company has stated |

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Student numbers by school at the start of each school year, the new
  intake, fee ranges, and plans for new campuses. Groups with schools in several
  countries give enrolment and revenue by country, at constant currency.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Births by year, which give the size of each age group years ahead. The number sitting the university entrance exam |
| Quotas | Each province's enrolment plan for private colleges |
| Funding | Rules on student loans and grants, where they pay part of the fee |
| Other countries | Each country's enrolment statistics by institution, and its rules on licensing programmes |
| Rules | Laws and notices on private education, fees and ownership. These matter more than any other series |

- **What leads revenue.** Enrolment at the start of the school year fixes most of that
  year's revenue. The change in fees paid in advance. Draft rules. The size of the age
  groups coming through.
- **Backtest.** Students times fees against revenue. Opening advances plus cash
  collected less revenue against the closing balance.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Teachers and campuses are fixed for the year, so each extra student is mostly profit
  once a school is running.
- Regulators expect schools to spend more on teaching, which lowers margins over time.
- A new campus loses money until its year groups fill, which takes as many years as the
  course lasts.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Campuses need land and buildings, often financed with debt.
- Fees arrive before the teaching, so cash is strong at the start of each school year.
- Many groups grew by buying schools, and carry goodwill.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Demand is steady through the economic cycle. Policy is the risk.
- Falling births reach each level of education on a known timetable.
- Traps:
  - growth in students that came from buying schools;
  - a fee rise assumed before it is approved;
  - capacity read as growth when the quota is the limit;
  - a margin before the teaching spend regulators require;
  - a not-for-profit school's cash counted as available for dividends;
  - an ownership structure the rules may disallow.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- What the rules will allow, and whether schools can register as for-profit.
- How falling births meet rising enrolment rates.
- Whether fees can keep rising.
- Whether and when dividends are paid.

Yardstick: P/E, with dividend yield where one is paid; EV/EBITDA for groups outside
China ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A college has 40,000 students paying 15,000 in tuition and 2,000 for boarding: revenue is
680m. Costs are 400m and profit is 280m.

Next year students grow 5% and fees 3%, so revenue grows 8.2%. Costs grow 4%. Profit is
735.4m less 416m, which is 319.4m, up 14%.

A four-year college admits 10,000 a year and has 40,000 students. If the quota is cut to
9,000 and nobody leaves early, it has 39,000 students next year and 36,000 after four
years: each year a quarter of the cut reaches revenue.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
