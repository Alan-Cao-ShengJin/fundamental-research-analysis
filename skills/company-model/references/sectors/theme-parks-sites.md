# Theme parks and tourist sites

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies sell a day out or a holiday in a place they run. Capacity is fixed and
so are most of the costs.

- **Theme parks** sell admission and what visitors spend inside on food, goods and
  hotels.
- **Tourist sites**, such as mountains and heritage sites in China, are run under a
  concession. The operator earns from cable cars, shuttle buses, shows and hotels. The
  ticket price is set by the authorities, and part of the ticket income goes to the
  body that owns the site. Often the operator books only its own share of the ticket,
  and pays the owner fees on its other income as well.

Demand follows what people spend on leisure, public holidays, the weather and how easy
the place is to reach. A new railway line or a new attraction brings visitors.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Visits** against **revenue per visit**.
- The **list price** of a ticket against what is collected after discounts, passes and
  free entry.
- The **ticket price** against the part of it the operator books as revenue.
- The sum of the **segments** against group revenue, where one segment sells to another.
- Profit from the park against profit from **homes sold** beside it.

Signs the card does not fit: the company mainly runs hotels (see the
[hotels card](hotels.md)) or casinos (see the [casinos card](casinos.md)); it runs cruise
ships (see the [cruise lines card](cruise-lines.md)); it builds
homes around its parks as its main business (see the
[property developers card](property-developers.md)); or it sells trips it does not run
(see the [online travel card](online-travel.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Visits for each park or site, yearly or quarterly, with revenue and margin
  by kind of income: tickets, cable cars, hotels and travel services.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Holidays | Trips and spending over each public holiday (the tourism ministry), and the sites' own visitor counts |
| Attendance | Yearly attendance rankings of the large parks |
| Transport | New railway lines and airports, and passenger numbers |
| Weather | Rain, heat and storms on peak days |

- **What leads revenue.** Visitor counts over holidays. New transport links. Approvals
  of ticket prices. The weather.
- **Backtest.** Visits times revenue per visit against revenue, for each kind of income.

## 4. Costs and margin

- A park's or a cable car's costs are mostly fixed, so an extra visitor is nearly all
  profit and a lost one nearly all loss.
- Fees to the site's owner move with revenue. Travel services and restaurants pass most
  of their revenue on as cost, so growth there adds little profit.
- A new park loses money for its first years.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Parks cost a great deal to build. Some operators carry heavy debt. Others, with old
  assets, carry almost none.
- Parks need new rides to bring visitors back, which is capex that returns every few
  years.
- A concession to run a tourist site expires, and its terms can change.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Leisure spending falls early in a downturn and returns fast.
- One bad summer of weather can decide a park's year.
- Traps:
  - record holiday visits with lower spending per visitor;
  - free and discounted visitors counted as paying ones;
  - a new park's first year taken as its normal level;
  - profit on homes sold beside a park read as profit from the park;
  - a ticket price the operator cannot raise;
  - growth in low-margin travel services read as growth in the site's profit;
  - debt falling due in a weak year.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Whether demand holds after a recovery.
- Whether prices can rise.
- What new parks and projects earn.
- How fast debt is paid down.

Yardstick: EV/EBITDA, with P/E beside it
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A park takes 3m visits at 300 each, 200 for the ticket and 100 inside: revenue is 900m.
Costs are 600m and fixed, so profit is 300m. Visits grow 10% at the same spend: revenue
is 990m and profit 390m, up 30%. If the extra visitors come on half-price tickets, they
add 60m and profit is 360m, up 20%.

A site sells a ticket for 190 and the operator books 57 of it. Its cable cars carry each
visitor twice at 80 a ride, which is 160. It pays the site's owner 15% of that, 24.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
