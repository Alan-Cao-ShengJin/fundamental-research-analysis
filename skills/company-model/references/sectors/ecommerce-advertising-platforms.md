# E-commerce and advertising platforms

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

These companies earn in three ways, and the large ones mix all three.

- **A marketplace.** Merchants sell to shoppers on the platform. The platform earns a
  commission on each sale and, more importantly, sells merchants advertising to be seen.
  It may also sell them delivery and payment services.
- **Direct retail.** The company buys goods and sells them itself. It books the full price
  as revenue and earns a thin margin after buying, storing and delivering.
- **Advertising on content.** The company runs a search engine, a social network or a
  video service, and sells advertisers access to its users' attention.

Shoppers decide how often they buy and how much they spend. Merchants decide how much of
their margin they will give up for traffic, which limits what a marketplace can take.
Advertisers' budgets follow the economy and what each platform returns per unit spent.
Rivals compete for users' time and with lower prices. Regulators set rules on competition,
on fees and on the use of personal data.

Three numbers get confused. **Gross merchandise value** is what shoppers spend on the
platform, and each company defines it differently. **Revenue** is the platform's fees for
a marketplace but the full sale price for a direct retailer, so the two cannot be
compared. **Take rate** is marketplace revenue divided by gross merchandise value.

Signs the card does not fit: the company matches riders with drivers or delivers meals
(see the [ride-hailing and delivery card](ride-hailing-delivery.md)); it is a retailer
that sells mainly through shops (see the [retail card](retail.md)); or a cloud, payments, lending, games or logistics unit
is more than about 15% of profit, or of revenue where profit is not split, in which case
that unit gets its own build.

## 2. The driver tree

Build each business the company reports separately, with history beside the forecast. Use
the route that matches each.

Where a marketplace publishes neither sales volume nor buyers, build each type of revenue
it reports on its own, and work out what each type is: advertising bought by merchants,
or commission and services charged on sales. Estimate sales volume from national online
retail sales and the platform's direction of share, from parcel volumes and from what
the platform owes merchants, and label it as an estimate.

A cross-border marketplace needs care. Where the platform sets prices and handles
delivery for its merchants, it may book close to the full sale price as revenue. Where
merchants do that themselves, it books a commission. Revenue per unit of sales then
moves with the mix of the two, so build them apart where the company allows.

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** Disclosure has thinned. Several large platforms no longer publish gross
  merchandise value or buyer numbers, and explain revenue growth in words. Most report
  revenue by segment and type each quarter. Advertising platforms often give users, the
  growth in advertisements shown and the growth in price per advertisement. Direct
  retailers give fulfilment cost.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Shopping | National online retail sales. Parcel volumes from the postal regulator, which track orders. Outside estimates of sales on the big promotion days |
| Attention | App usage and time-spent trackers (QuestMobile in China, Sensor Tower elsewhere) |
| Advertising | Forecasts and surveys of advertising spending from agencies |
| Rivals | Other platforms' reports and subsidy campaigns |
| Rules | Competition rulings and fines, fee rules, and changes to what data may be used for targeting |

- **What leads revenue.** Parcel volumes and online retail sales. Time spent on the app.
  Agencies' advertising forecasts. Promotion results. A rival's subsidy campaign.
- **Backtest.** Gross merchandise value times take rate against marketplace revenue, or
  each revenue type against its estimated driver where sales volume is not published.
  Advertisements shown times price against advertising revenue. Orders times value per
  order against product revenue.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Merchant advertising costs the platform almost nothing, so a rise in that part of the
  take rate is nearly all profit. It is also what merchants resent, and what a rival
  undercuts. Services such as delivery carry their own cost, so a take rate that rises
  through them adds much less.
- Direct retail earns a few points of margin. Scale helps through supplier rebates and
  fuller warehouses and delivery routes.
- Subsidies swing with competition, and can take a year's profit.
- Computing capacity is a growing cost, first as capex and then as depreciation.
- Advertising on content pays for the content or the traffic. Keep that cost against the
  revenue it earns.
- Share-based pay is large. Show profit with it and without it.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- Warehouses, delivery networks and data centres are the capex. Data centres have become
  the largest item for several platforms.
- Retailers and marketplaces collect from shoppers before paying suppliers and merchants,
  so they hold cash that is not theirs.
- Large platforms hold stakes in many other companies. Gains and losses on them move net
  profit and belong outside the operating build.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Shopping follows consumer spending. Advertising swings more than the economy does.
- Competition comes in waves: a new entrant with lower prices or a new format takes
  share, and incumbents answer with subsidies.
- A competition ruling, a fee rule or a limit on targeting can reset revenue. So can a
  tariff or a rule on small parcels, for cross-border sellers.
- Traps:
  - gross merchandise value defined differently, or no longer published;
  - a marketplace's revenue compared with a direct retailer's;
  - a take rate that rose through mix or accounting, not pricing;
  - subsidies taken off revenue at one company and shown as marketing at another;
  - buyers or users added across apps and counted twice;
  - profit measures that leave out share-based pay and investment results;
  - a segment's growth changed by units moved in or out of it.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- How far the take rate can rise before merchants leave.
- What it costs to hold share against a new rival.
- Whether spending on computing capacity pays back.
- Whether losses abroad and in new businesses buy lasting growth.
- What regulators will allow.

Yardstick: P/E on the core; a sum of the parts where there are several businesses, stakes
and net cash ([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

Shoppers spend 1,000 on a marketplace. It takes 1% in commission and merchants spend
another 3% on advertising, so revenue is 40. Its costs for that volume are 15, leaving 25.

A direct retailer with the same 1,000 of sales books revenue of 1,000. It earns a gross
margin of 8%, which is 80, and spends 6% on warehousing and delivery, which is 60,
leaving 20. Its revenue is 25 times the marketplace's and its profit is lower.

If merchants cut advertising to 2.5% of their sales, the marketplace's revenue falls to
35 and, with costs unchanged, its profit falls 20% to 20.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
