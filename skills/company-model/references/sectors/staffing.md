# Staffing and recruitment

Use with the [sector guide](index.md). The card is a guide: work out how this company
actually makes money before using it, and take each business unit separately. Skip the
rows that do not apply.

## 1. What is sold, to whom, and who decides demand

Staffing companies supply workers to clients. There are four lines of work.

- **Temporary staffing.** The staffing company employs and pays the worker and bills the
  client for each hour at a mark-up. Revenue is the whole bill. The cost of sales is the
  worker's pay and social charges. Gross profit is the mark-up.
- **Permanent placement.** A fee, usually a share of the first year's salary, for
  finding someone the client then employs. Where recruiters' pay is counted in
  operating costs, almost all of the fee is gross profit. Some companies put that pay
  in cost of sales and report a lower margin.
- **Outsourced services**: running a client's recruitment or its temporary workforce
  for a fee. In China, placing workers with a client for the long term and billing the
  client each month for each person's whole pay plus a fee, which leaves a margin of a
  few percent.
- **Professional staffing**, such as engineers, IT and healthcare staff, at higher rates
  and margins than factory and office work.

Clients' need for flexible labour decides demand. Temporary work is among the first
things to fall in a slowdown and the first to recover. Permanent hiring swings more.
Wage growth lifts revenue, and labour law decides what temporary work is allowed.

Numbers that get confused:

- **Revenue**, which includes workers' pay, against **gross profit**, which some
  companies call net fees and which is the real measure of size.
- Growth **per working day** against reported growth.
- **Temporary** against **permanent** work, as shares of gross profit.
- The company's **own staff** against the **workers it places**.
- **Gross margin** against the share of gross profit that becomes **operating profit**.
- Workers placed at the **end** of a period against the **average** over it.

Signs the card does not fit: the company runs an online jobs marketplace paid by
employers' advertising or subscriptions (see the
[e-commerce and advertising card](ecommerce-advertising-platforms.md)); it delivers
technology projects with its own teams (see the [IT services card](it-services.md)); or
it sells payroll software (see the [software card](software.md)).

## 3. The data behind the rows

- **Company.** The large European companies report each quarter: revenue by country and
  line of work, split into the company's own growth, acquisitions, currency and working
  days; a gross margin bridge; operating costs and own staff; receivables in days; and
  the trend at the end of the quarter. British recruiters report net fees by region and
  the number of consultants. Chinese companies give workers placed at the period end and
  revenue by line twice a year.
- **Industry:**

| Source | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Temporary work | Monthly temporary-help jobs in the US (Bureau of Labor Statistics) and the staffing association's weekly index. Monthly temporary employment in France (Prism'emploi). Similar series in the Netherlands, Germany and Japan |
| Hiring | Job openings, job-advertising indexes and unemployment claims |
| Wages | Wage growth, minimum wages and social charges |
| Peers | The largest companies report first and comment on each month |
| Rules | Rules on temporary work, payroll subsidies and tax credits |
| China | Yearly totals for the human-resources services industry, from the labour ministry. There is no monthly series |

- **What leads revenue.** Weekly and monthly temporary-work figures. Job openings. The
  growth rate at the end of the last quarter. Changes in the company's own headcount.
- **Backtest.** Growth per working day against the temporary-work figures for the
  country. Gross profit per own employee across a cycle.

## 4. Costs and margin

- Workers' pay passes through, so the gross margin on temporary work is thin and moves
  slowly.
- Permanent fees earn the highest margin and fall fastest.
- The company's own staff are the main cost and are cut with a lag. So operating profit
  swings several times as much as gross profit.
- Wage growth lifts revenue and gross profit in money with no more work done.
- Payroll subsidies and tax credits can be a large part of the margin, and governments
  change them.

## 5. Capacity, capex and working capital

- The business needs little fixed capital.
- It pays workers before clients pay it. Growth uses cash, and a downturn releases it.
- Acquisitions bring goodwill and debt.

## 6. Cycle, policy and traps

- Staffing turns early in the cycle, down and up.
- In some countries companies are hiring fewer temporary workers than they used to, for
  reasons that outlast the cycle.
- Traps:
  - reported growth not adjusted for working days;
  - revenue growth read as profit growth while the mix moves to large, low-margin
    accounts;
  - a recovery in permanent hiring assumed on a date;
  - the profit ratio of a peak year treated as normal;
  - cash released in a downturn read as strength;
  - a subsidy that is about to end;
  - growth that was bought.

## 7. What investors argue about, and the yardstick

- Where the cycle stands.
- Whether online platforms and AI take the recruiter's place.
- Whether temporary work in mature markets is shrinking for good.
- How much cost can be cut, and how much comes back when demand does.

Yardstick: P/E, read with where the cycle stands. At the bottom, investors look to
profit at a normal ratio to gross profit
([yardstick list](../../../valuation-thesis/references/yardsticks.md)).

## Worked build

A staffing company bills 1,000 for temporary work at a mark-up that leaves 18%, which
is 180. Permanent fees are 20. Gross profit is 200, its own costs are 160, and
operating profit is 40.

Temporary revenue falls 5% and permanent fees fall 25%. Gross profit is 171 plus 15,
which is 186, down 7%. With no cost cuts, operating profit is 26, down 35%. If costs are
cut 4%, it is 32.4, down 19%.

A quarter with 62 working days against 63 a year earlier lowers revenue 1.6% with no
change in demand.

All figures are invented. They show the relationships, not a forecast.
