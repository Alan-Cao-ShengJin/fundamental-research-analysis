# Research writing

The one standard for the writer and the reviewer. A report exists so that a reader who has never looked at the company understands what we found and can act on it. Good research writing is sharp, short and easy to read. It fails in two ways: stiff phrasing the reader has to decode, and padding the reader has to wade through. Professional means accurate, specific and honest about what we do not know. It does not mean formal.

## How to write the report

1. **Say it first.** Before drafting, tell the report as you would to a colleague in two minutes: the call and the reason in two sentences, then one sentence for each section. Write those sentences down. They become the headline, the key points and each section's takeaway. If you cannot say it, the analysis is not finished, so send it back.
2. **Draft in plain text, away from the builder.** Write each paragraph with no figures: the point, then the reason. Then add the two or three figures that carry it. Every other figure goes in the exhibit the paragraph cites.
3. **Edit three times.** Cut: delete every sentence the reader would not miss. Habits: rewrite each sentence that shows a habit from the list below. Stranger: read it as someone new to the company and rewrite whatever makes that reader stop.
4. **Then build.** Move the text into the builder and bind its figures to the model. Binding changes where a number comes from, never the sentence around it.

## Limits

- A paragraph makes one point, states it in its first sentence and runs to about 90 words at most.
- A sentence carries one idea and at most three figures. A paragraph carries about six.
- Most sentences are under 25 words.
- The text of a full report runs to 1,800-3,000 words. Exhibits carry the rest.
- A figure is given once in the body. A qualification is given once, beside the claim it limits, and only if it would change what the reader does.

A paragraph over a limit holds two points or a table's worth of figures. Split it, or move the figures to an exhibit and say what they show. Do not get under a limit by dropping words a sentence needs, or by joining sentences with semicolons and brackets.

## Habits that make a report sound machine-written

| Habit | What it looks like | Write this way |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saying what a thing is not | "The multiple, not the estimate, has fallen"; "timing rather than demand"; "a cross-check, not the target" | Say what it is. Keep a contrast only where the reader would otherwise assume the wrong thing. |
| Chained clauses | Three ideas joined by semicolons; figures packed into brackets | One sentence, one job. A figure the reader needs gets its own sentence. One they do not need goes in the exhibit. |
| Every paragraph the same shape | Claim, run of figures, "which means", then a closing line on what it does to EPS or the target | End when the point is made. Give the effect on the stock once, where it is not obvious. |
| Talking about the model | "the build applies", "the model grows", "the base case holds", "the anchor is used because" | Say what the business does and what we expect it to do. |
| Abstract subjects | "the volume risk is timing"; "minority access to cash is constrained" | Name who does what: "deliveries may slip"; "the parent decides". |
| Dressed-up words | embeds, underpins, anchors, reflects, regime, window, lever, headwind, robust, meaningful, key, notably | The plain word: assumes, supports, is based on, shows, period. "Strong" only with its number. |
| Stacked caution | "is consistent with", "read conditionally", "appears to", "(not confirmed)" on every date | Say once what we know and what we do not. |
| Signposts and filler | "it is worth noting", "importantly", "as discussed above", "taken together" | Delete them. |
| Reflex patterns | Lists of three by default; "not only... but also"; a colon before a reveal; a question the next sentence answers | Give as many items as there are, and state the point. |
| Slogans | "Cash is king, but only if the board lets it out" | The plain fact. |

Do not cure a habit with its neighbour: a dash for a semicolon, "instead of" for "rather than". Rewrite the sentence.

## Before and after

Invented companies and figures. Each "before" is accurate and still fails: the reader has to decode it or wade through it. Each "after" is plainer and shorter, and uses only facts already in the "before". A writer who needs a fact the analysis does not hold sends the point back to analysis and does not invent it. Figures taken out of a sentence stay in the cited exhibit.

**Lead with the idea.**

> Before: Revenue grew 24% in FY2025 to 4.1bn, gross margin widened 1.2 points to 38.4%, and capacity is guided to reach 60,000 tonnes in 2027 against 32,000 today; the broker component forecasts assume the plant opens in 2027, while we defer it to 2028 and forecast FY2027E EPS of 2.10 against consensus of 2.45.
>
> After: Brokers expect the new plant to nearly double capacity in 2027. We think it opens a year late, which puts 2027 profit about 14% below what analysts expect.

**Say how the business earns its profit before measuring it.**

> Before: Grocery distribution to independent retailers was 88% of FY2025 revenue of 9.2bn but 6% of operating profit of 610m; supplier shelf-space rebates of 340m and rental income of 235m on owned warehouses carried margins of 100% and 74%.
>
> After: The company delivers groceries to small shops but makes very little on delivery. Its profit comes from brands paying for shelf space and from rent on the warehouses it owns (Exhibit 2).

**One paragraph, one point. The exhibit holds the rest.**

> Before: Passenger volume recovered to 31.2m in FY2025 (+12.4%, 96% of FY2019), with international at 9.8m (+31%, 78% of FY2019) and domestic at 21.4m (+5.3%, 107%); aeronautical revenue per passenger rose 3.1% to 14.2 on the tariff reset effective April 2025, while retail spend per international passenger fell 6% to 41.5 as duty-free concessions were re-tendered at lower minimum guarantees (Exhibit 4). The model assumes international reaches 11.9m in FY2027E (95% of FY2019), which adds 2.1m passengers at 41.5 of retail spend and lifts retail revenue 21%; a 10% shortfall in international volume cuts FY2027E EPS by 0.04.
>
> After: Domestic traffic is back above its 2019 level. International traffic is at 78% and has further to recover. It matters most, because international passengers are the ones who spend in the shops. We expect it to reach 95% of the 2019 level by 2027, which lifts retail revenue by about a fifth (Exhibit 4).

**Say what the figures mean.**

> Before: From end-March 2025 (close 41.2, forward EPS 2.75) to end-September 2026 (52.6, 3.48), the price rose 27.7% and forward EPS 26.5%, so the forward P/E moved from 15.0x to 15.1x (range 14.0x-16.2x).
>
> After: The shares are up about a quarter in eighteen months, and so is expected profit. Investors have paid 14 to 16 times that profit throughout (Exhibit 5), so profit growth explains the whole rise.

**Say what it is.**

> Before: An orderbook of roughly four years of current output means the volume risk to 2028 is timing (slippage or customer deferral), not a collapse in demand, and the forecast therefore rests on delivery schedules rather than on new orders.
>
> After: The yards hold four years of orders. Deliveries may slip or customers may defer, but the work to 2028 is already booked.

**One sentence, one job.**

> Before: Peers pay out 34-97% of earnings (median 50%) and trade on 8.5-9.7x, against the company's 12.2x and 99%; on earnings excluding interest on its cash, which is a third of the share price, the company trades on 7.3x, so its premium is a cash effect (Exhibit 9).
>
> After: At 12 times earnings the shares look dearer than peers at about 9 times. A third of the share price is cash. Without the cash and the interest on it, the business trades on about 7 times (Exhibit 9).

**Write who does what.**

> Before: Governance supports the distribution of profit (100% payout) but not of capital (no return to date), and the 65% controlling shareholding constrains minority access to the cash balance.
>
> After: The company pays out all it earns but has never returned the cash it already holds. The parent owns 65% and decides. Other shareholders cannot force a payout.

**Use the plain word.**

> Before: The price embeds a structurally lower multiple, underpinned by concerns that the brand's growth regime has shifted, and near-term re-rating levers are limited.
>
> After: Investors now pay less for each dollar of profit because they think the brand has stopped growing. We see nothing in the next year that would change their mind.

**Give the steps, one short sentence each.**

> Before: Unless distributed, 1.00 of retained cash is worth about 0.40 to a holder, since its 3% deposit return is distributed and capitalised at the 7.5% required yield.
>
> After: A dollar kept in the bank earns about 3 cents a year, which is paid out. An investor wanting 7.5% pays 40 cents for 3 cents a year. So a dollar inside the company is worth 40 cents to a shareholder until the board hands it over.

**Give the business reason.**

> Before: The build applies a 3.4% return to 2H26 and 3.3% to 2027-28 on the cash balance after dividends, reflecting the one- to two-year deposit ladder.
>
> After: Interest income should fall only slowly. Most cash sits in one- to two-year deposits, so a rate cut bites only as each matures. We assume the return slips from 3.4% to 3.3%.

**State the valuation once, with its reason.**

> Before: The target capitalises FY2027E DPS of 0.60 at 7.5%, the mean FY2027E yield of the state-controlled utilities that the same income funds hold (Exhibit 12). It uses FY2027E, although the house default would still use FY2026E; the choice moves the target by 0.07 and leaves the rating unchanged, and the selection of the comparable set is a judgment.
>
> After: People own this stock for its dividend. The state-controlled utilities held by the same income funds yield 7.5%. On our 2027 dividend of 0.60 that gives a target of 8.00.

**When two facts pull apart, explain why and say which reading the evidence favours.**

> Before: Volume rose 14% in 1H26 while profit fell 4%. Unit profit was 4.95 in 1H25, 4.11 in 2H25 and 4.16 in 1H26; the decline was tested against customer mix (existing customers 4.98 to 4.18, Exhibit 4) and is attributed to input costs ahead of pricing, with the build restoring 4.90 by 2H27E.
>
> After: Volume rose 14% but profit fell 4%. Either new customers pay less, which lasts, or raw materials rose before prices could follow, which reverses. Profit per unit fell as much on existing customers (Exhibit 4), so we think it is cost and expect the margin back by late 2027.

**End when the point is made.**

> Before: Joining the index would let mainland funds buy the shares, and the next review is in March 2027. This is upside to the valuation rather than to earnings; it is not in the base case, would widen the investor base rather than raise profit, and each point of yield is worth 0.85 a share.
>
> After: Joining the index would let mainland funds buy the shares. The next review is in March 2027. We have not counted on it.

**Do not write to protect yourself.**

> Before: These are priced scenarios, not probabilities or loss limits, and the target should be read as an anchor that cannot be distinguished from the price, not as evidence of mispricing; with surplus cash distributed the target would be about 15% higher.
>
> After: Our target is 1% above the price, so the shares are fairly priced. A payout from the cash pile would add about 15% to our target.

**Cut padding.**

> Before: It is worth noting that, looking at the company's results in each year since 2022, what stands out is that margins have been moving steadily higher, which we think is an important point for investors to bear in mind.
>
> After: Margins have risen every year since 2022.

## Order of the argument

- Open with the call and the thesis, in one or two sentences a reader could repeat to a colleague.
- Then explain the business and its drivers, and what they do to earnings and cash. The long valuation section comes after that. The front page can show the call and target at once.
- Say how a business earns its profit, and why a number moved, before giving the figures.
- For each number the call depends on, the reader should be able to see where it comes from, what it means and what it does to the stock. That is a test of the report, not a shape for every paragraph. Most paragraphs make a point and give the reason.
- Give each argument one home. The front page and the brief summarise it, and later sections refer back in a clause.
- Select the evidence that changes earnings, cash, value or risk, and keep the strongest counter-case. Leave out routine background and any account of how the research was done.
- Say why the valuation yardstick fits and state the target once, with its basis. Alternatives that change the rating go in one exhibit. Do not explain house conventions.

## What editing must not change

- Every fact traces to a filing, a data series, an attributed comment or the model. If the analysis does not hold a fact the sentence needs, send the point back. Never fill a gap with something plausible.
- Our judgments are marked "we estimate", "we forecast" or "we assume", and stay distinguishable from reported data, guidance and consensus.
- Each figure keeps its period and unit. Use one EPS basis, one per-share currency and one horizon throughout, and label a second basis where it appears.
- A claim stays as wide as its evidence. A change in one company's mix is not a shift in industry demand. Cash that covers a dividend for years is not a dividend that cannot be cut.
- A shorter sentence keeps its condition: cutting words must not turn "if" into "will". The strongest counter-case stays.
- Dividend yield and price return stay separate.
- Editing never changes a forecast or the target. A factual problem goes back to the analysis that owns it.
- No wording from these instructions goes into the report.

Before release, reread every sentence of the report and brief against the evidence, and cut or correct what does not hold. Review and release follow the [publication workflow](../../report-layout/references/publication-review.md).

## Profit labels and valuation language

Use **net profit attributable to shareholders** for earnings after minorities; once defined, "attributable net profit" will do. Do not call it parent profit, and do not swap in group profit, operating profit, adjusted earnings or cash flow. Define any recurring, adjusted or cash measure where it first appears.

Introduce a valuation with its period and earnings basis ("about 25 times forecast FY2028 EPS"), then say why that multiple is reasonable. The multiplication alone is not the reasoning. Call the multiple a reasonable target, never fair value. Keep the forward earnings period set by the [decision contract](../../investment-conclusion/references/decision-contract.md#target-and-earnings-basis).

Keep apart: adjusted earnings and cash after investment; price return and dividend yield; a good business and a good return at this price.
